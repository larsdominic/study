How do you test whether a sequence is arithmetic?	Check the differences are equal: u<sub>2</sub> − u<sub>1</sub> = u<sub>3</sub> − u<sub>2</sub>. That constant is d.	IB::Maths SL_1.2
n<sup>th</sup> term of an arithmetic sequence	u<sub>n</sub> = u<sub>1</sub> + (n − 1)d (in the formula booklet). n − 1 steps, not n.	IB::Maths SL_1.2
Sum of the first n terms of an arithmetic sequence (two forms)	S<sub>n</sub> = n/2 (2u<sub>1</sub> + (n − 1)d) = n/2 (u<sub>1</sub> + u<sub>n</sub>) (both in the booklet).	IB::Maths SL_1.2
How many terms in a sigma sum from r = a to r = b?	b − a + 1. From r = 3 to r = 12 is 10 terms, not 9.	IB::Maths SL_1.2
You are given S<sub>n</sub>. How do you find u<sub>n</sub> and u<sub>1</sub>?	u<sub>n</sub> = S<sub>n</sub> − S<sub>n−1</sub>, and u<sub>1</sub> = S<sub>1</sub>.	IB::Maths SL_1.2
Simple interest: arithmetic or geometric?	Arithmetic: the same amount is added every year (for example 5000 + 189n).	IB::Maths SL_1.2
Data is nearly but not exactly arithmetic. How do you model it?	Approximate d by the average step: (last − first) ÷ (number of gaps). Predictions far outside the data are less reliable.	IB::Maths SL_1.2
How do you test whether a sequence is geometric?	Check the ratios are equal: u<sub>2</sub>/u<sub>1</sub> = u<sub>3</sub>/u<sub>2</sub>. That constant is r.	IB::Maths SL_1.3
n<sup>th</sup> term of a geometric sequence	u<sub>n</sub> = u<sub>1</sub>r<sup>n−1</sup> (in the booklet).	IB::Maths SL_1.3
Sum of the first n terms of a geometric sequence	S<sub>n</sub> = u<sub>1</sub>(r<sup>n</sup> − 1)/(r − 1) = u<sub>1</sub>(1 − r<sup>n</sup>)/(1 − r), r ≠ 1 (in the booklet).	IB::Maths SL_1.3
Two terms of a geometric sequence are given. Fastest way to find r?	Divide them: u<sub>5</sub>/u<sub>2</sub> = r<sup>3</sup>. Remember r<sup>2</sup> = 3 gives r = ±√3.	IB::Maths SL_1.3
Grows by 2.5% a year. Multiplier? After n years or in year n?	× 1.025. After n years: × 1.025<sup>n</sup>. In year n when year 1 is the start: × 1.025<sup>n−1</sup>.	IB::Maths SL_1.3

What is a hydrogen bond in water?	The attraction between a δ+ hydrogen of one water molecule and the δ− oxygen of a neighbouring molecule. It is between molecules; the O-H bonds inside a molecule are polar covalent.	IB::Biology A1.1
Why is a water molecule polar?	Oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen, so it pulls the shared electrons towards itself (O δ−, H δ+), and the bent shape (about 104.5°) stops the charges cancelling.	IB::Biology A1.1
Cohesion vs adhesion	Cohesion: water sticking to water (hydrogen bonds between water molecules). Adhesion: water sticking to other polar or charged materials such as cellulose, xylem walls, glass, soil.	IB::Biology A1.1
Two consequences of cohesion for organisms	Water is pulled up the xylem under tension as an unbroken column; surface tension lets small animals such as pond skaters stand on water.	IB::Biology A1.1
Which property forms a meniscus and drives capillary action in cell walls and soil?	Adhesion (to polar surfaces), with cohesion pulling the following molecules along. Not cohesion or surface tension alone.	IB::Biology A1.1
Hydrophilic, hydrophobic, amphipathic: meanings and one example each	Hydrophilic: attracted to water (glucose, salts). Hydrophobic: non-polar, not attracted (fats, oils). Amphipathic: both parts in one molecule (phospholipids).	IB::Biology A1.1
How are glucose, oxygen and cholesterol carried in blood?	Glucose: dissolved in plasma. Oxygen: a little dissolved, nearly all bound to haemoglobin. Cholesterol (and fats): inside lipoproteins.	IB::Biology A1.1
Four physical properties of water vs air that matter to aquatic animals	Buoyancy (much more support), viscosity (more drag, so streamlining), thermal conductivity (heat lost about 24 times faster, so blubber), specific heat capacity (stable water temperature).	IB::Biology A1.1

(HL) Where did Earth's water probably come from, and why did Earth keep it?	Mainly from asteroids (extraplanetary origin). Kept because Earth's gravity holds the atmosphere and its distance from the Sun keeps temperatures between 0 and 100 °C, so water stays liquid.	IB::Biology A1.1
(HL) What is the Goldilocks zone?	The range of distances from a star where a planet's surface is neither too hot nor too cold, so liquid water can exist. The search for life elsewhere starts with water.	IB::Biology A1.1
The three parts of a nucleotide and their drawing shapes	Phosphate (circle), pentose sugar (pentagon: deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), nitrogenous base (rectangle). Base on carbon 1′, phosphate on carbon 5′.	IB::Biology A1.2
Bases in DNA and in RNA	DNA: adenine, cytosine, guanine, thymine. RNA: adenine, cytosine, guanine, uracil (uracil replaces thymine).	IB::Biology A1.2

Three structural differences between DNA and RNA	DNA: two strands (double helix), deoxyribose, thymine. RNA: one strand, ribose, uracil.	IB::Biology A1.2
What forms the backbone of a DNA or RNA strand?	Alternating sugar and phosphate groups joined by covalent bonds (phosphate to carbon 3′ of the next sugar), made by condensation reactions.	IB::Biology A1.2
How many base sequences are possible with n bases? Why are codons triplets?	4<sup>n</sup>. Pairs give only 16 codes, fewer than 20 amino acids; triplets give 64.	IB::Biology A1.2

(HL) What is at the 5′ end and at the 3′ end of a strand? Which end grows?	5′ end: phosphate on carbon 5′. 3′ end: sugar with a free -OH on carbon 3′. New nucleotides are added only to the 3′ end, so strands grow 5′ to 3′.	IB::Biology A1.2
(HL) Purines and pyrimidines, and why each pair needs one of each	Purines (two rings): adenine, guanine. Pyrimidines (one ring): cytosine, thymine, uracil. Purine plus pyrimidine keeps the helix the same width everywhere, so it is stable.	IB::Biology A1.2

(HL) What did Chargaff find, and what did it falsify?	A ≈ T and G ≈ C, but the proportions differ between species and are not 25% each. This falsified the tetranucleotide hypothesis (four bases in equal, repeating amounts).	IB::Biology A1.2
