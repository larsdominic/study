Exam technique: Chemistry HL

How the HL papers work, the habits that win marks, and what every command word asks for.

Calculators are allowed on every paper, and you get a clean copy of the Chemistry data booklet for all of them. The other 20% is the internal assessment (one scientific investigation, 24 marks). Content: everything, SL and AHL.

Habits that win marks

  1. Paper 1A: never leave a question blank. The guide says no marks are deducted for incorrect answers, so a guess can only help. Cross out options you know are wrong first.
  2. Definitions word for word. First ionisation energy, isotopes, relative atomic mass: examiners look for the exact key words (for example "gaseous atoms", "one mole").
  3. State symbols in equations when the question is about states, ionisation energy or dissolving: (s), (l), (g), (aq). M(g) → M+(g) + e− has (g) on both sides.
  4. Units and significant figures. Give a unit with every quantity, and match the significant figures of the least precise data in the question (usually 3). Keep extra figures in the working.
  5. Use the data booklet. Constants (h, c, NA), relative atomic masses, E = hf and c = λf are in it. Practise finding them quickly; do not waste time memorising them.
  6. "Explain" needs a reason. "Ionisation energy increases across a period" is a description. "… because nuclear charge increases while shielding stays about the same" is the explanation that scores.
  7. Calculations: show each stage, as "Calculate" asks for the relevant stages. A wrong final answer can still earn most marks if the method is visible.
  8. Time. Paper 1 is 75 marks in 120 minutes (1.6 minutes per mark); Paper 2 is 90 marks in 150 minutes (about 1.7 minutes per mark).

Command terms

The level column is the assessment objective: 1 means recall (short), 2 means apply, 3 means analyse or evaluate (longer, with reasons). The higher the level, the more you need to write.

Command termLevelWhat the IB means (official definition)
Draw1Represent by means of a labelled, accurate diagram or graph, using a pencil. A ruler (straight edge) should be used for straight lines. Diagrams should be drawn to scale. Graphs should have points correctly plotted (if appropriate) and joined in a straight line or smooth curve.
State1Give a specific name, value or other brief answer without explanation or calculation.
Annotate2Add brief notes to a diagram or graph.
Calculate2Obtain a numerical answer showing the relevant stages in the working.
Describe2Give a detailed account.
Estimate2Obtain an approximate value.
Outline2Give a brief account or summary.
Comment3Give a judgement based on a given statement or result of a calculation.
Compare3Give an account of the similarities between two (or more) items or situations, referring to both (all) of them throughout.
Contrast3Give an account of the differences between two (or more) items or situations, referring to both (all) of them throughout.
Deduce3Reach a conclusion from the information given.
Determine3Obtain the only possible answer.
Discuss3Offer a considered and balanced review that includes a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses. Opinions or conclusions should be presented clearly and supported by appropriate evidence.
Evaluate3Make an appraisal by weighing up the strengths and limitations.
Explain3Give a detailed account including reasons or causes.
Predict3Give an expected result.
Sketch3Represent by means of a diagram or graph (labelled as appropriate). The sketch should give a general idea of the required shape or relationship, and should include relevant features.
Suggest3Propose a solution, hypothesis or other possible answer.
