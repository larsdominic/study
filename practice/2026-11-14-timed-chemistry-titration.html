Worked answer key: water of crystallisation (open after the timer)

Values used: relative atomic masses to two decimal places as in the IB data booklet: Cu 63.55, S 32.07, O 16.00, H 1.01.

  • M(CuSO4) = 63.55 + 32.07 + 4 × 16.00 = 159.62 g mol−1
  • M(H2O) = 2 × 1.01 + 16.00 = 18.02 g mol−1
  • If x = 5: M(CuSO4·5H2O) = 159.62 + 5 × 18.02 = 249.72 g mol−1, so water is 90.10 ÷ 249.72 = 36.08% of the mass.
  • Equation: CuSO4·5H2O(s) → CuSO4(s) + 5H2O(g). Blue crystals turn to a white powder.

Method A, trial 1 step by step:

  1. mass of hydrated salt = 25.55 − 24.53 = 1.02 g
  2. mass of anhydrous CuSO4 = 25.21 − 24.53 = 0.68 g
  3. mass of water lost = 25.55 − 25.21 = 0.34 g
  4. n(CuSO4) = 0.68 ÷ 159.62 = 0.00426 mol; n(H2O) = 0.34 ÷ 18.02 = 0.0189 mol
  5. x = n(H2O) ÷ n(CuSO4) = 0.0189 ÷ 0.00426 = 4.43 (from the unrounded moles; dividing the rounded ones gives 4.44, also fine)

  • Mean x = 4.50 (4.51 if you average the rounded values; either is fine). Two or three significant figures are justified, because the smallest masses have only two.
  • Uncertainty: each mass is a difference of two readings, so ±0.02 g. % uncertainty in x = % uncertainty in the water mass + % uncertainty in the anhydrous mass. Trial 1: 0.02 ÷ 0.34 = 5.9% plus 0.02 ÷ 0.68 = 2.9%, so 8.8%. The bigger samples (trial 5, 3.0%) are more precise.
  • Percentage error of the mean: (4.50 − 5) ÷ 5 × 100 = −9.9%, about −10%.
  • Method B: plot n(H2O) (y) against n(CuSO4) (x). The best-fit line has a gradient of about 4.6 (4.58 by least squares) and passes very close to the origin (intercept −0.0005 mol). Gradient = moles of water per mole of CuSO4 = x. Accept 4.4 to 4.7 from a hand-drawn line. (The worksheet mentions a "mass fraction": if you plot mass of water against mass of CuSO4 instead, the gradient is 0.517, and x = 0.517 × 159.62 ÷ 18.02 = 4.58, the same answer.)
  • Conclusion: x ≈ 4.5, about 10% below the literature value of 5. The error (10%) is larger than the random uncertainty (3% to 9%), so random error cannot explain it: there is a systematic error.
  • Most likely cause: incomplete dehydration. Water left in the residue makes the residue too heavy and the water lost too small, so x comes out too low. Fix: heat, cool and weigh again until two masses agree within 0.01 g (heating to constant mass).
  • Other errors and their direction: the anhydrous salt absorbing water from the air while it cools makes x too low (cool it in a desiccator, with the lid on); solid spitting out of the crucible makes x too high (lid partly on, heat gently at first); heating far too strongly can decompose CuSO4 further (the residue goes grey or black), which also makes x too high.

Background should cover: what water of crystallisation is; the formula CuSO4·xH2O and that x is a whole number of water molecules per formula unit; the dehydration equation and colour change; the assumption that all mass lost is water and the residue is pure anhydrous CuSO4; how x = n(H2O) ÷ n(CuSO4).

Method should cover: independent variable (initial mass of hydrated salt, for Method B), dependent variable (mass lost), controls (same balance, same crucible and lid, same heating method); weigh the empty crucible and lid, add salt, weigh, heat gently then strongly, cool in a desiccator, weigh, repeat to constant mass; at least five trials. Risk table: copper(II) sulfate is harmful if swallowed and irritates eyes and skin (goggles, wash hands); hot crucible (tongs, let it cool); copper compounds are toxic to aquatic life, so collect the residue for disposal and do not pour it down the sink.

Self-check, 1 point each (10 in total): (1) balanced equation; (2) both molar masses right; (3) masses of water and anhydrous salt for all five trials; (4) moles for all five trials; (5) x for each trial between 4.4 and 4.6; (6) a mean of about 4.5 with sensible significant figures; (7) a graph with labelled axes and a gradient of 4.4 to 4.7; (8) percentage error about −10%; (9) incomplete dehydration named as the main cause, with the direction (x too low); (10) one realistic improvement (heat to constant mass, or cool in a desiccator).

Error log

Error logabout 10 min
  1. Open the study sheet, Error log tab.
  2. Write one row for every mark you lost: the date, the subject, the question (for example "Back titration (e)"), what went wrong, and the rule that fixes it.
  3. Say what kind of mistake it was: did not know it (go back to the guide or handout), wrong method, careless slip (copying, signs, units) or ran out of time. Four careless slips in one set is a pattern worth a rule of its own.
  4. Timing: write down which question you were on when the timer ended. If you finished in extra time, how many minutes did it take? Speed problems and knowledge problems need different fixes.
  5. For set B, log any step of the calculation you could not do without the key, and any section of the write-up you left empty.

Not timed: Extended Essay, subject and three research questions

About 30 minEE planning
  1. Choose the EE subject. Most students pick a subject they take and enjoy, ideally one where they already know how to find sources or collect data. Write two sentences on why.
