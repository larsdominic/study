What a timed topic test is for

A topic test checks one topic the way an exam does: a fixed time, no notes, marks printed in brackets after each question, for example [3]. Today the times come from the test covers: they are exam pace, a bit faster than normal homework.

The point is not the score. The point is to find out which exact points you cannot yet write down under time, so you can fix them before the exam.

How a mark scheme gives marks

A mark scheme is the list of answers a marker accepts. For a written question it lists separate points, and each different correct point is worth one mark, up to the number in brackets. A scheme often lists more points than there are marks ("any 4 of these details").

  • Say the same thing twice: still one mark.
  • Your words can differ from the scheme if the meaning is the same.
  • For a calculation, the working is part of the marks.

Command words: what the question wants

The first word of a question tells you how much to write.

  • List or state: short answers, no explanation.
  • Outline: the main points, briefly.
  • Describe: say what something is like or what happens.
  • Explain: say why or how, with "because" and "so".
  • Distinguish: make the differences clear.
  • Suggest: a sensible idea, even for something you were not taught.

Rate: how fast something happens

A rate is a change divided by the time it took. If 0.55 units of glucose appear in 5 minutes, the rate is 0.55 ÷ 5 = 0.11 units per minute.

On a graph, a steep line is a fast rate, a flat line means nothing is happening any more. Always write "per minute" (min−1) in the unit of a rate.

Exam tips for Biology

Only what these InThinking mark schemes show; they are not official IB rules.

  • One point, one mark. Schemes list more points than marks ("any 4 of these details", "Max: 4"). For a 4-mark question, write at least four separate, clear points.
  • Use the numbers in the data. C1.1 Q5 III is answered with the figure: 0.14 more each minute.
  • Name it, then give an example. C1.2 Q7 gives a mark for each use of ATP and another for its example.
  • Tables: both columns. C1.2 Q6 gives a row's mark only when the aerobic and the anaerobic answers are both right.
  • Experiments: say what and how. C1.2 Q5 IV wants the variable and how you would change it; Q5 II wants what the control shows.
  • Use the key word. The schemes underline the words they look for: globular, fibrous, essential. A vague word may not get the mark.
  • Diagrams: label every part asked for. B1.2 Q4 marks each group (amine, carboxyl, alpha carbon, R group, peptide bond) separately.
