On the formula sheet (SL)

The IB guide says a copy of these formulae is provided in the exam. For SL you only need these six. All worked examples use one company so you can see how the ratios connect: Harbour Bikes Ltd (made-up figures): sales revenue $500 000, cost of sales $300 000, expenses $120 000, current assets $90 000 (of which stock $30 000), current liabilities $60 000, non-current liabilities $150 000, equity $250 000.

Gross profit margin (GPM) On formula sheet
GPM = gross profitsales revenue × 100%
Worked example
  1. Gross profit = sales revenue − cost of sales = $500 000 − $300 000 = $200 000
  2. GPM = 200 000500 000 × 100% = 40%
Profit margin On formula sheet
profit margin = profit before interest and taxsales revenue × 100%
Worked example
  1. Profit before interest and tax = gross profit − expenses = $200 000 − $120 000 = $80 000
  2. Profit margin = 80 000500 000 × 100% = 16%
Return on capital employed (ROCE) On formula sheet
ROCE = profit before interest and taxcapital employed × 100%
where capital employed = non-current liabilities + equity (printed on the sheet)
Worked example
  1. Capital employed = $150 000 + $250 000 = $400 000
  2. ROCE = 80 000400 000 × 100% = 20%
Current ratio On formula sheet
current ratio = current assetscurrent liabilities
Worked example
  1. 90 00060 000 = 1.5, written 1.5 or 1.5:1 (no % sign)
Acid test (quick) ratio On formula sheet
acid test ratio = current assets − stockcurrent liabilities
Worked example
  1. $90 000 − $30 000 = $60 000
  2. 60 00060 000 = 1.0 (1:1)
Average rate of return (ARR) On formula sheet
ARR = (total returns − capital cost) ÷ years of usecapital cost × 100%
Worked example
  1. A machine costs $100 000 and brings total returns of $140 000 over 5 years.
  2. Average yearly profit = ($140 000 − $100 000) ÷ 5 = $8000
  3. ARR = 8000100 000 × 100% = 8% a year
Ignore the HL lines. The same appendix also lists stock turnover, debtor days, creditor days, gearing ratio, NPV, capacity utilisation rate and productivity rate, plus a discount table. These are HL only and are not examined at SL.

Finance (unit 3): learn it

3.1Capital vs revenue expenditure
Capital expenditure buys non-current assets that last more than a year. Revenue expenditure pays day-to-day running costs.
Example: A $30 000 delivery van is capital expenditure; its fuel and the driver wages are revenue expenditure.
3.2Internal sources of finance
Personal funds (sole traders): the owner’s own savings. Retained profit: profit kept in the business after tax and dividends. Sale of assets: selling things the business owns but no longer needs.
Example: A bakery owner puts in $8000 of savings; later the firm sells an unused warehouse for $250 000.
3.2External sources: shares, loans, overdrafts, trade credit
Share capital: money from selling shares in a company (never repaid, but owners lose some control). Loan capital: borrowed for a fixed period, repaid with interest. Overdraft: the bank lets the account go below zero up to a limit; short term, high interest. Trade credit: buy from suppliers now, pay later.
Example: A 5-year $60 000 bank loan for a van; a café gets coffee beans now and pays the supplier in 30 days.
3.2External sources: crowdfunding, leasing, microfinance, business angels
Crowdfunding: small amounts from many people, usually online. Leasing: paying regularly to use an asset owned by someone else. Microfinance providers: small loans to people on low incomes who cannot get bank loans. Business angels: wealthy individuals who invest their own money in a small business for a share of it, often with advice.
Example: A start-up raises $40 000 from 800 online backers; a market trader gets a $500 microloan; an angel invests $100 000 for 20% of a tech firm.
3.2Short or long term (AO3)
Match the finance to its use: short-term needs (stock, a cash gap) suit overdrafts and trade credit; long-term assets suit loans, share capital or leasing. Judge cost, control, risk and the size of the business.
Example: Financing a new factory with an overdraft is a poor match; a long-term loan or share issue fits better.
3.3Types of cost and total revenue
Fixed costs do not change with output (rent). Variable costs rise with output (materials). Direct costs belong to one product (its ingredients). Indirect/overhead costs are shared (office salaries). Total revenue = price × quantity sold; revenue streams are the different sources of income.
Example: 2000 bikes × $15 rental = $30 000 total revenue from the rental stream.
3.4Profit and loss account layout
Sales revenue − cost of sales = gross profit; − expenses = profit before interest and tax; − interest = profit before tax; − tax = profit for the period; − dividends = retained profit.
Example: Harbour Bikes: PBIT $80 000, interest $10 000, tax 20%: profit before tax $70 000, tax $14 000, profit for period $56 000; after $20 000 dividends, retained profit $36 000.
3.4Balance sheet and intangible assets
Total assets − total liabilities = net assets = total equity (share capital + retained earnings). Intangible assets have no physical form: goodwill, patents, copyrights, trademarks, brands.
Example: Harbour Bikes: net assets $250 000 = total equity $250 000. (Its capital employed of $400 000 also counts the $150 000 of non-current liabilities, so do not mix the two up.)
3.5Reading the ratios (not printed)
Higher margins and ROCE are better; compare with past years and competitors. Common textbook guide: current ratio about 1.5 to 2, acid test about 1. Strategies (AO3): raise prices, cut costs, sell assets, chase debtors.
Example: Harbour Bikes: current ratio 1.5 and acid test 1.0 look healthy; GPM 40% versus profit margin 16% shows expenses take 24% of revenue.
3.7Cash flow and working capital
Profit is not cash. Working capital = current assets − current liabilities. In a cash flow forecast: net cash flow = cash inflows − cash outflows, closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow, and the closing balance is next month’s opening balance.
Example: Opening $5000, inflows $12 000, outflows $14 500: net cash flow −$2500, closing balance $2500. Harbour Bikes working capital = $90 000 − $60 000 = $30 000.
3.8Payback period (not printed)
Time for the net cash flows to repay the cost. Count whole years, then months = amount still needed ÷ that year’s net cash flow × 12.
Example: Cost $50 000, net cash flows $20 000, $20 000, $15 000: after 2 years $10 000 is still needed, 10 000 ÷ 15 000 × 12 = 8 months, so 2 years 8 months.

Break-even analysis (5.5): learn it

5.5Contribution
Contribution per unit = price − variable cost per unit. Total contribution = contribution per unit × units sold (= total revenue − total variable costs).
Example: Price $25, variable cost $15 per unit: contribution $10 per unit; 5500 units give total contribution $55 000.
5.5Break-even quantity
Break-even quantity = fixed costscontribution per unit. Round up to a whole unit. Break-even revenue = break-even quantity × price.
Example: Fixed costs $40 000: 40 000 ÷ 10 = 4000 units, break-even revenue 4000 × $25 = $100 000.
5.5Profit or loss
Profit = total contribution − fixed costs (= total revenue − total costs).
Example: At 5500 units: $55 000 − $40 000 = $15 000 profit.
5.5Margin of safety
Margin of safety = actual output − break-even quantity (in units, or multiply by price for revenue). Negative means a loss.
Example: 5500 − 4000 = 1500 units (or $37 500 of sales).
5.5Target profit output and target price
Target profit output = fixed costs + target profitcontribution per unit
Target price = fixed costs + target profitquantity + variable cost per unit
Example: Target profit $20 000: (40 000 + 20 000) ÷ 10 = 6000 units. To make it on 5000 units: 60 000 ÷ 5000 + 15 = $27.
5.5Changes in price or cost
Recalculate the contribution, then the break-even quantity. A higher price or lower variable cost lowers break-even; higher fixed costs raise it. On the chart, redraw the line that changed.
Example: Price up to $27: contribution $12, break-even 40 000 ÷ 12 = 3333.3, so 3334 units.
5.5Break-even chart and limitations
Units on the x-axis, $ on the y-axis. Fixed costs: horizontal line. Total costs: start at fixed costs. Total revenue: start at 0. Break-even where total revenue meets total costs. Limitations (AO3): assumes everything made is sold, straight-line costs and revenue, one product, and a static price.
Example: Label the break-even point, the margin of safety and the profit and loss areas on every chart.

The SL toolkit: learn what each tool shows

ToolkitSWOT analysis
Strengths and weaknesses are internal; opportunities and threats are external. Shows where a business stands before a decision.
Example: Strength: loyal customers. Threat: a new competitor opens next door.
ToolkitAnsoff matrix
Growth options by product (existing or new) and market (existing or new): market penetration, product development, market development, diversification. Risk rises towards diversification.
Example: Selling existing bikes in a new country is market development.
ToolkitSTEEPLE analysis
External factors: Social, Technological, Economic, Environmental, Political, Legal, Ethical.
Example: A new e-bike subsidy is Political (or Economic); a rise in interest rates is Economic.
ToolkitBCG matrix
Products by market growth (vertical) and relative market share (horizontal): stars (high, high), cash cows (low growth, high share), question marks (high growth, low share), dogs (low, low).
Example: A best-selling bike in a mature market is a cash cow: use its cash to fund question marks.
ToolkitBusiness plan
A document setting out the business idea, objectives, marketing, operations, human resources and finance (forecasts). Used to plan and to persuade lenders and investors.
Example: A bank asks for a business plan with a cash flow forecast before granting a loan.
ToolkitDecision trees
Square = decision, circle = chance node with probabilities adding to 1. Expected value = Σ (probability × outcome); subtract the cost of the option; pick the highest net value.
Example: Launch: 0.6 × $500 000 + 0.4 × $100 000 = $340 000; minus cost $200 000 gives $140 000.
ToolkitDescriptive statistics
Mean, mode, median, bar charts, pie charts, infographics, quartiles, standard deviation. Use them to summarise data from the case and say what they show.
Example: Weekly sales 12, 15, 15, 20, 38: mean 20, median 15, mode 15; the mean is pulled up by one strong week.
ToolkitCircular business models
Five types: circular supply models, resource recovery models, product life extension models, sharing models, product service system models.
Example: Repairing and reselling used bikes is product life extension; renting bikes by the hour is a sharing model.
