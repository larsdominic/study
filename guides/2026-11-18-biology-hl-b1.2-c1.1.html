Atoms, molecules and bonds

Everything is made of tiny particles called atoms: carbon (C), hydrogen (H), oxygen (O), nitrogen (N) and sulfur (S) are the ones in proteins. Atoms join into molecules.

  • Covalent bond: two atoms share electrons. Strong. It holds the atoms of one molecule together, drawn as a solid line, like C-N.
  • Hydrogen bond: a weak attraction between a slightly positive H and a slightly negative O or N nearby.
  • Ionic bond: the attraction between a positive and a negative charge.

Rule of thumb: strong covalent bonds build a chain; many weak bonds decide how the chain folds.

Charges, polar and non-polar

Opposite charges (+ and −) attract; like charges push apart. A group of atoms is polar when it has a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end, for example −OH. Polar and charged groups are attracted to water: they are hydrophilic (water-loving).

Groups made only of carbon and hydrogen, such as −CH3, have no charged ends: they are non-polar and hydrophobic (water-hating). In water they cluster together, like oil drops.

Monomers, polymers, condensation and hydrolysis

A polymer is a long chain of small repeating units, the monomers, like beads on a string. Proteins are polymers of amino acids; starch and glycogen are polymers of glucose.

A condensation reaction joins two monomers and releases a water molecule. Hydrolysis is the opposite: water is used to split the bond, as in digestion.

Chemical reactions, energy and catalysts

In a chemical reaction, bonds break and new bonds form. The starting molecules are reactants (for an enzyme: the substrate); the new ones are products.

Every reaction needs a push of energy to get started, even one that releases energy overall, like a match that must be struck before it burns. A catalyst makes a reaction go faster without being used up. In living things the catalysts are enzymes, which are proteins.

Genes decide the order of amino acids

A gene is a section of DNA. Its order of bases is a code for the order of amino acids in one polypeptide. The cell reads the gene and joins amino acids in that order at a ribosome.

So a change in the DNA (a mutation) can change one amino acid in the protein. You learn how the code is read in D1.2; today you only need: gene → order of amino acids → shape of the protein.

pH: acid, neutral, alkaline

The pH scale runs from about 0 to 14. pH 7 is neutral (pure water); below 7 is acidic (stomach juice is about pH 2); above 7 is alkaline.

Acids contain many hydrogen ions, H+. Groups on a protein can pick up or lose H+, which changes their charge. That is why pH can change a protein's shape.

Rates, units and sketch graphs

A rate is how much something changes per unit of time. In units, a power of −1 means "per": cm3 s−1 is read "cubic centimetres per second".

A sketch graph shows the shape of a relationship without exact numbers: rising, peaking, levelling off. The thing you change goes on the x-axis (across), the thing you measure on the y-axis (up).

Percentage change: (difference ÷ starting value) × 100.

B1.2 · SL and HL

Proteins

Official guiding question: What is the relationship between amino acid sequence and the diversity in form and function of proteins?

By the end of this section (official statement, then what it means for you):

B1.2.1 Generalized structure of an amino acid

Proteins are polymers, and their monomers are amino acids. Every amino acid has the same core: one central carbon atom, the alpha carbon, with four things bonded to it: an amine group (−NH2), a carboxyl group (−COOH), a hydrogen atom and an R-group.Learn Only the R-group differs between the 20 amino acids, so the R-group gives each amino acid its properties.

B1.2.2 Condensation reactions forming dipeptides and longer chains of amino acids

Word equation:Learn amino acid + amino acid → dipeptide + water. The −OH of one carboxyl group and one −H of the next amine group leave as a water molecule, and the C and the N join by a covalent bond: the peptide bond. Adding more amino acids the same way gives a polypeptide. A chain of n amino acids has n − 1 peptide bonds, and n − 1 water molecules were released when it was made. Splitting a peptide bond by adding water (as in digestion) is hydrolysis.

Worked example: counting peptide bonds. "Insulin is made of two polypeptide chains, of 21 and 30 amino acids. How many peptide bonds does one insulin molecule contain?"
  1. Count each chain separately: 21 − 1 = 20 and 30 − 1 = 29.
  2. Add: 20 + 29 = 49 peptide bonds.
Why this step? Why not 21 + 30 − 1 = 50?Peptide bonds only join neighbours in one chain; insulin's two chains are joined by disulfide bonds instead.
Finish it. Haemoglobin has four chains: two of 141 amino acids and two of 146. How many peptide bonds? First half done: the two 141 chains have 2 × (141 − 1) = 280.
Show the answerThe two 146 chains: 2 × (146 − 1) = 290. Total 280 + 290 = 570.

B1.2.3 Dietary requirements for amino acids

Essential amino acids cannot be synthesised by the body, so they must come from food. Non-essential amino acids can be made in the body from other amino acids.Learn You do not need to name examples. Vegan diets need attention: many plant foods contain little of one or more essential amino acids, so vegans eat a variety of protein sources (for example beans together with grains) to get all of them.

B1.2.4 Infinite variety of possible peptide chains

The genetic code codes for 20 amino acids. A chain can have any number of them, from a few to thousands, in any order. So with n amino acids there are 20n possible sequences: almost infinite variety. Examples of polypeptides: the chains of insulin, haemoglobin and collagen.

Worked example. "How many different chains of 3 amino acids are possible? And of 5?"
  1. Each of the 3 positions can be any of 20: 20 × 20 × 20 = 203 = 8 000.
  2. For 5 positions: 205 = 3 200 000.
Why this step? Why multiply, not add?Each choice at one position combines with every choice at the next, so choices multiply.
Finish it. How many chains of 3 amino acids are possible if all three must be different? First half done: position 1 has 20 choices; position 2 has only 19, because one amino acid is already used.
Show the answerPosition 3 has 18 choices, so 20 × 19 × 18 = 6 840.

B1.2.5 Effect of pH and temperature on protein structure

A protein's 3D shape is held by weak bonds between R-groups (details in the HL part). High temperature makes the molecule vibrate so much that these bonds break. A pH far from normal changes the charges on R-groups, so ionic and hydrogen bonds break. Either way the shape changes permanently: the protein is denatured and loses its function.Learn The peptide bonds do not break, so the amino acid sequence stays the same. Egg white turning solid when cooked is denaturation.

Exam trap. Write "bonds between R-groups break" and "3D shape changes"; "peptide bonds break" gets no mark. Low temperature does not denature. Write "denatured", never "killed".
Self-check B1.2 (write two key words for each on paper, then open)
  1. Name the four things bonded to the alpha carbon.
  2. Give the word equation for making a dipeptide.
  3. A polypeptide of 75 amino acids: how many water molecules were released when it was made?
  4. What makes an amino acid "essential"?
  5. How many different dipeptides are possible?
  6. Which bonds break when a protein is denatured, and which do not?

Answers. 1: amine group, carboxyl group, R-group, hydrogen atom. 2: amino acid + amino acid → dipeptide + water. 3: 74. 4: the body cannot synthesise it, so it must come from the diet. 5: 202 = 400. 6: the bonds between R-groups (hydrogen and ionic bonds) break; peptide bonds do not.

B1.2 · additional higher level

How a protein gets its shape HL

Official guiding question: How are protein molecules affected by their chemical and physical environments?

By the end of this section (official statement, then what it means for you):

B1.2.6 Chemical diversity in the R-groups of amino acids

R-groups are either hydrophobic (non-polar, made of carbon and hydrogen only) or hydrophilic. Hydrophilic R-groups are polar (for example with −OH) or charged; charged ones are acidic (negative, −COO−) or basic (positive, −NH3+).Learn You do not need named examples. The mix of R-groups decides how a chain folds and so what the protein can do.

B1.2.7 Impact of primary structure on the conformation of proteins

The primary structure is the sequence of amino acids. The sequence, and the exact position of each amino acid, decide which R-groups end up next to each other and bond, so they decide the 3D shape (the conformation). So the shape is precise, predictable and repeatable. Changing one amino acid (a mutation) can change the shape and stop the protein working.

B1.2.8 Pleating and coiling of secondary structure of proteins

Hydrogen bonds form between the C=O of one amino acid and the N−H of another a few places along the chain. They are part of the backbone, not the R-groups, and form at regular positions.Learn

  • Alpha helix: the chain coils, with hydrogen bonds between one turn and the next.
  • Beta-pleated sheet: stretches of chain lie side by side and are linked by hydrogen bonds; the sheet is folded like a fan (pleated).

Each hydrogen bond is weak, but many together make the structure stable.

B1.2.9 Dependence of tertiary structure on hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide covalent bonds and hydrophobic interactions

The tertiary structure is how the whole polypeptide folds into its 3D shape. It is held by four kinds of interaction between R-groups:Learn

Bond or interactionForms between
Hydrogen bondspolar R-groups
Ionic bondsa positive and a negative R-group. Amine groups in R-groups gain H+ (become −NH3+), carboxyl groups lose H+ (become −COO−). This is why pH matters.
Disulfide bonds (covalent, the strongest)the sulfur atoms of two cysteines: −S−S−. Cysteine is the only amino acid you need to name.
Hydrophobic interactionsnon-polar R-groups, which cluster together away from water
Worked example: which bond? "Two R-groups meet when a chain folds: −CH2−COO− and −CH2−CH2−NH3+. Which bond forms?"
  1. Any −SH on both? No, so not a disulfide bond.
  2. Any charges? Yes: one negative, one positive. Opposite charges attract: an ionic bond.
Why this step? Why check for −SH first?Only cysteine (−SH) makes the covalent disulfide bond, so it is the quickest to rule out. Then check charges (ionic), then −OH or −NH (hydrogen bond); only C and H means hydrophobic.
Finish it. Which bond: (a) −CH2−OH with −CH2−OH; (b) −CH3 with −CH(CH3)2? First step done for (a): no −SH and no charges.
Show the answer(a) Both are polar (−OH), so a hydrogen bond. (b) Only carbon and hydrogen: non-polar, so a hydrophobic interaction.

B1.2.10 Effect of polar and non-polar amino acids on tertiary structure of proteins

In proteins that dissolve in water, hydrophobic amino acids cluster in the core, away from water; hydrophilic ones are on the surface. Integral proteins, which cross a membrane, have a band of hydrophobic amino acids inside the hydrophobic middle of the membrane and hydrophilic parts outside in the watery fluid.

B1.2.11 Quaternary structure of non-conjugated and conjugated proteins

Quaternary structure: two or more polypeptides joined into one protein. Not every protein has it; many are a single chain.

  • Non-conjugated: made only of polypeptides. Insulin: two chains joined by disulfide bonds. Collagen: three chains wound into a triple helix.
  • Conjugated: also has a non-polypeptide part (a prosthetic group). Haemoglobin: four chains, each with a haem group containing iron, which binds oxygen.

For context: cryogenic electron microscopy now lets scientists image single protein molecules, which the eye could never see.

B1.2.12 Relationship of form and function in globular and fibrous proteins

Globular, example insulinFibrous, example collagen
Shaperounded, compactlong, narrow strands
In waterusually solubleinsoluble
Form fits functioninsulin is a soluble hormone carried in blood; its precise shape binds to receptors on target cellsthree chains in a triple helix, many molecules cross-linked into fibres: great strength when pulled, in skin, tendons, ligaments and blood vessel walls

Learn the whole table.

Worked example: a 3-mark "suggest". "A mutation replaces a non-polar amino acid in the core of a soluble enzyme with a negatively charged one. Suggest the effect."
  1. The primary structure changes: one R-group is different.
  2. The new R-group is hydrophilic, so it cannot take part in the hydrophobic interactions of the core, and it may form ionic bonds in new places.
  3. So the chain folds differently: the tertiary structure, and possibly the active site, changes, and the enzyme may no longer bind its substrate.
Why this step? Why start from the primary structure?Mark schemes follow the chain: sequence, bonds, shape, function. Each link is usually one mark.
Finish it. Now a polar amino acid on the outer surface of the same enzyme is replaced by another polar one. Suggest the effect. First point done: the primary structure changes, but the new R-group is still hydrophilic.
Show the answerIt is still on the surface, where hydrophilic groups belong, and can still form hydrogen bonds, so the folding hardly changes and the enzyme probably still works: little or no effect.
Self-check B1.2 HL (write two key words for each on paper, then open)
  1. Between which groups do the hydrogen bonds of secondary structure form?
  2. Name the four bonds or interactions of tertiary structure.
  3. Which one is covalent, and which amino acid forms it?
  4. Where are hydrophobic R-groups in a soluble globular protein?
  5. Why is haemoglobin conjugated but insulin not?
  6. Give two differences between globular and fibrous proteins.

Answers. 1: C=O of one amino acid and N−H of another, in the backbone. 2: hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bonds, hydrophobic interactions. 3: the disulfide bond, between two cysteines. 4: in the core, away from water. 5: haemoglobin has haem groups (with iron), a non-polypeptide part; insulin is only polypeptide. 6: any two of rounded against long and narrow; soluble against insoluble; precise shape for binding against strength.

C1.1 · SL and HL

Enzymes and metabolism

Official guiding question: In what ways do enzymes interact with other molecules?

By the end of this section (official statement, then what it means for you):

C1.1.1 Enzymes as catalysts and C1.1.2 Role of enzymes in metabolism

A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up. Enzymes are biological catalysts, made of protein. They make reactions fast enough at body temperature for life. Metabolism is the complex network of interdependent and interacting chemical reactions in a living thing.Learn Each enzyme is specific, so many enzymes are needed, and a cell controls its metabolism through which enzymes it makes and how active they are.

C1.1.3 Anabolic and catabolic reactions

AnabolismCatabolism
What happensbuilds macromolecules from monomersbreaks macromolecules into monomers or smaller molecules
Type of reactioncondensationhydrolysis; oxidation in respiration
Energyneeds energyreleases energy
Examplesprotein synthesis, glycogen formation, photosynthesisdigestion, respiration

Learn the table. Metabolism = anabolism + catabolism.

C1.1.4 Enzymes as globular proteins with an active site for catalysis

Enzymes are globular proteins. The active site is the pocket where the substrate binds and reacts. It is made of only a few amino acids, but the folding of the whole enzyme holds them in place, giving the site its shape and chemical properties.

C1.1.5 Interactions between substrate and active site to allow induced-fit binding

When the substrate binds, both the substrate and the enzyme change shape slightly, so they fit more tightly: induced fit.Learn This strains bonds in the substrate, which helps them break. The substrate and enzyme together are the enzyme-substrate complex. It replaced the older "lock and key" model with a rigid active site.

C1.1.6 Role of molecular motion and substrate-active site collisions in enzyme catalysis

Substrate and enzyme molecules move at random. A reaction only happens when a substrate molecule collides with an active site; faster movement or more molecules means more collisions. Sometimes one side is fixed (immobilised): a large substrate such as DNA, or an enzyme embedded in a membrane, such as ATP synthase.

C1.1.7 Relationships between the structure of the active site, enzyme-substrate specificity and denaturation

Specificity: the active site's shape and chemical properties match (are complementary to) one substrate, so only that substrate binds. Denaturation: heat or extreme pH breaks the bonds that hold the enzyme's 3D shape, the active site changes shape, the substrate no longer fits, no enzyme-substrate complexes form, and activity is lost, usually for good.

C1.1.8 Effects of temperature, pH and substrate concentration on the rate of enzyme activity

  • Temperature. Rising temperature gives molecules more kinetic energy, so collisions with the active site are more frequent and the rate rises. Above the optimum, the enzyme denatures and the rate falls steeply.
  • pH. Each enzyme has an optimum pH (often near 7; the stomach enzyme pepsin about 2). Away from it, the charges on R-groups change, ionic and hydrogen bonds break, and the active site changes shape; at extreme pH the enzyme denatures.
  • Substrate concentration. More substrate means more collisions, so the rate rises. It levels off when all the active sites are occupied at any moment: the amount of enzyme now limits the rate.
Worked example: 2-mark "explain". "Explain the change in rate as the temperature rises from 10 °C to 35 °C (below the optimum)."
  1. The enzyme and substrate molecules gain kinetic energy and move faster.
  2. So they collide more often and more enzyme-substrate complexes form per second: the rate increases.
Why this step? Why mention enzyme-substrate complexes?The mark is for linking collisions to the active site: more complexes, more product per second.
Finish it. "Explain the change in rate above the optimum temperature." First point done: the bonds holding the enzyme's 3D shape vibrate and break.
Show the answerSo the active site changes shape and the substrate no longer fits; enzyme-substrate complexes cannot form; the enzyme is denatured and the rate falls.
Exam trap. "Describe" the graph = say what it does, with numbers from the axes ("rises to a peak at 40 °C, then falls to zero at 60 °C"). "Explain" = give the reasons. Always name the active site.

C1.1.9 Measurements in enzyme-catalysed reactions

Rate = amount of product made (or substrate used) ÷ time.Learn The unit is "amount per time", for example cm3 s−1 (cubic centimetres per second). Methods: collect the oxygen from catalase splitting hydrogen peroxide (gas syringe, or upside-down measuring cylinder of water); or time how long amylase takes to digest starch (iodine stops turning blue-black) and use rate = 1 ÷ time. Repeat three times and use the mean.

Worked example. "Catalase releases 24 cm3 of oxygen in 40 s. Calculate the rate."
  1. Rate = amount ÷ time = 24 ÷ 40 = 0.6.
  2. Units: cm3 ÷ s, so 0.6 cm3 s−1.
Why this step? Why write the units?There is often a separate mark for the unit: amount unit, then time unit with power −1.
Finish it. Starch disappears after 50 s at 30 °C and after 125 s at 20 °C. Use rate = 1 ÷ time. How many times faster is the reaction at 30 °C? First half done: at 30 °C, 1 ÷ 50 = 0.02 s−1.
Show the answerAt 20 °C, 1 ÷ 125 = 0.008 s−1. 0.02 ÷ 0.008 = 2.5 times faster.

C1.1.10 Effect of enzymes on activation energy

Energy is needed to break bonds in the substrate before it can react: the activation energy. Energy is released when the new bonds of the products form. Enzymes lower the activation energy, so many more molecules can react at body temperature.Learn The overall energy change of the reaction stays the same.

Self-check C1.1 (write two key words for each on paper, then open)
  1. Anabolic or catabolic: digestion of starch; glycogen formation?
  2. What does induced fit say that lock and key did not?
  3. Why does the rate level off at high substrate concentration?
  4. Why does an enzyme stop working at 70 °C, but not at 5 °C?
  5. 18 cm3 of oxygen in 45 s: what is the rate?
  6. Does an enzyme change the energy released by a reaction?

Answers. 1: catabolic; anabolic. 2: both the enzyme and the substrate change shape when binding. 3: all the active sites are occupied at any moment. 4: at 70 °C it is denatured (active site changes shape); at 5 °C it is only slow, because collisions are rare. 5: 18 ÷ 45 = 0.4 cm3 s−1. 6: no, it only lowers the activation energy.

C1.1 · additional higher level

Pathways and inhibitors HL

Official guiding question: What are the interdependent components of metabolism?

By the end of this section (official statement, then what it means for you):

C1.1.11 to C1.1.13 Where reactions happen, heat, and pathway shapes

  • Intracellular enzymes work inside cells: glycolysis in the cytoplasm, the Krebs cycle in the mitochondria. Extracellular enzymes are secreted and work outside cells: digestive enzymes in the gut.
  • Heat. No energy transfer in metabolism is 100% efficient, so some energy is always released as heat. Mammals and birds (and some other animals) depend on this heat to keep their body temperature constant.
  • Linear pathways run from a start molecule to an end product: glycolysis (glucose to pyruvate). In cyclical pathways the last step regenerates the first molecule: the Krebs cycle and the Calvin cycle (details in C1.2 and C1.3).

C1.1.14 Allosteric sites and non-competitive inhibition

An allosteric site is a binding site on the enzyme away from the active site; only specific substances fit it. When an inhibitor binds there, the enzyme changes shape and the active site changes enough to stop catalysis. Binding is reversible. The inhibitor does not compete with the substrate, so adding more substrate does not help: the maximum rate is lower.

C1.1.15 Competitive inhibition

A competitive inhibitor has a shape similar to the substrate and binds reversibly to the active site, blocking it. Substrate and inhibitor compete, so at high substrate concentration the substrate wins most collisions and the rate gets close to the normal maximum. Example: statins inhibit an enzyme in the liver that makes cholesterol, lowering blood cholesterol.

C1.1.16 Regulation of metabolic pathways by feedback inhibition

The end product of a pathway inhibits the first enzyme of that pathway, by binding to its allosteric site. Example: the pathway from threonine to isoleucine. When isoleucine builds up, it binds to the first enzyme (threonine deaminase) and the whole pathway slows. When isoleucine is used up, it leaves the enzyme and the pathway restarts: no waste.

C1.1.17 Mechanism-based inhibition

The inhibitor binds to the active site and is chemically changed there, forming a permanent covalent bond: the enzyme is blocked for good. Penicillin does this to transpeptidase, the bacterial enzyme that builds cross-links in the cell wall. Without them the wall is weak and the bacterium bursts. Resistance: in some bacteria a change in the transpeptidase's active site means penicillin no longer binds, while the enzyme still works.

Exam trap. Non-competitive inhibitors do not "block the active site"; they change its shape from elsewhere. And competitive inhibition is reversible: say so, because mark schemes often give a mark for it.

1. A polypeptide of 150 amino acids is made by condensation reactions. How many water molecules are released?

One water molecule per peptide bond, and a chain of n amino acids has n − 1 peptide bonds: 149. 150 forgets that the first amino acid has nothing to join to yet.

2. Which statement about essential amino acids is correct?

"Essential" means essential in the diet. Option 4 describes non-essential amino acids. Plant foods do contain essential amino acids, which is why a varied vegan diet can supply them all.

3. An enzyme is heated to 80 °C and denatured. Which bonds are NOT broken?

Denaturation breaks the weaker bonds that hold the 3D shape. Peptide bonds are strong covalent bonds in the chain itself, so the primary structure is unchanged.

4. (HL) What holds an alpha helix in shape?

Secondary structure comes from hydrogen bonds in the backbone, not from R-groups. Disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions belong to tertiary structure; peptide bonds make the primary structure.

5. (HL) Which protein is conjugated?

Conjugated means it has a non-polypeptide part: haemoglobin's haem groups with iron. Insulin and collagen have several chains but are only polypeptide, so they are non-conjugated. Several chains is quaternary structure, not conjugation.

6. In an enzyme experiment the rate stops rising when more substrate is added. Why?

With excess substrate every active site is busy, so the amount of enzyme limits the rate. Substrate does not denature enzymes, and if substrate had run out the rate would fall, not level off.

7. (HL) Isoleucine binds to the allosteric site of the first enzyme in the pathway that makes isoleucine. This is an example of

The end product inhibits the first enzyme of its own pathway: feedback (end-product) inhibition. It binds the allosteric site reversibly, so it is not competitive and not mechanism-based.
