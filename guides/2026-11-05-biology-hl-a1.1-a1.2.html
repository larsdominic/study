Atoms, elements and molecules

Everything is made of tiny particles called atoms. An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom. Living things are built mainly from hydrogen (H), carbon (C), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), phosphorus (P) and sulfur (S).

Each atom has a tiny positive centre, with even smaller negative electrons moving around it.

Atoms join together to make molecules. A formula tells you which atoms: H2O is 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. The small low number after a symbol counts the atoms of that element. Water is a tiny molecule; DNA and proteins contain thousands or millions of atoms.

Bonds: strong ones and weak ones

A bond is what holds particles together. Two kinds matter today:

  • Covalent bond: two atoms share a pair of electrons. It is strong and holds the atoms of one molecule together. Drawn as a solid line, for example O-H.
  • Hydrogen bond: a weak attraction between molecules (or between two distant parts of one big molecule). Drawn as a dashed line. About 20 times weaker than a covalent bond, but there are huge numbers of them.

Rule of thumb: strong covalent bonds build a molecule; weak hydrogen bonds decide how molecules stick to each other, and so how a substance behaves.

Charges, ions and polarity

Electric charge comes in two kinds, positive (+) and negative (−). Opposite charges attract; like charges repel (push apart).

An ion carries a full charge because it has lost or gained electrons: Na+, Cl−.

In some covalent bonds the electrons are shared unevenly. Oxygen pulls them harder than hydrogen, so in water the O end becomes slightly negative (δ−) and each H end slightly positive (δ+). The Greek letter δ (delta) means "a little". A molecule with δ+ and δ− ends is polar. When electrons are shared evenly, as in oils and fats, there are no charged ends: non-polar.

Polar things attract polar and charged things. Non-polar things do not mix with them.

Dissolving

When a substance dissolves, it breaks up into single molecules or ions that spread evenly through a liquid. The liquid is the solvent, the substance that dissolves is the solute, and the mixture is a solution. Sugar (solute) in tea (solvent) makes a solution.

"Like dissolves like": water is polar, so it dissolves ions and polar molecules, but not non-polar ones such as oil, which stays as a separate layer.

Inside cells and in blood, almost everything that moves around or reacts is dissolved in water.

Heat, temperature and energy units

Energy is measured in joules (J); 1 kJ = 1 000 J. Temperature (in °C) tells you how fast the particles are moving on average. Heating gives the particles energy, so they move faster and the temperature rises.

Water exists as solid ice (below 0 °C), liquid water, and a gas called water vapour. At 100 °C it boils. Liquid turning into gas at the surface is evaporating; gas turning back into liquid is condensing.

In units, a power of −1 means "per": J kg−1 °C−1 is read "joules per kilogram per degree Celsius".

Cells and the big molecules of life

All living things are made of cells. A cell has a thin outer membrane and watery cytoplasm inside. Animal, plant and fungus cells also have a nucleus that holds the DNA; bacteria are simpler cells with no nucleus.

Four groups of large molecules do most of the work in cells:

  • carbohydrates: sugars, starch, cellulose
  • lipids: fats, oils, phospholipids
  • proteins: chains of amino acids; enzymes are proteins
  • nucleic acids: DNA and RNA

Many large molecules are polymers: long chains of small repeating units (monomers), like beads on a string.

Isotopes: what 32P and 35S mean

The centre of an atom contains particles called protons and neutrons. All atoms of one element have the same number of protons, but the number of neutrons can differ. These different forms of the same element are isotopes.

The small raised number is the mass number (protons + neutrons). 32P is phosphorus with mass number 32; 35S is sulfur with mass number 35.

Both are radioactive: they give off radiation that a detector can measure. Living things use them just like normal phosphorus and sulfur, so they work as tags to follow one kind of molecule through an experiment.

Powers, percentages and ratios

Powers: 43 means 4 × 4 × 4 = 64. The small raised number says how many 4s are multiplied together, so 4n means n fours multiplied, where n can be any whole number.

Percentages: "per cent" means "out of 100". All the parts of a whole add up to 100%. To find 28% of 4 000, work out 0.28 × 4 000 = 1 120.

A ratio such as A : T compares two amounts. Dividing one by the other gives a single number; an answer close to 1 means the two amounts are almost equal.

A1.1.1 Water as the medium for life

The first cells arose in water, and water is still the medium in which most processes of life happen. The cytoplasm is mostly water, and the reactions of metabolism happen between molecules dissolved in it.

A1.1.2 Polar bonds and hydrogen bonds

In a water molecule, oxygen shares a pair of electrons with each hydrogen atom: two covalent bonds. Oxygen is more electronegative: it pulls the shared electrons harder, so it gets a small negative charge, δ−, and each hydrogen a small positive charge, δ+. These are polar covalent bonds, and because the molecule is bent, the whole molecule is polar.

A hydrogen bond is the attraction between a δ+ hydrogen of one water molecule and the δ− oxygen of another.Learn One hydrogen bond is weak, but there are huge numbers of them.

Worked example: a 3-mark explanation. "Explain how hydrogen bonds form between water molecules." One marking point per step:
  1. Oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen, so the electrons in each O-H covalent bond are pulled towards the oxygen.
  2. So oxygen is slightly negative (δ−) and each hydrogen slightly positive (δ+): the bonds are polar, and as the molecule is bent, the whole molecule is polar.
  3. The δ+ hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of a neighbouring molecule: this attraction is a hydrogen bond.
Why this step? Why does step 2 say the molecule is bent?In a straight molecule the two δ+ ends would sit on opposite sides and the charges would cancel. The bend leaves a δ− end and a δ+ side, so whole molecules attract each other.
Exam trap. Hydrogen bonds are between molecules; the O-H bonds inside one molecule are covalent. In drawings: δ− on O, δ+ on H, and the dashed line never joins H to H or O to O.

A1.1.3 Cohesion

Cohesion is water molecules sticking to each other, because of hydrogen bonds between them.Learn Two consequences:

  • Transport under tension in xylem. Water evaporating from leaves pulls on the water in the xylem. Cohesion lets the whole column be pulled up without breaking.
  • Surface tension. Hydrogen bonds at the surface make it act like a thin skin, so small animals such as pond skaters can live on it.

A1.1.4 Adhesion

Adhesion is water molecules sticking to other substances that are polar or charged.Learn Cellulose in plant cell walls, xylem walls, glass and soil particles are polar, so water adheres to them; it does not adhere to non-polar wax.

Capillary action: adhesion pulls water along narrow polar spaces, and cohesion drags the next molecules behind. This moves water through plant cell walls and holds it in the small spaces in soil, where roots can reach it.

Finish it. "Explain why water rises up a very narrow glass tube." [3]

  2. Your turn: ...
  3. Your turn: ...
Show the answer2: Adhesion pulls the water up along the walls of the narrow tube. 3: Cohesion (hydrogen bonds between water molecules) pulls the molecules behind up too, so the whole column rises: capillary action.
Exam trap. Cohesion is water to water; adhesion is water to something else. A meniscus, or water climbing a wall, is adhesion: mark schemes refuse "cohesion" or "surface tension" for it.

A1.1.5 Water as a solvent

Water molecules are polar, so they are attracted to ions and to other polar molecules. When salt dissolves, the δ− oxygen ends surround each Na+ ion and the δ+ hydrogen ends surround each Cl− ion, so the ions separate and spread out. Polar molecules such as glucose dissolve because they form hydrogen bonds with water.

  • Hydrophilic: attracted to water. Ions and polar molecules: salts, glucose, amino acids.Learn
  • Hydrophobic: not attracted to water because non-polar. Fats and oils cluster together instead of dissolving.Learn
  • Amphipathic: a hydrophilic part and a hydrophobic part in the same molecule, such as a phospholipid (phosphate head, fatty acid tails).Learn

Medium for metabolism: most enzymes catalyse reactions in solution, where dissolved molecules move and collide. Medium for transport: blood plasma carries dissolved glucose, amino acids, ions and urea; xylem sap carries mineral ions. Some molecules work because they are hydrophobic and insoluble, such as the phospholipids of membranes.

Water's specific heat capacity is about 4 200 J kg−1 °C−1. It is high because heat must first break hydrogen bonds before the molecules can move faster.

The guide's two examples: the black-throated loon (Gavia arctica), a bird that flies in air and dives under water, with a streamlined body and dense bones that cut its buoyancy; and the ringed seal (Pusa hispida), insulated against icy water by thick blubber. In the exam either the common or the scientific name is accepted.

Also in your packs: evaporating water takes a lot of energy (high latent heat of vaporisation), so the evaporation of sweat cools the body.

A1.1.7 Where Earth's water came from HL

The IB limits this to one hypothesis: much of Earth's water was brought by asteroids that collided with the young Earth (an extraplanetary origin). Earth kept the water for two reasons: gravity, strong enough to hold the atmosphere and its water vapour, and temperatures low enough for water vapour to condense into liquid oceans.

A1.1.8 Water and the search for life elsewhere HL

All known life needs liquid water, so the search for life on other planets and moons starts with the search for water. The Goldilocks zone is the range of distances from a star where a planet's surface is neither too hot nor too cold for liquid water.Learn Earth is inside the Sun's Goldilocks zone.

A1.2.1 DNA is the genetic material

All living organisms store genetic information as DNA. Some viruses, such as HIV, use RNA; viruses are not considered living.

A1.2.2 The parts of a nucleotide

DNA and RNA are polynucleotides: polymers ("poly" = many) of monomers called nucleotides. Each nucleotide has three parts:Learn

  1. a phosphate group (draw a circle),
  2. a pentose sugar with 5 carbon atoms (draw a pentagon): deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA,
  3. a nitrogen-containing base (draw a rectangle).

A1.2.3 The sugar-phosphate backbone

The phosphate of one nucleotide forms a covalent bond with the sugar of the next, making a strong, continuous chain: the sugar-phosphate backbone. The bases stick out to the side.

A1.2.4 Bases: the code

DNA has adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G) and thymine (T). RNA has adenine, cytosine, guanine and uracil (U) instead of thymine.Learn Only the base sequence varies, so it carries the information, like letters in a text.

A1.2.5 RNA is made by condensation

RNA is a single strand. Each new nucleotide joins it in a condensation reaction: a covalent bond forms between phosphate and sugar, releasing water. To draw RNA: pentagons joined by circles, a rectangle (A, C, G or U) on each pentagon, sugars labelled "ribose".

A1.2.6 DNA: a double helix

DNA has two strands twisted into a double helix, joined by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases: adenine with thymine, guanine with cytosine.Learn The strands run in opposite directions (antiparallel); draw them that way, but you need not draw the twist.

A1.2.8 Why complementary pairing matters

Pairing depends on hydrogen bonds, and A only pairs with T, G only with C, so each strand can act as a template for the other. In replication, new nucleotides pair with each old strand, giving two identical DNA molecules. In expression, RNA nucleotides pair with a DNA template (transcription).

Worked example: base percentages. "A double-stranded DNA molecule is 2 000 base pairs long and 22% of its bases are adenine. Find the percentage of each base and the number of guanine nucleotides."
  1. A pairs with T, so T = A = 22%.
  2. A + T = 44%, so G + C = 100% − 44% = 56%, and G = C = 56% ÷ 2 = 28%.
  3. 2 000 base pairs = 2 × 2 000 = 4 000 nucleotides.
  4. Guanine nucleotides = 0.28 × 4 000 = 1 120.
Why this step? Why double the 2 000 in step 3?A base pair is two nucleotides, one on each strand, and the percentages are of all the bases in the molecule.
Finish it. "A DNA molecule of 1 000 base pairs has 35% cytosine. Find the percentage of adenine and the number of adenine nucleotides."
  1. C pairs with G, so G = 35% and G + C = 70%.
  2. Your turn: A + T and A.
  3. Your turn: nucleotides and adenine count.
Show the answer2: A + T = 100% − 70% = 30%, so A = 15%. 3: 1 000 base pairs = 2 000 nucleotides; adenine = 0.15 × 2 000 = 300.
Exam trap. A = T and G = C only work for double-stranded DNA. Write base names in full (adenine, not A) and label sugars "ribose" or "deoxyribose", not just "sugar". No thymine in RNA.

A1.2.9 Limitless information

Any base can follow any other, and DNA can have any length. With 4 bases possible at each position, n bases can be arranged in 4n ways (5 bases: 45 = 1 024), so DNA stores enormous information in very little space.

A1.2.10 One code for all life

The genetic code (which three bases stand for which amino acid) is almost the same in all organisms. Nothing in chemistry forces it, so all life most likely inherited it from one ancestor: universal common ancestry.

A1.2.11 Directionality

The 5′ end of a strand has a phosphate on carbon 5′; the 3′ end has a free -OH on carbon 3′.Learn Each phosphate links carbon 5′ of one sugar to carbon 3′ of the next, so a strand has a direction. New nucleotides are only added at the 3′ end, so DNA (replication) and RNA (transcription) are built 5′ to 3′, and ribosomes read messenger RNA 5′ to 3′ (translation).

Worked example: write the complementary strand. "One strand is 5′-AGCTTAGC-3′. Write its partner, then write it 5′ to 3′."
  1. Pair each base: A G C T T A G C gives T C G A A T C G.
  2. Antiparallel, so opposite the 5′ end is a 3′ end: 3′-TCGAATCG-5′.
  3. Read it backwards to write it 5′ to 3′: 5′-GCTAAGCT-3′.
Why this step? Why read it backwards in step 3?Sequences are written 5′ to 3′. The partner runs the other way, so its 5′ end is on the right in step 2.

A1.2.12 Purine-to-pyrimidine pairing

Purines (adenine, guanine) have two rings; pyrimidines (cytosine, thymine, uracil) have one.Learn Each pair is a purine plus a pyrimidine, so A-T and C-G pairs have equal length and the helix has the same shape whatever the sequence: stable.

A1.2.13 The nucleosome

In eukaryotes, a nucleosome is DNA wrapped around a core of eight histone proteins, held in place by a further histone (H1) attached to linker DNA, which runs on to the next nucleosome.Learn Positive histones attract the negative phosphates of DNA. Nucleosomes help supercoil DNA, packing a very long molecule into a small nucleus. Prokaryotes have no histones, so no nucleosomes. (The IB expects you to have viewed one in molecular visualisation software: a 3D model you can rotate.)

A1.2.14 The Hershey-Chase experiment

Hershey and Chase (1952) used a virus that infects bacteria, made only of a protein coat and DNA.

1. In liquid water, hydrogen bonds form between

A hydrogen bond is between molecules: δ+ H of one, δ− O of another. Inside one molecule the O-H bonds are covalent (option 1). H with H, or O with O, would bring like charges together, which repel.

2. Water moves through the narrow spaces in plant cell walls, even upwards, mainly because of

Cellulose is polar, so water adheres to it and is pulled along the narrow spaces; cohesion drags the next molecules behind: capillary action. Cohesion alone has nothing to pull against. Specific heat capacity is about temperature, not movement.

3. A ringed seal loses body heat much faster in water at 2 °C than it would in air at 2 °C. Which property of water best explains this?

Water conducts heat away from the skin about 24 times faster than air. Viscosity and buoyancy affect movement and support, not heat. Latent heat of vaporisation is about cooling by evaporation, as in sweating.

4. (HL) Which statement best explains why Earth has kept liquid water at its surface?

Retention needs gravity and temperatures that let water vapour condense. Asteroids are the hypothesis for where the water came from, not why it stays. Earth is inside the Goldilocks zone, not outside it.

5. Which components make up the backbone of one strand of DNA?

The backbone is sugar-phosphate-sugar-phosphate, linked by covalent bonds, and in DNA the sugar is deoxyribose. Ribose is the RNA sugar. Hydrogen bonds link the two strands through the bases; they are not part of the backbone.

6. In a sample of double-stranded DNA, 18% of the bases are guanine. What percentage of the bases are adenine?

G = C = 18%, so G + C = 36% and A + T = 64%. A = 64% ÷ 2 = 32%. 18% assumes A = G; 36% is G + C; 64% is A + T, forgetting to halve.

7. Which feature is found in RNA but not in DNA?

RNA has ribose and uracil; DNA has deoxyribose and thymine. Cytosine and phosphate are in both. Two antiparallel strands describe DNA, not RNA.

8. Explain how the polarity of water molecules allows sodium chloride to dissolve in water. [3]

Any three of these marking points, [1 mark] each:
