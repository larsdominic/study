A1.1 · SL and HL

Water

One small, bent, polar molecule. Almost every property that makes water the medium of life comes from the hydrogen bonds between its molecules.

By the end of this section I can

A1.1.1 Water as the medium for life

Life is thought to have begun in water, and every cell still works in it. The fluid part of the cytoplasm, the cytosol, is mostly water, and the chemical reactions of metabolism happen between molecules dissolved in it. Water is also a reactant (in hydrolysis and in photosynthesis) and a product (in condensation reactions and in respiration). Even xerophytic plants, which are adapted to very dry places, keep water inside their cells: they are adapted to save it, not to live without it.

A1.1.2 Polar bonds and hydrogen bonds

Inside a water molecule, oxygen shares a pair of electrons with each hydrogen atom: two covalent bonds. Oxygen attracts shared electrons much more strongly than hydrogen does (it is more electronegative), so the electrons spend more time near the oxygen. The oxygen end gets a small negative charge, δ−, and each hydrogen a small positive charge, δ+. These are polar covalent bonds. Because the molecule is bent (about 104.5°), the charges do not cancel out: the whole molecule is polar.

A hydrogen bond is the attraction between a δ+ hydrogen of one molecule and the δ− oxygen (or nitrogen) of another molecule.Learn One hydrogen bond is weak, roughly a twentieth of the strength of a covalent bond, and hydrogen bonds keep breaking and re-forming in liquid water. But there are so many that together they hold water together strongly.

Worked example: a 3-mark explanation. "Explain how hydrogen bonds form between water molecules." A model answer, one marking point per step:
  1. Oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen, so the electrons in each O-H covalent bond are pulled towards the oxygen.
  2. This makes the oxygen slightly negative (δ−) and the hydrogens slightly positive (δ+): the bonds are polar, and because the molecule is bent the whole molecule is polar.
  3. The δ+ hydrogen of one water molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of a neighbouring molecule: this attraction is a hydrogen bond.
Check: three separate ideas (cause, charges, attraction between molecules), each in its own sentence.
Exam trap. Hydrogen bonds are between molecules. The O-H bonds inside a water molecule are covalent. "Water has hydrogen bonds between its hydrogen and oxygen atoms" loses the mark unless you add "of neighbouring molecules". In drawings, the δ+ goes on H and the δ− on O, and the dashed line never joins H to H or O to O.

A1.1.3 Cohesion

Cohesion is water molecules sticking to each other, because of hydrogen bonds between them.Learn Two consequences for living things:

  • Transport under tension in xylem. Water evaporates from leaves (transpiration) and this pulls on the water in the xylem. Because the molecules cohere, the whole column is pulled up as one, even in a tree tens of metres tall, without breaking. The water is under tension (negative pressure), which is why xylem vessels need strong walls that do not collapse.
  • Surface tension. At the surface, water molecules are pulled inwards and sideways by hydrogen bonds, so the surface behaves like a thin elastic skin. Small animals such as pond skaters (water striders) can stand on it without sinking.

A1.1.4 Adhesion

Adhesion is water molecules sticking to other substances that are polar or charged, again by hydrogen bonds or attraction to charges.Learn Cellulose in plant cell walls, the lignified walls of xylem, glass and the surfaces of soil particles are all polar, so water adheres to them. Water is not attracted to non-polar, waxy surfaces.

  • Capillary action. Adhesion pulls water up and along narrow polar spaces; cohesion drags the molecules behind along with it. This moves water through the tiny spaces in plant cell walls, helps water rise in narrow xylem vessels, and holds water in the small spaces between soil particles, where roots can reach it.
  • The curved surface of water in a glass tube (a meniscus) is caused by adhesion to the glass.
Worked example: cohesion or adhesion? "Rain forms round drops on a waxy leaf, but spreads out and soaks into a paper towel. Explain."
  1. The leaf cuticle is wax, which is non-polar. Water cannot form hydrogen bonds with it, so there is almost no adhesion.
  2. Cohesion (hydrogen bonds between the water molecules) then pulls the water into the shape with the smallest surface: a round drop.
  3. A paper towel is made of cellulose, which has many polar -OH groups. Water forms hydrogen bonds with it: strong adhesion.
  4. Adhesion pulls the water along the narrow gaps between the cellulose fibres, and cohesion pulls more water behind it: capillary action soaks the towel.
Exam trap. Cohesion is water to water; adhesion is water to something else. A meniscus, or water climbing a wall, is adhesion; mark schemes do not accept "cohesion" or "surface tension" for it. If asked about a column of water moving up the xylem, give both: cohesion keeps the column unbroken, adhesion to the walls helps it rise and stops it falling back.

A1.1.5 Water as a solvent

Because water molecules are polar, they are attracted to other polar molecules and to ions. When salt dissolves, the δ− oxygen ends of water molecules surround each positive Na+ ion and the δ+ hydrogen ends surround each negative Cl− ion, so the ions separate and spread through the water. Polar molecules such as glucose dissolve because they form hydrogen bonds with water.

  • Hydrophilic ("water-loving"): attracted to water. Ions and polar molecules: salts, glucose, amino acids.Learn
  • Hydrophobic ("water-hating"): not attracted to water because they are non-polar. Fats and oils, waxes; they cluster together in water instead of dissolving.Learn
  • Amphipathic: one part hydrophilic and another part hydrophobic in the same molecule. Phospholipids: hydrophilic phosphate head, hydrophobic fatty acid tails. This is why they form membranes.Learn

Medium for metabolism. Dissolved molecules can move around and collide, so reactions can happen; enzymes act on dissolved substrates in the cytoplasm. Medium for transport. Blood plasma carries dissolved glucose, amino acids, ions and urea around the body. Xylem sap carries dissolved mineral ions up from the roots; phloem sap carries dissolved sucrose.

A1.1.6 Physical properties of water and life in aquatic habitats

Animals that live in water face a very different physical world from animals in air. Four properties to compare:

The specific heat capacity of water is about 4 200 J kg−1 °C−1 (4.2 J per gram). It is high because heat energy must first break hydrogen bonds before the molecules can move faster; only then does the temperature rise.

Two animals often used to show this:

  • Black-throated loon (Gavia arctica), a diving bird that lives both in air and under water. It flies in air, but under water it swims with feet set far back on a streamlined body, which reduces drag. It has heavier, less air-filled bones than most flying birds and can press air out of its feathers, so it is less buoyant and can dive.
  • Ringed seal (Pusa hispida), an Arctic mammal that lives in icy water. A thick layer of blubber (fat conducts heat poorly) insulates it against the high thermal conductivity of cold water, and also adds buoyancy. Its streamlined body reduces drag. It must surface to breathe air, using breathing holes it keeps open in the ice.

Also in your packs: two more thermal properties

  • High latent heat of vaporisation. Evaporating water takes a lot of energy (about 2.4 kJ per gram at body temperature), because every molecule that escapes must break its hydrogen bonds. So evaporation of sweat, of water during panting, or of water from leaves in transpiration removes a lot of heat: it cools.Learn
  • Ice floats. In ice, hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open lattice, so ice is less dense than liquid water. Ice forms on top of ponds and insulates the water below, so aquatic organisms survive winter.
Worked example: why does a seal need blubber? "A ringed seal and a land mammal of the same size both have a body temperature of 37 °C. The seal swims in water at 2 °C, the land mammal stands in air at 2 °C. Explain why the seal needs much more insulation."
  1. Both lose heat from the body surface to the colder surroundings, because there is a 35 °C temperature difference.
  2. Water has a much higher thermal conductivity than air (about 24 times), so heat passes from the skin into water far faster.
  3. Water also has a high specific heat capacity, so the water next to the skin absorbs a lot of heat without warming up much; the temperature difference stays large.
  4. So without insulation the seal would lose heat many times faster than the land mammal. Blubber, a thick layer of fat, conducts heat poorly and slows this loss.

A1.1.7 Where Earth's water came from HL

Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago as a very hot ball of rock. A leading hypothesis is that much of the water now on Earth was brought later from outside the planet (it is extraplanetary), mainly by asteroids (and possibly comets) that collided with Earth. Evidence: water locked in certain asteroids (carbonaceous chondrite meteorites) has a ratio of heavy hydrogen (deuterium) to ordinary hydrogen similar to that of Earth's oceans.

Why Earth kept its water:

  • Gravity: Earth is massive enough to hold on to an atmosphere, including water vapour, so the water does not escape into space.
  • Temperature: Earth's distance from the Sun keeps most of the surface between 0 °C and 100 °C, so water vapour condenses and stays liquid as oceans.

A1.1.8 Water and the search for life elsewhere HL

All life we know needs liquid water, so the search for life on other planets and moons starts by looking for water. The Goldilocks zone (habitable zone) is the range of distances from a star where a planet's surface is neither too hot (water boils away) nor too cold (water freezes), so liquid water can exist.Learn Earth is inside the Sun's Goldilocks zone. Places studied for possible life include Mars (polar ice and signs that liquid water flowed in the past) and moons such as Europa and Enceladus, which are outside the zone but have liquid oceans under their ice because they are heated from inside.

Worked example (HL). "Mars has water ice at its poles. Suggest why there is no liquid water on its surface today."
  1. Mars is farther from the Sun than Earth: its average surface temperature is about −60 °C, so water freezes.
  2. Mars has a much smaller mass and weaker gravity than Earth, and has lost most of its atmosphere; surface pressure is less than 1% of Earth's.
  3. At such low pressure, any liquid water warmed above freezing would quickly evaporate. So water exists as ice or vapour, not as a liquid.
  4. Link to A1.1.8: no surface liquid water makes surface life unlikely, which is why searches look for water underground or in the past.

Answers. 1: oxygen is more electronegative than hydrogen, so it pulls the shared electrons of the O-H bonds towards itself. 2: compare with the figure above: each dashed line must go from an H of one molecule to the O of another; δ− on O, δ+ on each H. 3: adhesion; cellulose has many polar -OH groups, so water forms hydrogen bonds with it (with cohesion pulling the next molecules along: capillary action). 4: amphipathic: it has a hydrophilic phosphate head and hydrophobic fatty acid tails. 5: water is dense and gives a large buoyant force; a seal with blubber has an overall density close to water so it floats easily, while a diving loon must push against that upward force (it reduces buoyancy with dense bones and by pressing air out of its feathers). 6: Earth's gravity holds its atmosphere and water vapour; its distance from the Sun keeps temperatures mostly between 0 and 100 °C so water condenses and stays liquid. The Goldilocks zone is the range of distances from a star where liquid water can exist on a planet's surface.

A1.2.1 DNA is the genetic material

All living organisms, from bacteria to humans, store their genetic information as DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid). Some viruses use RNA (ribonucleic acid) as their genetic material instead, for example influenza viruses, HIV and the coronavirus that causes COVID-19. Viruses are not usually counted as living organisms, because they cannot carry out metabolism or reproduce outside a host cell; that is why the statement says "all living organisms".

A1.2.2 The parts of a nucleotide

DNA and RNA are polynucleotides: polymers ("poly" = many) made of monomers called nucleotides. Each nucleotide has three parts:Learn

  1. a phosphate group (draw a circle),
  2. a pentose sugar, with 5 carbon atoms (draw a pentagon): deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA,
  3. a nitrogen-containing base (draw a rectangle).

A1.2.3 The sugar-phosphate backbone

Nucleotides are joined by a covalent bond between the phosphate of one nucleotide and the carbon 3′ of the sugar of the previous one (this link is called a phosphodiester bond). Alternating sugar and phosphate groups form a strong, continuous chain: the sugar-phosphate backbone. The bases stick out to the side of the backbone, in any order.

A1.2.4 Bases: the code

DNA has four bases: adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G) and thymine (T). RNA has adenine, cytosine, guanine and uracil (U): uracil replaces thymine.Learn The backbone is the same along the whole molecule; only the sequence of bases varies, so the base sequence is what carries the genetic information, like the order of letters in a text.

A1.2.5 RNA is made by condensation

RNA is a single strand of nucleotides. Each new nucleotide is joined to the growing strand in a condensation reaction: the bond between phosphate and sugar forms and a molecule of water is released. (Breaking the bond again uses water: hydrolysis.) A drawing of an RNA strand is a vertical chain of pentagons and circles, each pentagon with a rectangle (A, C, G or U) on its side, and the label "ribose" on the sugars.

A1.2.6 DNA: a double helix

DNA is made of two strands, twisted into a double helix. The bases of one strand pair with the bases of the other, held by hydrogen bonds. Pairing is complementary: adenine pairs with thymine, and guanine pairs with cytosine.Learn The two strands run in opposite directions: they are antiparallel (one runs 5′ to 3′, the other 3′ to 5′; see the HL section).

A1.2.8 Why complementary pairing matters

Because A always pairs with T and G with C, one strand fixes the sequence of the other. So each strand can be a template: in replication, new nucleotides pair with the bases of each old strand, giving two identical DNA molecules. In expression, RNA nucleotides pair with a DNA template to make messenger RNA (transcription), and then transfer RNA anticodons pair with the messenger RNA codons (translation). You will meet the details in D1.1 and D1.2; for now, the key point is that the pairing rule makes copying accurate.

Worked example: base percentages. "A double-stranded DNA molecule is 2 000 base pairs long and 22% of its bases are adenine. Find the percentage of each base and the number of guanine nucleotides."
  1. A pairs with T, so T = A = 22%.
  2. A + T = 44%, so G + C = 100% − 44% = 56%.
  3. G pairs with C, so G = C = 56% ÷ 2 = 28%.
  4. 2 000 base pairs = 2 × 2 000 = 4 000 nucleotides (two per pair).
  5. Guanine nucleotides = 28% of 4 000 = 0.28 × 4 000 = 1 120.
Check: A and T are 880 each (22% of 4 000), G and C 1 120 each: 880 + 880 + 1 120 + 1 120 = 4 000. Correct.
Exam trap. These rules (A = T, G = C) only work for double-stranded DNA, and only for the whole molecule. In one strand on its own the four bases can be in any amounts. And a "base pair" is two nucleotides: double the number of base pairs before you count nucleotides.

A1.2.9 Limitless information

Any base can follow any other, so with 4 possible bases at each position, a sequence of n bases can be arranged in 4n different ways. This number grows so quickly that DNA can store practically limitless information.

Worked example. "How many different sequences of 5 bases are possible? What is the shortest group of bases that could give each of the 20 amino acids its own code?"
  1. 5 bases: 45 = 4 × 4 × 4 × 4 × 4 = 1 024 sequences.
  2. Groups of 1 base give 41 = 4 codes: fewer than 20.
  3. Groups of 2 give 42 = 16 codes: still fewer than 20.
  4. Groups of 3 give 43 = 64 codes: at least 20. So the shortest is 3 bases, which is why the genetic code uses triplets (codons).
For comparison: 10 bases give 410 = 1 048 576 sequences, already more than a million.

A1.2.10 One code for all life

The genetic code (which triplet of bases stands for which amino acid) is the same in almost all organisms: bacteria, plants, fungi and animals. There is no chemical reason why a particular triplet must mean a particular amino acid, so the best explanation for everyone sharing the same code is that all life inherited it from a common ancestor: universal common ancestry. (A few small variations exist, for example in mitochondria, but the code is otherwise conserved.) This is also why a human gene can be read correctly by a bacterium in genetic engineering.

Exam trap. Write the bases in full the first time (adenine, thymine, cytosine, guanine, uracil): one mark scheme in your pack refuses the letters alone. Label RNA sugars "ribose" and DNA sugars "deoxyribose", not just "sugar" or "pentose". And never put thymine in RNA.

A1.2.11 Directionality

Every strand of DNA or RNA has two different ends. At the 5′ end there is a phosphate attached to carbon 5′ of the first sugar. At the 3′ end there is a sugar with a free -OH on carbon 3′.Learn Inside the strand, each phosphate links carbon 5′ of one sugar to carbon 3′ of the next, so the strand has a direction, 5′ to 3′, like an arrow.

Why it matters:

  • Replication: DNA polymerase can only add new nucleotides to the free 3′ end, so new DNA is always built in the 5′ to 3′ direction.
  • Transcription: RNA is also built 5′ to 3′.
  • Translation: ribosomes read messenger RNA from its 5′ end towards its 3′ end.

In the double helix the two strands are antiparallel: one runs 5′ to 3′ and its partner runs 3′ to 5′ alongside it. In the figure above, the top strand runs 5′ to 3′ left to right, the bottom strand 5′ to 3′ right to left.

Worked example: write the complementary strand. "One strand of DNA is 5′-AGCTTAGC-3′. Write its complementary strand, showing both ends, then write it in the 5′ to 3′ direction."
  1. Pair each base: A with T, G with C. A G C T T A G C gives T C G A A T C G.
  2. The partner strand is antiparallel, so under the 5′ end of the first strand is the 3′ end of the second: 3′-TCGAATCG-5′.
  3. Sequences are normally written 5′ to 3′, so read it backwards: 5′-GCTAAGCT-3′.
Check: line up 5′-AGCTTAGC-3′ above 3′-TCGAATCG-5′: every pair is A-T or G-C, and the ends are opposite. Correct.

A1.2.12 Purine-to-pyrimidine pairing

The bases come in two shapes. Purines, adenine and guanine, have two rings. Pyrimidines, cytosine, thymine and uracil, have one ring.Learn Every base pair in DNA is one purine plus one pyrimidine (A-T, G-C). So every rung of the ladder is the same length (three rings across), and the two backbones stay the same distance apart, about 2 nm, along the whole molecule (look at the figure: the long and short rectangles always add up to the same length). A purine-purine pair would be too wide and a pyrimidine-pyrimidine pair too narrow; either would distort the helix. The regular, constant width lets the base pairs stack neatly on top of one another, which makes the double helix stable. The hydrogen bonds also only line up correctly for A with T and G with C, so the pairing is specific.

A1.2.13 The nucleosome

In eukaryotes, DNA is wrapped around proteins called histones. A nucleosome is a core of eight histone proteins (two each of four types) with DNA wound around it about twice (about 1.7 turns). A further histone, H1, sits where the DNA enters and leaves and holds it in place. A short stretch of linker DNA joins one nucleosome to the next, so DNA looks like a string of beads under the electron microscope.Learn

Histones are rich in positively charged amino acids, which attract the negatively charged phosphates of the DNA backbone. Nucleosomes let a very long DNA molecule be packed (supercoiled) into a small nucleus, and how tightly they pack controls which genes can be reached and expressed. Prokaryotes have no histones, so their DNA does not form nucleosomes (it is "naked" DNA).

Worked example: from results to conclusion. "Explain how the results show that DNA, not protein, is the genetic material of the phage."
  1. The genetic material is whatever enters the bacterium, because the bacterium then makes new viruses from those instructions.
  2. 35S labels only protein. It was found in the supernatant, with the empty coats, so protein did not enter the bacteria.
  3. 32P labels only DNA. It was found in the pellet, inside the bacteria, so DNA entered.
  4. Therefore DNA, not protein, carries the instructions to make new phages. (The experiment depended on new technology, radioactive isotopes, which let the two molecules be traced separately.)

A1.2.15 Chargaff's data

Before the structure of DNA was known, the tetranucleotide hypothesis said DNA was a simple repeating unit of the four nucleotides, so all four bases would be present in equal amounts (25% each) in every organism. A molecule like that would be far too simple to carry genetic information. Erwin Chargaff measured the bases in DNA from many species. Worked example: analyse the human row.
  1. Ratio A : T = 31.0 ÷ 31.5 = 0.98; ratio G : C = 19.1 ÷ 18.4 = 1.04. Both close to 1, so A ≈ T and G ≈ C.
  2. Purines A + G = 31.0 + 19.1 = 50.1%; pyrimidines T + C = 31.5 + 18.4 = 49.9%: about half each.
  3. But A (31.0%) is far from G (19.1%), and none of the four is near 25%. Yeast is similar (A 31.3, G 18.7), while Mycobacterium tuberculosis has the opposite pattern (A 15.1, G 34.9): the proportions differ between species.
  4. Conclusion: the amounts are not equal, so the tetranucleotide hypothesis is falsified. The pattern A ≈ T and G ≈ C (Chargaff's rules) was later explained by complementary base pairing in Watson and Crick's double helix.
Exam traps. (1) Hershey-Chase: 35S = protein = supernatant; 32P = DNA = pellet. Swapping them reverses the conclusion. (2) "A = T" does not mean "A = 25%": the A + T share varies between species. (3) A nucleosome has eight histones in its core, not four; H1 is outside the core. (4) The 5′ end has the phosphate, the 3′ end the free sugar -OH, and new nucleotides are only added at the 3′ end.

Ten questions, Paper 1A style (multiple choice)

Quick quiz

Decide on your answer before you click. Every question explains the trap after you answer. Write any you miss into the Error log. Questions 5, 9 and 10 are HL.

1. In liquid water, hydrogen bonds form between

A hydrogen bond is between molecules: δ+ H of one, δ− O of another. Inside one molecule the O-H bonds are covalent (option 1). H with H, or O with O, would bring like charges together, which repel (options 3 and 4).

2. Transpiration pulls water up the xylem of a 30 m tree as a continuous column. Which property of water mainly stops the column from breaking?

Hydrogen bonds between the water molecules hold the column together while it is under tension: cohesion. Adhesion is water sticking to the walls; it helps water rise but it is not what links one molecule to the next. Latent heat explains why transpiration cools the leaf, not the pull.

3. Which substance is transported in human blood mainly by being dissolved in the plasma?

Glucose is polar and forms hydrogen bonds with water, so it dissolves. Oxygen is non-polar and only slightly soluble: almost all of it travels bound to haemoglobin. Cholesterol and triglycerides are hydrophobic and travel inside lipoproteins.

4. A ringed seal loses body heat much faster in water at 2 °C than it would in air at 2 °C. Which property of water best explains this?

Water conducts heat away from the skin about 24 times faster than air. Viscosity and buoyancy affect movement and support, not heat. Latent heat of vaporisation matters for cooling by evaporation (sweating), not for a seal under water.

5. (HL) Which statement best explains why Earth has kept liquid water at its surface?

Retention needs gravity (to hold the atmosphere and water vapour) and the right temperature (inside the Goldilocks zone, not outside it). Asteroids are the hypothesis for where water came from, not for why it stays. Photosynthesis splits water; it does not create Earth's oceans.

6. Which components make up the backbone of one strand of DNA?

The backbone is sugar-phosphate-sugar-phosphate, linked by strong covalent bonds, and in DNA the sugar is deoxyribose. Ribose is the RNA sugar. Hydrogen bonds link the two strands through the bases; they are not part of the backbone.

7. In a sample of double-stranded DNA, 18% of the bases are guanine. What percentage of the bases are adenine?

G = C = 18%, so G + C = 36% and A + T = 64%. A = T = 64% ÷ 2 = 32%. 18% assumes A = G; 36% is G + C; 64% is A + T, forgetting to halve.

8. Which feature is found in RNA but not in DNA?

RNA has ribose and uracil; DNA has deoxyribose and thymine. Cytosine and phosphate are in both. Two antiparallel strands describe DNA, not RNA.

9. (HL) A new nucleotide is added to a growing DNA strand. Its phosphate group forms a covalent bond with which carbon of the sugar at the end of the strand?

New nucleotides join the free -OH on carbon 3′, so strands grow 5′ to 3′. Carbon 5′ is where each nucleotide's own phosphate sits; carbon 1′ carries the base; carbon 2′ has just H in deoxyribose.

10. (HL) In the Hershey-Chase experiment, after blending and centrifuging, most of the radioactivity in the pellet of bacteria came from

DNA, labelled with 32P, entered the bacteria and went down into the pellet. 35S labels protein, and the coats stayed in the supernatant. DNA contains no sulfur, and the protein coat has almost no phosphorus, so options 2 and 3 are impossible labels.
