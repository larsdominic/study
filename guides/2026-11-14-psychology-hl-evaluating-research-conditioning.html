What it means to evaluate a study

To evaluate a study is to weigh up its strengths and its limitations, then decide how far you can trust its conclusion. A study is only as good as the way it was done.

Three questions do most of the work:

  • Cause: did the thing the researcher changed really cause the result, or could something else explain it?
  • Measuring: was the behaviour measured in a way that is consistent and accurate?
  • Reach: would the result also hold for other people and in real life?

Today's first half gives each of these questions its proper name.

Monday's experiment words, in one example

A researcher wants to know if music helps memory. She changes one thing: music or silence. That is the IV (independent variable). She measures one thing: how many of 20 words people recall. That is the DV (dependent variable).

"Music" and "silence" are the two conditions. The people taking part are participants. If chance (a coin toss) decides who goes into which condition, that is random allocation. Anything else that could change the score, such as a noisy room for one group only, is a confounding variable.

Consistent is not the same as correct

Imagine throwing darts. If all your darts land close together, you are consistent. If they land on the bullseye, you are accurate.

You can be consistent but wrong: all darts close together in the top corner. In research, consistent is called reliable and accurate is called valid. A good measure needs both.

Reflexes: responses you do not choose

A reflex is something your body does automatically, without you deciding. Your mouth waters when you smell food. You blink when something flies at your eye. You jump at a sudden loud bang.

You cannot stop a reflex just by wanting to. Classical conditioning is about how a new signal can come to trigger one of these automatic responses.

Learning by linking things together

Your brain links things that keep happening together. If your phone's message sound has often been followed by news from a friend, you may feel a little lift just hearing it. The smell of a certain shampoo may remind you of a holiday.

Nobody taught you these links on purpose. They formed because two things happened together many times. Psychologists call this learning by association.

Consequences shape what we do

What happens after we do something changes how often we do it again. Praise for helping makes helping more likely. A fine for parking in the wrong place makes it less likely.

In operant conditioning, "positive" and "negative" are used like plus and minus in maths: positive means something is added, negative means something is taken away. They do not mean good and bad.

Research methods · guide p28, p55-56, glossary p67 · RM01 slides 60-70 and 124-127

Experimental designs, order effects and counterbalancing

Does each person do one condition, or all of them? That choice decides what can go wrong.

By the end of this section I can

The design says how participants are spread over the conditions. Your deck teaches three (slides 62-68); the guide lists their typical problems (participant variability, practice and order effects) for the IA (p55-56).

Independent samples

Who: different people in each condition, by random allocation

Good: no order effects; harder to guess the aim

Problem: participant variables: the groups may differ by chance

Repeated measures

Who: the same people do every condition

Good: each person is compared with themselves; fewer people needed

Problem: order effects; easier to guess the aim; needs two equally hard sets of materials

Matched pairs

Who: people are pre-tested, paired with someone similar, one of each pair per condition

Good: groups balanced on that trait; no order effects

Problem: pre-testing takes time; if one drops out, the pair is lost

Repeated measures design: "A research design in which the same group of participants is measured or tested more than once under different conditions. This design allows for the examination of changes within the same individuals." Learn word for wordGuide glossary, p67

Participant variables are differences between people (ability, mood, language level) that could affect the DV.

Order effects and counterbalancing

Order effects are changes in performance caused by the order of the conditions, not by the IV (slide 124): a practice effect (better the second time) or a fatigue or boredom effect (worse the second time). Counterbalancing controls them: half the participants do A then B, the other half B then A (slide 127). Practice or tiredness now affects both conditions equally. It balances order effects out; it does not stop them.

Worked example 1: choose a design. "Does chewing gum improve concentration?" You have 30 students. Understand and apply
  1. Operationalise: IV = chewing mint gum or not during a 5-minute spot-the-difference task; DV = differences found.
  2. People differ a lot at spotting details, so participant variables are a big risk.
  3. Choose repeated measures: each student is compared with themselves, and you get 30 scores per condition instead of 15.
  4. Doing the task twice gives practice, so use two equally hard pictures and counterbalancing: 15 do gum first, 15 do no gum first.
Why this step? Why does step 4 need two different pictures?

With the same picture, students would remember where the differences are, so the second score would rise because of memory, not gum. The two pictures must be equally hard, or picture difficulty becomes a confounding variable.

Finish it. "Does reading on paper or on a screen give better recall?" You have only 12 students and some are much better readers than others. Step 1 is done: IV = reading a 300-word article on paper or on a tablet; DV = correct answers out of 10. Write the design you would choose and one control, then open.
Answer

Repeated measures (each student compared with themselves, which matters with only 12 people of very different reading ability), using two articles of equal length and difficulty, and counterbalancing: 6 read on paper first, 6 on the tablet first. Matched pairs (pre-test reading, pair similar readers) is also a good answer.

Exam traps. (1) Repeated measures needs fewer participants. RM01 slide 63 says the opposite: a slip on the slide. Slide 66 ("one group might just have better memory skills") sits under repeated measures but describes independent samples. (2) Order effects only happen when the same people do more than one condition: never give them as a problem of independent samples.
Self-check: designs (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. 15 people learn one word list in the morning and another in the evening. Design? One problem?
  2. You need 20 scores per condition. How many people for independent samples, and for repeated measures?

Answers. 1: repeated measures; order effects (practice, or tiredness in the evening) and lists that may not be equally hard. 2: 2 × 20 = 40, and 20.

Research considerations · guide p27 and glossary p67-69 · RM01 slides 107-114 and 118

Reliability and validity

A bathroom scale that always shows the same weight is reliable. If that weight is your real weight, it is also valid (slide 109).

By the end of this section I can

Reliability: "The consistency of measurement tools or methods. A reliable measure or assessment should yield consistent results when administered repeatedly under similar conditions and with a similar population." Learn word for wordGuide glossary, p67
  • Test-retest reliability (class word): the same people do the same test twice; similar scores mean it is reliable.
  • Inter-rater reliability (guide p21): two or more people score the same thing separately; if they agree, the scoring is reliable. One marker alone gives no check.

Validity asks: are we really measuring, or showing, what we claim? The guide names five kinds (p27):

KindThe question it asks
InternalDid the IV really cause the change in the DV? (slide 114)
ExternalCan the results be generalised to other people and settings?
ConstructDoes the measure capture the idea (the construct) it is meant to? Owning an iPhone is a poor measure of "pro-American attitudes" (slide 118).
ContentDoes it cover all parts of the idea? An "anxiety" scale that only asks about sleep does not.
FaceAt first sight, does it look like it measures the right thing?

Reliable but not valid: a scale that is always 2 kg too heavy is consistent but wrong. A valid measure must also be reliable (slide 113).

Extra, not needed today: two notes on the wording

The guide glossary (p69) words internal validity as "the extent that a measurement conducted within a study accurately measures or assesses what it claims to measure". Your deck and the guide's concept causality (p21, "internal vs external validity") use the cause-and-effect meaning; use that one when you evaluate an experiment. Slides 111-112 also mention internal consistency, Cronbach's alpha and criterion validity: not in the guide's list, not needed for the IB exam.

Worked example 2: judge a measure. One observer counts how many times each child pushes another during a 15-minute break, as a measure of aggression. Understand and apply
  1. Reliability: one observer, so nobody checks the counts. Fix: two observers count separately with the same checklist and compare (inter-rater reliability).
  2. Construct validity: pushing can be part of a friendly game, and aggression can be words, so the count may miss or wrongly include the construct.
  3. Fix: a checklist agreed in advance ("hits, kicks or pushes with angry words or face; insults").
Why this step? Why are steps 1 and 2 about different problems?

Step 1 asks whether the count is consistent (reliability); step 2 asks whether it counts the right thing (validity). Two observers could agree perfectly and still both count friendly pushing.

Finish it. A school measures "stress" with one question: "How many hours did you sleep last night?" Step 1 is done: reliability: sleep changes from night to night, so asking again next week may give a different answer (low test-retest reliability). Write step 2 (validity) and one fix, then open.
Answer

Construct and content validity are low: sleep is only one possible sign of stress (it can also be short because of a late film), and one question misses worry, mood and body signs. Fix: a short questionnaire with several items on worry, mood, sleep and body tension, checked against how students describe their own stress.

Exam traps. (1) Reliable = consistent, valid = accurate. (2) "Valid" alone is vague: say which kind. (3) A bigger sample does not make a questionnaire more reliable or valid.
Self-check: reliability and validity (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. Two teachers mark the same 10 essays; their marks differ by up to 8 out of 20. Which kind of reliability is low?
  2. A stopwatch is always started half a second late. Reliable? Valid?

Answers. 1: inter-rater reliability. 2: reliable (the same error every time) but not valid (every time is wrong).

Research considerations · guide p21, p27-28, p56, glossary · RM01 slides 115-129

Threats to internal validity: participant and researcher bias

Anything except the IV that could explain the result is a threat to internal validity.

By the end of this section I can

A confounding variable could explain the result instead of the IV (Monday's experiments section). Look for it in the materials (one word list is easier), the measure (low construct validity) and the people themselves:

Participant bias

Participant bias: "Occurs when participants in a study alter their behaviour or responses due to their awareness of being observed or their expectations about the study's purpose." Learn word for wordGuide glossary, p66

Demand characteristics are clues that tell participants what the researcher wants (guide p56 lists "demand characteristics/social desirability bias"). Your deck names four kinds (class words, slides 119-122):

  • Expectancy effect: acting the way they think the researcher wants.
  • Screw-you effect: working out the aim and doing the opposite (rare).
  • Social desirability effect: giving the answer that looks good, not the true one.
  • Reactivity: acting differently just because they are watched.

Reduce it (slide 123): do not reveal the exact aim (mild deception, then a full debriefing); independent samples; a single-blind design (participants do not know their condition, for example a placebo drink). Deception must not cause harm.

Researcher bias

Researcher bias: "When a researcher's personal values or expectations influence the design, data collection or interpretation of findings, potentially leading to biased results." Learn word for wordGuide glossary, p67
  • Confirmation bias: "The tendency to seek, interpret and remember information that confirms pre-existing beliefs while ignoring or downplaying contradictory evidence" (glossary p63).
  • Funding bias (class word): whoever pays may influence the result.
  • Publication bias (glossary p66): studies with positive, significant results are more likely to be published.

Reduce it (slide 129): a double-blind design; random allocation; several researchers score the same data and compare (researcher triangulation); peer review before publication.

Double-blind design: "When neither the participants nor the researchers conducting the study are aware who is in the control group and who is in the experimental group. This is done to minimize bias and increase the reliability of results." Learn word for wordGuide glossary, p64

Worked example 3: spot the biases. A drinks company pays for a study of its energy drink. Participants are told it "boosts focus", then do a reaction-time test. The person timing them knows who had the drink. Drinkers react faster. Understand and apply
  1. Participant bias: "boosts focus" is a demand characteristic; drinkers may try harder (expectancy effect).
  2. Researcher bias: the timer expects faster times (confirmation bias); the company paid (funding bias).
  3. Either could explain the faster times instead of the drink, so internal validity is low.
  4. Fixes: a placebo drink that looks and tastes the same; neither participants nor timer know who had which (double-blind); a computer records the times.
Why this step? Why does step 3 matter for the marks?

Naming a bias earns little. The marks come from explaining how it could change the results in this study, and so why we cannot be sure the IV caused them.

Finish it. A therapist tests her own new therapy. She interviews each client afterwards and rates how much better they are, knowing who had her therapy. Clients were told the therapy "works for most people". Step 1 is done: participant bias: clients told it "works" may say they feel better to please her (expectancy effect). Write step 2 (researcher bias) and one fix for each, then open.
Answer

Researcher bias: she believes in her therapy and knows who had it, so she may rate those clients as more improved (confirmation bias). Fixes: clients are not told what result is expected; a second interviewer who does not know who had which treatment does the ratings (double-blind), ideally with a standard questionnaire.

Exam traps. (1) Demand characteristics are about the participants, not the researcher. (2) Single-blind hides the condition from participants only; double-blind also from the researchers collecting the data.
Self-check: biases (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. In an interview people say they always recycle, but their bins show otherwise. Which bias?
  2. Why does an independent samples design reduce demand characteristics?

Answers. 1: social desirability effect (participant bias). 2: each person sees only one condition, so the aim is harder to work out.

Research considerations · guide p27-28 and glossary · RM01 slides 130-133

External validity: can we generalise?

A result is only useful outside the study if it holds for other people and other places.

By the end of this section I can

Generalizability: "The extent to which research findings or conclusions drawn from a study can be applied to a different population or other settings. It demonstrates the external validity of research." Learn word for wordGuide glossary, p65
  • Population validity (class word): is the sample representative? Only volunteers, one school or one country may not stand for everyone (Monday's sampling section).
  • Ecological validity (class word): are the setting and task like real life? Learning nonsense words in a silent lab is not.

The trade-off: more control raises internal validity but often lowers ecological validity.

The guide lists three more words here (p27-28), used mainly for qualitative research such as interviews (later in the course): transferability, how far findings apply to other settings (the qualitative word for generalising); credibility, how far the study gives a true picture of what participants think and feel; reflexivity, the researcher's awareness of how their own views and biases may shape the study.

Worked example 4: evaluate generalisation. 30 psychology students at one American university learn 20 unrelated words in a silent lab. The researcher concludes that "background noise harms everyone's memory". Understand and apply
  1. Population validity: one university, subject, age group and country; children or older adults may react differently.
  2. Ecological validity: unrelated words in a silent lab are not like revising real notes at home.
  3. So "everyone" goes too far. Improve: a stratified sample of ages from several schools, and a real study task (read a text, answer questions) in a normal room.
Why this step? Why a stratified sample in step 3?

It includes each group (age, school) in the same proportions as the population, so the sample looks like the people the conclusion is about.

Finish it. A study of phone use and sleep asks 50 volunteers from one gym in Singapore to wear a sleep tracker at home for a week. Step 1 is done: ecological validity is fairly high, because they sleep in their own beds with their own phones. Write step 2 (population validity), then open.
Answer

Population validity is low: volunteers from one gym in one city are probably fitter and more health-minded than most people, and may differ in age and income, so the results may not generalise to teenagers or people who do not exercise.

Exam traps. (1) Population validity is about who; ecological validity about where and how. (2) A small sample is not the same as a biased one.
Self-check: generalising (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. A stress study uses only male office workers in Tokyo. Main problem?
  2. Which guide word is the qualitative version of generalisability?

Answers. 1: population validity. 2: transferability.

RM04: model answers for questions 1-4

Write your own answers first, then open each box and tick the points you made. These answers are ours, written to the IB command terms: your teacher may also reward other good points. RM04 also uses "identify", "name" and "justify", which are not on the Psychology command-term list (guide p60-61): treat "identify" and "name" like state (a short answer), and "justify" as "give a reason, with evidence from the study".

Question 1 [4]: counterbalancing and internal validity

Counterbalancing is a way to control order effects in a repeated measures design: half of the participants do condition A first and then B, the other half do B first and then A, so any effect of order is spread evenly over both conditions.

In Ms Patel's study all 24 students did Condition A (no breathing) on Monday and Condition B (breathing) on Friday, so Condition B always came second. Students may have done better in the second mock because of a practice effect: they had already sat one mock that week and knew the format. They may also have felt less anxious simply because the second mock felt familiar. So the improvement could be caused by the order rather than by the breathing exercise. This lowers internal validity: we cannot be confident that the IV (breathing) caused the change in the DV (anxiety and exam score).

Checklist (1 mark each): counterbalancing defined (two orders, half and half) · states that every student did A before B here · names an order effect and links it to Condition B · concludes the cause may be the order, so internal validity is lower.

Question 2 [4]: one other confounding variable

One confounding variable is the exam paper: Condition A used Mock Exam 1 and Condition B used Mock Exam 2. If Mock Exam 2 was easier, students would score higher in Condition B whether or not they did the breathing exercise.

Justification: the two papers are different materials, and nothing in the study says they were checked to be equally hard. The difference in difficulty changes together with the IV, so it is a second possible cause of the 6-mark rise (6 out of 50 is 12%). Ms Patel cannot tell whether the breathing or the easier paper caused it, which lowers internal validity.

Checklist: one confounding variable that is not order (the different papers; or the day, since Friday came after four more days of revision; or Ms Patel leading the exercise herself) · how it differs between the conditions · how it could raise the Condition B scores · link to not being able to say the breathing caused the result.

Question 3 [4]: demand characteristic

The most likely demand characteristic is the expectancy effect (participant bias): participants act the way they think the researcher wants.

Ms Patel told the class she was testing whether breathing exercises "reduce your exam stress and help you perform better", and she is their own teacher, so students knew the expected result. Before Mock Exam 2 they may have rated their anxiety lower on her questionnaire to please her, and tried harder in the second exam to "help" the study. So the lower anxiety and higher scores in Condition B may come from students' expectations, not from the breathing exercise itself.

Checklist: names the expectancy effect (social desirability effect also accepted if explained) · telling the aim, and her being their teacher, gave the clue · effect on the self-report anxiety scores · effect on exam effort, so the result may not be caused by the IV.

Question 4 [4]: researcher bias and one fix

A researcher bias here is confirmation bias: Ms Patel expects the breathing exercise to work, marks both exams herself with her own rubric, knows which script belongs to which condition, and nobody checks her marking. She may, without meaning to, give borderline answers in Condition B the benefit of the doubt and mark Condition A more strictly.

Change: blind marking with a second marker. Another teacher removes names and dates and gives each script a code, so the marker does not know whether it is from Monday or Friday. A second teacher who does not know the hypothesis also marks every script with the same rubric, and the two sets of marks are compared (inter-rater reliability).

Checklist: names a researcher bias (confirmation bias, or researcher expectations) · uses the details of the study (she knows the condition, own rubric, no cross-check) · one specific change (scripts coded, or an independent second marker) · says how it reduces the bias.

The cognitive approach, and why conditioning sits in it

The cognitive approach explains behaviour through what happens in the mind between what we sense and what we do.

By the end of this section I can

The guide (p24-25) sees "the mind acting as an information processor that bridges the gap between a stimulus input and the behavioural output." Your class slides (LC01) give four assumptions (class content, not a guide line):

  1. Humans are information processors: input (your phone's "ding") → processing (you recognise a WhatsApp message) → output (you pick up the phone).
  2. Mental processes can be studied scientifically, for example with an experiment on noise and memory.
  3. Mental representations guide behaviour: what you believe and expect shapes what you do.
  4. Soft determinism: we still have some free will, so changing how we think can change what we do.

The cognitive approach "emerged as a reaction to behaviourism", which introduced conditioning (p24), and the guide still lists conditioning under it (Table 3.2, p25): "Conditioning is a simpler form of learning that can operate on the level of overt behaviours without requiring participation of cognitive processes" (p33).

Classical conditioning

A signal that meant nothing comes to trigger an automatic response, because it keeps arriving just before something that already triggers it.

By the end of this section I can

Classical conditioning: "The process of classical conditioning and its role in behaviour." Learn word for wordGuide, Table 3.2, p25. Also in learning and cognition (p33): "Examples of classical and operant conditioning as a way of learning."

Classical conditioning is learning by association: a neutral stimulus is paired again and again with a stimulus that already causes a reflex, until it alone causes a similar response (LC02). The response is involuntary.

The five terms (Pavlov's dogs)

Pavlov, studying digestion, noticed dogs salivated before the food arrived. He paired a sound (a metronome, not a bell, LC04) with food.

TermMeaning (LC02)Pavlov
UCS unconditioned stimuluscauses a natural, unlearned responsefood
UCR unconditioned responsethe natural response to the UCSsalivating at the food
NS neutral stimuluscauses no natural response before conditioningmetronome, at first
CS conditioned stimulusthe old NS, after repeated pairing with the UCSmetronome, after pairing
CR conditioned responsethe learned response to the CSsalivating at the metronome alone

Extinction: if the CS keeps coming without the UCS, the CR fades and stops (the metronome without food). Spontaneous recovery: after a rest, the CR can suddenly come back, usually weaker, which shows the learning was not fully wiped out.

Its role in behaviour

  • Taste aversion (LC02): Garcia (1955) gave rats sweet saccharin water during radiation, which made them sick. They then avoided saccharin for over a month, after a single pairing; LC03 adds that it works even when the sickness comes much later.
  • Aversion therapy (LC02): a drug that causes vomiting (UCS) is given with alcohol (NS); alcohol becomes a CS for nausea (CR), so the patient avoids it. Its misuse as "conversion therapy" is a responsibility (ethics) example.
  • Emotions and fears (LC04): a strict teacher's tone can come to make students anxious; adverts pair a product with happy music.
Worked example 5: label a new example. A toddler is at a party. A balloon pops loudly next to her (she startles and cries) just as a clown walks in. This happens three times. Now she cries whenever she sees a clown. Understand and apply
  1. What causes a response without learning? The loud pop: UCS. Startle and fear: UCR.
  2. What meant nothing at first? The clown: NS.
  3. Pairing: clown + pop, three times.
  4. After: the clown alone is the CS, and the fear at the clown is the CR.
  5. Role in behaviour: classical conditioning can explain how a fear of something harmless is learned.
Why this step? Why is the fear a UCR in step 1 but a CR in step 4?

The behaviour is the same (fear), but what triggers it is different. Fear at the loud pop is natural, so it is the UCR. Fear at the clown was learned, so it is the CR. Always name the trigger.

Operant conditioning

Behaviour that is followed by a good result is repeated; behaviour followed by a bad result is not.

By the end of this section I can

Operant conditioning: "The process of operant conditioning and its role in behaviour." Learn word for wordGuide, Table 3.2, p25. Learning and cognition (p33) adds: "Application of operant conditioning to change behaviour."

Thorndike's law of effect: behaviours followed by pleasant consequences are more likely to be repeated; those followed by unpleasant consequences less likely (LC06). Skinner built operant conditioning on it (rats pressing a lever for food). The behaviour is voluntary and learned from its consequences; classical conditioning links two stimuli that come before an involuntary response.

Four kinds of consequence

  • Positive reinforcement: a sticker for finished homework.
  • Negative reinforcement: finishing homework so that parents stop nagging (the nagging is removed).
  • Positive punishment: extra chores for breaking curfew.
  • Negative punishment: losing video-game time for not doing chores.

Problems with punishment (LC06): it may only hide the behaviour; it can cause fear or anger; it does not teach what to do instead.

Schedules of reinforcement

How often the reward comes matters: a reward every time is learned fast but stops fast once rewards end (extinction); an unpredictable reward is the hardest to stop, which is why gambling is hard to give up.

Applying it to change behaviour

Dallery et al. (2017) (LC07): smokers were paid only when an online breath test showed they had not smoked (positive reinforcement); a control group was paid just for sending samples. After 4 weeks, 39.6% versus 13% had stopped, about three times as many; by 3 and 6 months the difference was gone. The idea, not the numbers, is what you need.

Worked example 6: plan a behaviour change. Parents want their 7-year-old to brush his teeth every evening without being asked. Understand and apply
  1. Target behaviour, stated so you can count it: brushing for 2 minutes before 8 pm without a reminder.
  2. Choose a consequence: positive reinforcement, a sticker on a chart straight after brushing; 7 stickers earn a trip to the park.
  3. Explain why it should work: by the law of effect, a pleasant consequence that follows the behaviour quickly makes it more likely to be repeated.
  4. Plan for later: once brushing is a habit, give stickers only some evenings (an unpredictable schedule), so the habit does not stop as soon as the stickers do.
Why this step? Why does step 2 give the sticker straight after brushing?

The child must connect the consequence with the behaviour. A reward that comes much later may reinforce whatever he happened to be doing then instead.

8. Paper 1 Section A style. Describe operant conditioning with reference to one example of human behaviour. [4]

Show model answer

Operant conditioning is learning from the consequences of voluntary behaviour. It is based on Thorndike's law of effect: behaviour followed by a pleasant consequence is more likely to be repeated, and behaviour followed by an unpleasant consequence is less likely. Reinforcement increases a behaviour; punishment decreases it. "Positive" means something is added and "negative" means something is taken away.

Example: a student who gets praise and a house point each time she hands in homework on time (positive reinforcement) is more likely to hand it in on time again. If she instead loses her break when it is late (negative punishment), late homework becomes less likely. The consequence comes straight after the behaviour, so she links the two.

Markband (guide p45): 3-4 marks = detailed, accurate knowledge (law of effect, reinforcement vs punishment, positive vs negative) and an example that is relevant and explained. 1-2 marks = limited knowledge, or an example that is named but not explained.

9. Paper 2 Section A style. Explain the concept of causality in relation to your class's Skittles experiment (buzzer every 2 minutes, then a Skittle, salivation measured on a tissue). [4]

Show model answer

Causality means showing that one variable causes a change in another. In the Skittles experiment the claim is that the buzzer (now a CS) caused salivation (CR) because it had been paired with the Skittle. To be sure, the buzzer would have to be tested alone, with no Skittle, and salivation measured then.

Other explanations weaken this claim. The buzzer came every 2 minutes, so students could have started salivating because they expected a sweet at regular times, not because of the buzzer. Hunger and the sight of the bag of Skittles could also cause salivation. There was no control group (for example students who got Skittles at random times with no buzzer), so we cannot rule these out. Sounding the buzzer at unpredictable times and adding a control group would make the causal claim stronger.

Markband (guide p47, Paper 2 Section A question 2): 3-4 marks = the concept is well developed and the links to the study are clear and detailed. 1-2 marks = the concept is relevant but limited, with only some links.

10. Research methods. Explain how researcher bias could affect the results of an experiment, and one way to reduce it. Use an example. [4]

Show model answer

Researcher bias is when a researcher's personal values or expectations influence the design, data collection or interpretation of findings. For example, a psychologist who believes a new reading app helps children, and who scores the children's reading tests herself knowing who used the app, may give app users the benefit of the doubt on unclear answers (confirmation bias). The app group would then score higher because of her expectations, not the app, which lowers internal validity.

To reduce it, a double-blind design can be used: the tests are scored by someone who does not know which children used the app, so their expectations cannot affect the scores.

Checklist: accurate definition (1) · how it changes the results in this study (1) · link to internal validity or causality (1) · one specific way to reduce it, explained (1).
