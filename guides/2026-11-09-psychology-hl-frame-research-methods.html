Before you start (about 8 min)

This page builds on the ideas below. Read them first so you do not have to stop later. Words with a dotted underline anywhere on the page: tap them for a short explanation.

What psychology is: a science about people

Psychology tries to answer questions about why people act, think and feel as they do. Why do we forget names? Why do teenagers copy their friends? Why do some people feel anxious before tests?

It is a science: a psychologist does not just give an opinion, but collects evidence from real people and checks whether an idea fits that evidence. Other people must be able to repeat the check.

Psychology is not mind reading, and it is not only about therapy. Most of it is careful studies: giving people a task, measuring what they do, and comparing groups.

How a scientific study works

Almost every study follows the same steps:

  • Question: does exercise make people happier?
  • Prediction: people who exercise for a week will rate their mood higher than people who do not.
  • Collect information: ask both groups to rate their mood from 1 to 10.
  • Compare: is the exercise group higher?
  • Conclusion: does the evidence support the prediction or not?

One study is never the final word. If other researchers repeat it and get the same result, we trust it more.

Cause, or just a link?

On days when more ice cream is sold, more people get sunburnt. Does ice cream cause sunburn? No: hot sunny weather causes both. The two things are linked, but one does not cause the other.

Much of today is about this problem. To show that X causes Y, a researcher changes X on purpose, keeps everything else the same, and checks whether Y changes. If something else also changed (like the weather), you cannot tell which one caused the result.

Averages, and comparing two groups

The mean is the usual average: add up all the scores and divide by how many there are. Mood scores 6, 7 and 8 have a mean of 21 ÷ 3 = 7.

Studies often compare the mean of two groups: exercise group 7, no-exercise group 4.2. A bigger mean in one group looks like an effect, but with only a few people a difference can happen by luck.

Psychologists use statistics (maths for data) to decide whether a difference is big enough not to be luck. You will learn the details later in HL data analysis; today you only need the idea.

Population and sample: tasting the soup

To check whether a big pot of soup needs salt, you do not eat the whole pot. You stir it and taste one spoonful.

  • The pot is the population: everyone you want to know about.
  • The spoonful is the sample: the people you actually study.
  • Stirring matters: an unstirred spoonful from the top might taste nothing like the rest. A sample that is not like the population gives a wrong answer about the population.

Today's sampling section is about different ways of choosing the spoonful, and what can go wrong with each.

What "random" means in science

In everyday talk, random means odd or unplanned ("a random question"). In science it means something exact: decided by chance, so every option has the same chance.

Tossing a coin is random: heads and tails each have a 1 in 2 chance. A computer picking 60 names from a list of 600 is random: every name has the same 1 in 10 chance.

Walking up to whoever you happen to see is not random in this sense, because people who are not there have no chance at all. Keep this in mind for random sampling and random allocation.

How IB exams are marked

IB Psychology papers have no multiple choice. You write answers, and examiners mark them with markbands: descriptions of what an answer at each level looks like (for example 1-2 marks: limited knowledge; 3-4 marks: detailed knowledge, example explained).

The first word of each question is a command term (describe, explain, evaluate...). It tells you what kind of answer reaches the top band. Answering "describe" when the question says "explain" keeps you in a lower band, even if everything you wrote is true.

Your final grade is from 1 to 7.

Still stuck on a word? Do not search for long. Write it in the Error log tab of the study sheet starting with "word:" (for example "word: composition") and carry on. It will be explained in the next guide.

What psychology studies

Your class deck starts with one sentence (RM01 slide 1): psychology is "the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes" Learn word for word.

  • Behaviour is what a person does that someone else can see or record: speaking, smiling, how fast you press a button, your heart rate.
  • Mental processes happen inside the head and cannot be seen: paying attention, remembering, thinking, deciding. Psychologists work them out from behaviour (how many words you recall) or from what people say about themselves.
  • Scientific means ideas are tested with evidence that others can check, not with opinion.

Worked example 3: one topic, three command terms. Watch how the same topic, operationalisation (explained in the variables section), needs a bigger answer as the command term climbs.
  1. Describe operationalisation. What it is plus detail: "Operationalisation means stating exactly how a variable will be changed or measured. For example, memory can be operationalised as the number of words out of 20 that a participant writes down in two minutes."
  2. Explain why researchers operationalise variables. Add reasons: "...because a vague idea like memory cannot be counted fairly. A precise measure means every participant is scored the same way, and other researchers can repeat the study exactly and check whether they get the same result."
  3. Evaluate operationalisation. Strengths and limitations, then a judgement: "A strength is precision, which allows the study to be repeated. A limitation is that recalling a word list is narrower than everyday memory, so the measure may not capture the whole idea. Overall it is necessary, but the chosen measure should be judged for how well it matches the idea being studied."
Exam traps. (1) An answer that only describes when the question says explain stays in the lower markband: explain needs reasons or causes. (2) Paper 1 Section A (4 marks): the top band needs "detailed knowledge" and an example that "is relevant and explained"; an example that is relevant but not explained stays in the 1-2 band (p45). Naming a study is not enough: say how the example shows the idea. (3) The same page says "Knowledge of details of research studies will not be assessed" (p45), and the example "may be a research study or a real or hypothetical situation". So dates and sample sizes earn nothing in Section A; a clear explanation does. (4) In the specimen markscheme only the first example you give is credited, so choose one and develop it.

The six concepts

Paper 1 Section C and Paper 2 Section B name concepts in the question, so you must know what each one means. Each concept also has a list of "related terms" in the guide, but these "will not be used to set concept-based questions" (p20). The questions use the six concept names.

The opening statements Learn word for word:

Bias (p20)

"A bias is a limitation in objective thinking; it is a tendency to perceive information through a cognitive filter of experience and preference."

In plain words: Nobody sees things perfectly neutrally: what we expect and like colours what we notice. In research, the researcher, the participants or the sample can tilt the results.

Today: sampling bias

Causality (p21)

"Psychologists investigate the relationship between variables with the goal of determining cause-and-effect relationships."

In plain words: Does X really cause Y, or is something else going on?

Today: true experiments, random allocation, confounding variables

Change (p21)

"A key focus of psychological research is how to apply theory and psychological findings with the goal of changing individual behaviours."

In plain words: Using psychology to help people change, for example to stop smoking or overcome a fear.

Today: later, with conditioning and social learning

Measurement (p22)

"A fundamental challenge for psychologists is that human behaviour is difficult to observe and objectively measure."

In plain words: Much of what psychologists study is inside the head, so it is hard to see or count fairly.

Today: constructs, operationalisation, quantitative and qualitative data

Perspective (p22-23)

"No single perspective explains behaviour sufficiently on its own; each provides a means by which to interpret and examine behaviour."

In plain words: Each approach is one lens. You need more than one to see the whole picture.

Today: the three approaches

Responsibility (p23)

"Psychologists rely on humans as participants in their research; the relationship between researchers and participants is based on the premise of treating each other with respect."

In plain words: Researchers must treat the people they study fairly: ask for consent, protect them from harm, let them leave.

Today: later, with ethics

Memory hook for the six: Bias, Causality, Change, Measurement, Perspective, Responsibility, in alphabetical order, as the guide lists them.

Your class concept sheets

Your teacher's booklet has a page set for each concept; only two are in your folder. Each has a "Questions to ask of any study" box that turns a concept into a checklist:

  • GLO - Concept Measurement: how was the variable operationalised? Is the data direct or indirect, quantitative or qualitative? Is the measure reliable and valid? Would another method give a fuller picture?

Worked example 4: two concepts on one study. RM01 slides 4-8: one group exercises 30 minutes a day for a week, a control group does no exercise, and both rate their mood from 1 to 10. The exercise group's average goes from 4 to 7; the control group ends at 4.2.
  1. Measurement. Mood, which cannot be seen, was turned into a number: a self-rating from 1 to 10. That makes groups easy to compare, but people may rate themselves higher because they guess what the researcher hopes to see.
  2. Causality. There is a control group, which helps: without exercise, mood barely changed. But the slides point out that if the exercise group also slept better, sleep could explain the result. Then we cannot say exercise caused the better mood.
  3. So one study, two concepts, two different questions. This is the kind of thinking Paper 2 Section B rewards.
Exam traps. (1) "Perspective" in IB Psychology is not your personal opinion: it means a way of explaining behaviour (biological, cognitive, sociocultural, or a cultural viewpoint). (2) "Bias" in the guide is about thinking and research, not only about being unfair to a group. (3) Learn the six names in the guide's form: "measurement", not "measuring"; "causality", not "cause".

Today is about quantitative research, and mainly the experiment. Interviews and observations, which are often qualitative, come later.

Variables and constructs

A variable is, in the guide's words, "Any factor or characteristic that can vary and is subject to measurement or manipulation in research." Reaction time, number of words recalled and hours of sleep are variables.

A construct is "An abstract idea, concept or variable that cannot be directly observed but is used to explain or measure aspects of human behaviour. Examples include intelligence and self-esteem." (guide glossary). Stress, happiness and anxiety are constructs too: you cannot say "she has 6 units of stress" (RM01 slide 12).

Operationalisation Learn word for word: "Stating exactly how a variable will be manipulated or measured in experimental research." (guide glossary). Once a construct has been operationalised, it is a variable you can measure.

The independent and dependent variable

An experiment asks "If I change X, does Y change?" (RM01 slide 18).

  • The independent variable (IV) is the thing the researcher changes on purpose. It always has at least two conditions, for example exercise and no exercise.
  • The dependent variable (DV) is the thing the researcher measures, to see whether it changed. It "depends" on the IV.
Worked example 5: operationalise a construct. A researcher wants to study stress. Understand and apply
  1. Is it a construct? Yes: you cannot see stress directly.
  2. Find something you can measure that goes up and down with it. Option A, the body: heart rate in beats per minute, measured with a finger clip while the participant sits still for one minute. Option B, self-report: the total score on a 10-question stress questionnaire, each question scored 1 to 5, so totals run from 10 to 50.
  3. Check each one: could another researcher do exactly the same thing? Yes, the device, the timing and the scoring are all stated.
  4. Link to the concept of measurement: heart rate is objective but also rises with exercise or coffee; the questionnaire asks the person directly but depends on honest answers. Neither is perfect, which is exactly the guide's point about measurement.
Worked example 6: identify and operationalise the IV and the DV. Research question: "Does testing yourself with flashcards help you remember vocabulary better than rereading?" Understand and apply
  1. What does the researcher change? The way of revising. That is the IV.
  2. Operationalise the IV by stating both conditions exactly. Condition A: 10 minutes with 20 flashcards (Spanish word on the front, English on the back), saying the answer before turning each card. Condition B: 10 minutes rereading the same 20 Spanish and English word pairs on a printed list.
  3. What is measured? How much is remembered. That is the DV.
  4. Operationalise the DV: the number of the 20 Spanish words correctly translated into English in a written test 24 hours later (a score from 0 to 20).
  5. Check: "memory" or "how well they revise" would be too vague. With the wording above, anyone could run the study again.
Exam traps. (1) "The DV is memory" is not operationalised; "the number of words out of 20 recalled in 2 minutes" is. (2) Give the IV as its conditions ("flashcards or rereading"), not as one of them ("flashcards"). (3) The IV is changed by the researcher; the DV is never changed on purpose, only measured.
Self-check: variables (answer in your head, then open)
  1. A researcher shows half the participants a sad film clip and half a neutral one, then counts how many biscuits each person eats in 10 minutes. Name the IV and the DV.
  2. Is self-esteem a variable or a construct?
  3. Interview transcripts about how teenagers feel on exam results day: quantitative or qualitative data?
  4. Give one operationalised DV for "anxiety before a test".

Answers. 1: IV: type of film clip (sad or neutral); DV: number of biscuits eaten in 10 minutes. 2: a construct; it becomes a variable once it is operationalised, for example as a score on a self-esteem questionnaire. 3: qualitative (words, not numbers). 4: for example, self-rated anxiety on a scale from 1 to 10 just before the test, or heart rate in beats per minute one minute before the test.

True experiment and quasi-experiment

The guide lists one research method as "experiments (true or quasi-)" (p28). Its glossary defines both:

True experiment: "A controlled experiment where researchers manipulate an independent variable to observe its impact on a dependent variable, using random allocation of participants to conditions to ensure causation." Learn word for wordGuide glossary, quoted in RM02
Quasi-experiment: "A research method that resembles a true experiment but lacks random assignment of participants to different groups. In a true experiment, researchers randomly assign participants to different conditions. In a quasi-experiment, researchers often have limited control over participant assignment, meaning it is unable to establish a cause-and-effect relationship." Learn word for wordGuide glossary, quoted in RM02

Random allocation (also called random assignment) means chance decides which condition each participant goes into, for example by tossing a coin or drawing names. It spreads differences between people, such as ability or motivation, evenly across the conditions, so they cannot explain a difference in the DV. In a quasi-experiment the groups exist already (age, gender, culture, a diagnosis), so the researcher cannot use chance to place people.

True experiment

IV changed by the researcher: yes

Random allocation: yes

Setting: usually controlled, often a laboratory

Cause and effect: strongest evidence

Example: caffeine or decaf, chosen by chance, then an alertness test (RM01 slide 48)

Field experiment (class word)

IV changed by the researcher: yes

Random allocation: usually yes

Setting: real life

Cause and effect: good, but less control

Example: Piliavin et al. (1969): helping on a real New York subway train (GLO Measurement)

Quasi-experiment

IV changed by the researcher: no: the groups exist already

Random allocation: no

Setting: any

Cause and effect: weaker; the guide says it cannot establish cause and effect

Example: Maguire et al. (2000): London taxi drivers and non-drivers, brain scans compared (RM02)

Natural experiment (class word)

IV changed by the researcher: no: a real event changes it

Random allocation: no

Setting: real life

Cause and effect: weaker

Example: a smoking ban: health measured before and after (RM01 slides 92-94)

Field and natural experiments are not in the guide's list or glossary. Your class deck (slide 92) says a natural experiment is a kind of quasi-experiment and "may simply be referred to as a quasi-experiment on exam day". A field experiment is a true experiment that happens in real life.

Controls and confounding variables

Extraneous variable: "Variables other than the independent variable in an experiment that can potentially influence the dependent variable. If not controlled, they can become confounding variables." Learn word for wordGuide glossary. Your worksheet RM02 calls them "external (also called extraneous or confounding) variables"; the guide's learning objective says "external variables".
  • Extraneous variable: anything apart from the IV that could affect the DV, for example noise, time of day, tiredness, the words used in the test.
  • Control variable: an extraneous variable that the researcher keeps the same in every condition (RM01 slide 20).
  • Confounding variable: an extraneous variable that was not controlled and differs between the conditions. Now there are two possible causes of the result, and you cannot tell which one it was (RM01 slide 19).

Three main ways to control them: standardisation (the same instructions, materials, room and timing for everyone), random allocation (spreads differences between people evenly) and a control group or control condition to compare against. This is why the guide links external variables to causality: only when they are controlled can you say the IV caused the change in the DV.

Worked example 7: spot and fix a confounding variable. In the flashcard study from worked example 6, the flashcard group is tested at 9 am, and the rereading group at 3 pm, straight after a PE lesson. The flashcard group scores higher. Understand and apply
  1. List what differs between the groups apart from the IV: time of day and tiredness after sport.
  2. Could that affect the DV (words recalled)? Yes: tired students may recall less.
  3. So time of day is a confounding variable: the higher score may be caused by the flashcards or by being fresh in the morning.
  4. Fix: test both groups at the same time of day in the same room, read the same instructions from a script (standardisation), and put students into groups by drawing names (random allocation).
Worked example 8: name the type of experiment. Ask two questions in order: did the researcher change the IV? Did chance decide who went into which condition? Understand and apply
  1. 60 students are put by coin toss into "revise in silence" or "revise with recorded café noise", in the same classroom, then take the same test. Researcher changed the IV, chance decided the groups: true experiment.
  2. The memory scores of 15-year-olds who speak two languages are compared with those who speak one. Nobody can be given a language by chance: quasi-experiment.
  3. A city moves school start time from 7:30 to 8:30, and researchers compare students' sleep diaries from the term before and the term after. The change came from the city, not the researcher: natural experiment (you may call it a quasi-experiment).
  4. An actor drops a pile of books in a real school corridor, wearing school uniform on some days and casual clothes on others (decided by coin toss), and the researcher counts how many passers-by help. Researcher changed the IV, in a real setting: field experiment.

Exam traps. (1) The difference between a true and a quasi-experiment is who decides group membership, not where the study happens: a true experiment can happen outside a lab, and a quasi-experiment can happen in one. Careful: RM01 slide 97 (next session's slides) highlights answer A, "Quasi-experiments take place in naturalistic environments", but by the guide's definition the right answer is B: random allocation. (2) A quasi-experiment still has an IV and a DV; it is not "just a correlation". (3) Extraneous and confounding are not the same word: an extraneous variable only could affect the DV; a confounding one is uncontrolled and differs between conditions.

Hypotheses

A hypothesis turns a research question into a statement that the data can support or contradict.

By the end of this section I can

The guide has no separate line for hypotheses, but you need them to "Design a study to investigate a research question" (AO3, p16), for Paper 2 Section A question 4 and for your IA. "Suggest" means "Propose a solution, hypothesis or other possible answer."

  • Research question: a question. "Does testing yourself help you remember?"
  • Experimental hypothesis: a testable statement that predicts the effect of the IV on the DV (RM01 slide 51).
  • Null hypothesis (H0): the IV will have no effect on the DV, and any difference is due to chance (slide 54).
  • One-tailed (directional): predicts which way the result will go: "more", "fewer", "faster" (slide 57).
  • Two-tailed (non-directional): predicts a difference but not which way: "will affect", "there will be a difference" (slide 58).

Recipe Understand and apply: [participants in condition A] will [more / fewer / differ in] [operationalised DV] than [participants in condition B].

After the study, a statistical test decides whether to reject the null hypothesis (the difference is too big to be chance) or retain it. Psychologists do not say a study "proves" a hypothesis (slide 56).

Worked example 9: three hypotheses for the flashcard study.
  1. One-tailed: "Students who revise 20 Spanish words for 10 minutes with self-testing flashcards will correctly translate more of the words in a test 24 hours later than students who reread the word list for 10 minutes."
  2. Two-tailed: "There will be a difference in the number of the 20 Spanish words correctly translated 24 hours later between students who revise with self-testing flashcards for 10 minutes and students who reread the list for 10 minutes."
  3. Null: "There will be no difference in the number of the 20 Spanish words correctly translated 24 hours later between the flashcard condition and the rereading condition; any difference will be due to chance."
  4. Checklist for every hypothesis: both conditions of the IV named; the DV given as a number you could record; the same people (who) and timing (when) stated; a statement, not a question.
Exam traps. (1) "Flashcards improve memory" is not a hypothesis you can test: no conditions, no operationalised DV. (2) The null hypothesis is "no difference", not the opposite prediction ("rereading is better"). (3) Never write "prove" or "this will show that"; data support or fail to support a hypothesis.

  2. "The colour of the test paper (blue or white) will affect the number of maths questions answered correctly in 15 minutes." One-tailed or two-tailed?

Answers. 1: one-tailed (it says "lower"). 2: two-tailed ("will affect", no direction). 3: "There will be no difference in the number of maths questions answered correctly in 15 minutes between students given blue paper and students given white paper; any difference will be due to chance."

Sampling techniques

You cannot test everyone, so the people you pick decide how far your results reach.

By the end of this section I can

Population, sample and bias

  • Target population: the whole group you want to know about, for example all Grade 11 students in Singapore.
  • Sample: the smaller group who actually take part. RM01 slide 28: sampling is selecting "a subset of individuals from a larger population to participate in a study".
  • Representative: the sample is like the population in the ways that matter (age, gender, school type). Then you can generalise: apply the results to the whole population.
  • Sampling bias (guide glossary): "This occurs when the sample used in a research study is not representative of the population from which it was drawn and to which the findings may be generalized." Example from the deck (slide 25): asking only children near the sweet stall which carnival ride is best.

The five techniques

The five names Learn word for word, each with how it works, one advantage and one disadvantage:

Self-selected (also called volunteer)

How: People offer to take part, for example after seeing an advert or a post.

Advantage: Easy to get many people; they are willing and have chosen to take part.

Disadvantage: Volunteer bias: volunteers tend to be alike (keen, interested, strong opinions), so the sample may not be representative.

Opportunity (also called convenience)

How: Whoever is available and willing at the time.

Advantage: Quick, cheap and easy.

Disadvantage: High risk of sampling bias; hard to generalise.

Stratified

How: Split the population into subgroups (strata) by a key feature, such as year group, then pick randomly from each subgroup in the same proportion as in the population.

Advantage: Every subgroup is fairly represented, so results generalise well.

Disadvantage: Needs detailed information about the population; slower and harder to organise; some people fit more than one subgroup.

Random

How: Every member of the target population has an equal chance of being picked, for example by a computer from a full list.

Advantage: Reduces selection bias; the sample is likely to be representative.

Disadvantage: Needs a complete list of the population; impractical for very large or spread-out populations; people who refuse can still bias it.

Snowball

How: The first participants recruit others they know who fit the study.

Advantage: Reaches hidden or hard-to-reach groups; people may trust the study more.

Disadvantage: Participants know each other and tend to be similar, so the sample is biased; privacy concerns.

Worked example 10: a stratified sample, with numbers. A school has 200 Grade 11 students: 120 take the IB Diploma and 80 take another programme. A researcher wants a stratified sample of 30. Understand and apply
  1. Find the fraction of the population to sample: 30 out of 200 = 0.15 (15%).
  2. Apply it to each stratum: 120 × 0.15 = 18 IB students; 80 × 0.15 = 12 others.
  3. Check: 18 + 12 = 30, and 18 : 12 is the same ratio as 120 : 80 (3 : 2).
  4. Then choose the 18 at random from the IB list and the 12 at random from the other list. Picking the first 18 IB students you meet would not be stratified sampling.
Worked example 11: choose and justify a technique. A researcher wants to interview teenagers who look after a sick parent at home (young carers) about their stress. Understand and apply
  1. Is there a list of the population? No: young carers are often not known to schools, so random and stratified sampling are not possible.
  2. Choose snowball sampling: find a few young carers through a support group, and ask each to pass the study on to other young carers they know.
  3. Justify: it reaches a hidden group, and young carers may trust a study that a friend recommends.
  4. One disadvantage: participants found through the same support group and friendships are likely to be similar (same area, same kind of support), so the results may not generalise to all young carers.

Simply Psychology's free page Sampling methods explains the same techniques. It also covers systematic, cluster, quota and purposive sampling, which are not in the IB guide; you do not need them.

Exam traps. (1) Random sampling is not random allocation. Random sampling: everyone in the population has an equal chance of being in the study. Random allocation: chance decides which condition each participant goes into. A true experiment needs random allocation; it can still use an opportunity sample. (2) "Stopping people at random in the street" is opportunity sampling, not random sampling: people who are not in that street that day have no chance of being picked. (3) RM01 slide 39 (filling equal numbers of men, women and age groups until quotas are full) is quota sampling. It looks like stratified sampling but people are not picked at random, and it is not one of the five guide techniques. (4) For a disadvantage, say why the sample is unrepresentative, linked to the study, not just "it is biased".

The DV is what is measured: words recalled. Background noise is the IV (what the researcher changes). The type of words is an extraneous variable to keep the same.

Choosing the 40 from the register is random sampling; the coin toss decides the condition, which is random allocation. Mixing these two up is the classic trap.

Nobody can be allocated to "only child" by chance: the groups already exist, so it is quasi. The first and third are true experiments; the second is a field experiment (still random allocation).

Only the first says exactly what is counted and how, so anyone could repeat it. The others name the construct (memory) without saying how it is measured.

The students choose themselves by signing up: self-selected (volunteer). The trap is opportunity, where the researcher approaches whoever is around.

25 out of 250 is 10%: 150 × 0.1 = 15 and 100 × 0.1 = 10. Stratified means the same proportions as the population, not equal numbers; 10 and 15 swaps the groups.
