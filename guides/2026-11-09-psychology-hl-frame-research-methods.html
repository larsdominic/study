How a scientific study works

Psychology is a science about why people act, think and feel as they do. A psychologist does not just give an opinion: they collect evidence from real people.

  • Question: does exercise make people happier?
  • Prediction: people who exercise for a week will rate their mood higher.
  • Collect and compare: both groups rate their mood from 1 to 10. Is the exercise group higher?
  • Conclusion: does the evidence support the prediction?

If other researchers repeat a study and get the same result, we trust it more.

Cause, or just a link?

On days when more ice cream is sold, more people get sunburnt. Ice cream does not cause sunburn: hot weather causes both. The two are linked, but one does not cause the other.

To show that X causes Y, a researcher changes X on purpose, keeps everything else the same, and checks whether Y changes. Much of today is about this.

Population and sample: tasting the soup

To check whether a pot of soup needs salt, you stir it and taste one spoonful.

  • The pot is the population: everyone you want to know about.
  • The spoonful is the sample: the people you actually study.
  • An unstirred spoonful from the top might taste nothing like the rest: a sample that is not like the population gives a wrong answer.

What "random" means in science

In everyday talk random means odd ("a random question"). In science it means decided by chance, so every option has the same chance. A computer picking 60 names from a list of 600 is random: every name has a 1 in 10 chance.

Walking up to whoever you happen to see is not random: people who are not there have no chance at all.

How IB exams are marked

IB Psychology papers have no multiple choice. Examiners mark written answers with markbands: descriptions of each level (for example 1-2 marks: limited knowledge; 3-4 marks: detailed knowledge, example explained).

The first word of each question is a command term (describe, explain, evaluate...). Answering "describe" when it says "explain" keeps you in a lower band, even if everything you wrote is true.

Worked example 2: one topic, three command terms. Operationalisation (stating exactly how a variable is measured; see variables) needs a bigger answer as the command term climbs.
  1. Describe operationalisation. What it is, with detail: "Stating exactly how a variable will be changed or measured. For example, memory as the number of words out of 20 written down in two minutes."
  2. Explain why researchers operationalise variables. Add reasons: "...because a vague idea like memory cannot be counted fairly. A precise measure scores everyone the same way, so others can repeat the study and check the result."
  3. Evaluate operationalisation. Strength, limitation, judgement: "It is precise, so the study can be repeated. But a word list is narrower than everyday memory. Overall it is necessary, but the measure must be judged for how well it matches the idea."
Why this step? Why does the evaluate answer end with "Overall..."?

An appraisal is a judgement. Listing strengths and limitations without saying what they add up to is only half of "evaluate".

Finish it. "Explain why researchers use a control group." First half done: "A control group is a group that does not get the thing being tested, for example people who do no exercise in an exercise study. Researchers use it because ____."
Answer

"...it shows what happens without the IV. If the exercise group's mood rises and the control group's mood stays the same, the rise is more likely to be caused by exercise than by something that happened to everyone, such as a sunny week." The "because" with a reason is what turns a description into an explanation.

Exam traps. (1) Only describing when the question says explain keeps you in the lower markband. (2) Paper 1 Section A (4 marks): the top band needs "detailed knowledge" and an example that "is relevant and explained" (p45). (3) In the specimen markscheme only the first example is credited, so choose one and develop it.

Concepts · guide p20-23

The six concepts

By the end of this section I can

Paper 1 Section C and Paper 2 Section B name concepts in the question. The six names Learn word for word: Bias, Causality, Change, Measurement, Perspective, Responsibility (alphabetical, as the guide lists them).

Bias (p20)

"A bias is a limitation in objective thinking; it is a tendency to perceive information through a cognitive filter of experience and preference."

Asks: what tilted the results: the researcher, the participants or the sample?

Causality (p21)

"Psychologists investigate the relationship between variables with the goal of determining cause-and-effect relationships."

Asks: does X really cause Y, or is something else going on?

Change (p21)

"A key focus of psychological research is how to apply theory and psychological findings with the goal of changing individual behaviours."

Asks: how can psychology help people change, for example stop smoking?

Measurement (p22)

"A fundamental challenge for psychologists is that human behaviour is difficult to observe and objectively measure."

Asks: how was something inside the head turned into data, and how fair is that?

Perspective (p22-23)

"No single perspective explains behaviour sufficiently on its own; each provides a means by which to interpret and examine behaviour."

Asks: what would the biological, cognitive or sociocultural lens add or miss?

Responsibility (p23)

"Psychologists rely on humans as participants in their research; the relationship between researchers and participants is based on the premise of treating each other with respect."

Asks: were participants treated fairly: consent, no harm, free to leave?

Worked example 3: two concepts on one study. RM01 slides 4-8: one group exercises 30 minutes a day for a week, a control group does no exercise, and both rate their mood from 1 to 10. The exercise group's average goes from 4 to 7; the control group ends at 4.2.
  1. Measurement. Mood, which cannot be seen, was turned into a number: a self-rating from 1 to 10. That makes groups easy to compare, but people may rate themselves higher because they guess what the researcher hopes to see.
  2. Causality. The control group helps: without exercise, mood barely changed. But if the exercise group also slept better, sleep could explain the result. Then we cannot say exercise caused the better mood.
Why this step? Why mention sleep in step 2?

Causality is about ruling out other causes. Naming one specific other cause (sleep) shows you understand the concept, instead of just saying "it may not be causal".

Finish it. Study: students rate how much they slept last night (1 to 10) and say how long they used their phone after 10 pm. Those with more phone time rate their sleep lower. Measurement (done): sleep was measured by self-rating, which is quick but depends on honest, accurate memory. Causality: ____.
Answer

Nobody changed phone use on purpose and nothing else was controlled, so something else (stress, homework, a noisy home) could cause both late phone use and poor sleep. The study shows a link, not that phones cause poor sleep.

Exam traps. (1) "Perspective" is not your personal opinion: it means a way of explaining behaviour (biological, cognitive, sociocultural, or a cultural viewpoint). (2) "Bias" in the guide is about thinking and research, not only about being unfair to a group. (3) Use the guide's form: "measurement", not "measuring"; "causality", not "cause".

Variables, constructs, operationalisation

  • Variable (guide glossary): "Any factor or characteristic that can vary and is subject to measurement or manipulation in research." Reaction time, words recalled, hours of sleep.
  • Construct (guide glossary): "An abstract idea, concept or variable that cannot be directly observed but is used to explain or measure aspects of human behaviour. Examples include intelligence and self-esteem." You cannot say "she has 6 units of stress" (RM01 slide 12).
  • Operationalisation (guide glossary): "Stating exactly how a variable will be manipulated or measured in experimental research." Once a construct is operationalised, you can measure it.

The independent and dependent variable

An experiment asks "If I change X, does Y change?" (RM01 slide 18). The independent variable (IV) is changed on purpose and has at least two conditions (exercise, no exercise). The dependent variable (DV) is measured.

Worked example 4: identify and operationalise the IV and the DV. Research question: "Does testing yourself with flashcards help you remember vocabulary better than rereading?" Understand and apply
  1. What does the researcher change? The way of revising. That is the IV.
  2. Operationalise the IV by stating both conditions exactly. Condition A: 10 minutes with 20 flashcards (Spanish word on the front, English on the back), saying the answer before turning each card. Condition B: 10 minutes rereading the same 20 Spanish and English word pairs on a printed list.
  3. What is measured? How much is remembered. That is the DV.
  4. Operationalise the DV: the number of the 20 Spanish words correctly translated into English in a written test 24 hours later (a score from 0 to 20).
Why this step? Why spell out condition B as well, not just "rereading"?

The IV is both conditions. If B is vague (how long? which words?), another researcher could not repeat it, and the two conditions might differ in more than the revision method.

Finish it. Research question: "Does chewing gum help concentration?" IV (done): condition A, chewing one piece of mint gum during the task; condition B, no gum. DV: ____.
Answer

For example: the number of spelling mistakes found, out of 30 hidden in a printed text, in 5 minutes. Any answer works if it is a number you could record and someone else could measure the same way. "Concentration" alone is not operationalised.

Exam traps. (1) "The DV is memory" is not operationalised; "the number of words out of 20 recalled in 2 minutes" is. (2) Give the IV as its conditions ("flashcards or rereading"), not as one of them.
Self-check: variables (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. Half the participants watch a sad film clip and half a neutral one; then the researcher counts how many biscuits each person eats in 10 minutes. Name the IV and the DV.
  2. Is self-esteem a variable or a construct?
  3. Interview transcripts about exam results day: quantitative or qualitative data?
  4. Give one operationalised DV for "anxiety before a test".

Answers. 1: IV: type of film clip (sad or neutral); DV: number of biscuits eaten in 10 minutes. 2: a construct; it becomes a variable once operationalised, for example as a score on a self-esteem questionnaire. 3: qualitative (words, not numbers). 4: for example, self-rated anxiety from 1 to 10 just before the test, or heart rate in beats per minute one minute before the test.

Extra, not needed today: operationalising stress, two ways

A researcher wants to study stress, a construct. Option A, the body: heart rate in beats per minute, measured with a finger clip while the participant sits still for one minute. Option B, self-report: the total score on a 10-question stress questionnaire, each question scored 1 to 5, so totals run from 10 to 50. Both are repeatable because the device, timing and scoring are stated. Heart rate is objective but also rises with exercise or coffee; the questionnaire asks the person directly but depends on honest answers. Neither is perfect, which is the guide's point about measurement.

Experiments, controls and confounding variables

True experiment and quasi-experiment

The guide lists "experiments (true or quasi-)" (p28):

True experiment: "A controlled experiment where researchers manipulate an independent variable to observe its impact on a dependent variable, using random allocation of participants to conditions to ensure causation."Guide glossary, quoted in RM02
Quasi-experiment: "A research method that resembles a true experiment but lacks random assignment of participants to different groups. In a true experiment, researchers randomly assign participants to different conditions. In a quasi-experiment, researchers often have limited control over participant assignment, meaning it is unable to establish a cause-and-effect relationship."Guide glossary, quoted in RM02

Random allocation: chance (a coin toss) decides each participant's condition, which spreads differences between people evenly, so they cannot explain a difference in the DV. In a quasi-experiment the groups exist already (age, gender, a diagnosis).

True experiment

Researcher changes the IV; random allocation. Example: caffeine or decaf by chance, then an alertness test (RM01 slide 48).

Quasi-experiment

Groups exist already; no random allocation. Example: Maguire et al. (2000): London taxi drivers and non-drivers, brain scans compared (RM02).

Field experiment (class word)

A true experiment in real life. Example: Piliavin et al. (1969): helping on a real New York subway train.

Natural experiment (class word)

A real event changes the IV; a kind of quasi-experiment. Example: a smoking ban (RM01 slides 92-94).

Controls and confounding variables

Extraneous variable: "Variables other than the independent variable in an experiment that can potentially influence the dependent variable. If not controlled, they can become confounding variables."Guide glossary. The guide's learning objective says "external variables"; RM02 uses both words.

A control variable is kept the same in every condition (RM01 slide 20). A confounding variable was not controlled and differs between the conditions, so there are two possible causes (slide 19). Controls: standardisation (same instructions, materials, room, timing), random allocation, and a control group. Only then can you say the IV caused the change: the link to causality.

Worked example 5: spot and fix a confounding variable. In the flashcard study, the flashcard group is tested at 9 am and the rereading group at 3 pm, straight after PE. The flashcard group scores higher. Understand and apply
  1. What differs apart from the IV? Time of day and tiredness after sport.
  2. Could that affect the DV (words recalled)? Yes: tired students may recall less.
  3. So time of day is a confounding variable: the higher score may come from the flashcards or from being fresh.
  4. Fix: same time, same room, same scripted instructions (standardisation), groups by drawing names (random allocation).
Why this step? Why ask in step 2 whether it could affect the DV?

Only a difference that could change the DV is a problem. If the groups had different-coloured pens, that would not matter; tiredness could change recall, so it confounds.

Model answers: RM02 activities 1, 2 and 9 (open after you have written yours)

1. Three differences between true and quasi-experiments. (a) Allocation: in a true experiment chance decides each participant's condition; in a quasi-experiment the groups already exist (for example age or gender), so there is no random allocation. (b) The IV: in a true experiment the researcher manipulates it; in a quasi-experiment it is often a characteristic people already have. (c) Control and cause: a true experiment has more control over extraneous variables and gives stronger evidence of cause and effect; the guide says a quasi-experiment cannot establish cause and effect.

2. The significance of random assignment. Chance decides which condition each participant joins, so differences between people (ability, motivation, tiredness) are spread evenly across the conditions. The IV is then the only systematic difference between the groups, so a difference in the DV can be put down to the IV. That is why the guide's definition says random allocation is used "to ensure causation".

9. How external variables threaten internal validity. Internal validity (class glossary) is whether a study actually measures what it claims to: in an experiment, whether the change in the DV really comes from the IV. An external (extraneous) variable can also affect the DV. If it is not controlled and differs between the conditions, it becomes confounding, and the result could be caused by it instead of the IV. Example: worked example 5, where testing time differed between the groups.

Hypotheses

By the end of this section I can

Not a separate line in the guide, but needed for Paper 2 Section A question 4 (design) and your IA.

  • Experimental hypothesis: a testable statement predicting the effect of the IV on the DV (RM01 slide 51).
  • Null hypothesis (H0): the IV will have no effect on the DV, and any difference is due to chance (slide 54).
  • One-tailed (directional): says which way: "more", "fewer", "faster" (slide 57).
  • Two-tailed (non-directional): predicts a difference but not which way: "will affect", "there will be a difference" (slide 58).

Recipe Understand and apply: [participants in condition A] will [more / fewer / differ in] [operationalised DV] than [participants in condition B]. After the study, a statistical test decides whether to reject the null hypothesis (the difference is too big to be chance) or retain it.

Worked example 7: three hypotheses for the flashcard study.
  1. One-tailed: "Students who revise 20 Spanish words for 10 minutes with self-testing flashcards will correctly translate more of the words in a test 24 hours later than students who reread the word list for 10 minutes."
  2. Two-tailed: the same, but "there will be a difference in the number of words correctly translated" instead of "more".
  3. Null: "There will be no difference in the number of the 20 Spanish words correctly translated 24 hours later between the flashcard condition and the rereading condition; any difference will be due to chance."
  4. Checklist: both IV conditions named; the DV a number you could record; who and when stated; a statement, not a question.
Why this step? Why does the null hypothesis end "due to chance"?

It says what you expect if the IV does nothing: any small difference is luck. The statistical test then checks whether the real difference is too big to be luck.

Finish it. A caffeine study. One-tailed (done): "Participants who drink a cup of coffee with caffeine will press a key faster, on average over 20 trials of a reaction-time test, than participants who drink a cup of decaf." Null: "There will be no difference in ____."
Answer

"There will be no difference in average reaction time over 20 trials between participants who drink coffee with caffeine and participants who drink decaf; any difference will be due to chance."

Exam traps. (1) "Flashcards improve memory" is not testable: no conditions, no operationalised DV. (2) The null is "no difference", not the opposite prediction. (3) Never write "prove" (slide 56): data support or fail to support a hypothesis.

Sampling techniques · guide p28 · RM01 slides 21-42 · RM03

Sampling techniques

By the end of this section I can

  • Target population: the whole group you want to know about (all Grade 11 students in Singapore). The sample is the smaller group who take part.
  • Representative: like the population in the ways that matter. Then you can generalise the results to the population.
  • Sampling bias (guide glossary): "This occurs when the sample used in a research study is not representative of the population from which it was drawn and to which the findings may be generalized."

The five techniques

Self-selected (volunteer)

How: people offer to take part, for example after seeing an advert.

+ many willing people. − volunteer bias: volunteers tend to be alike.

Opportunity (convenience)

How: whoever is available and willing at the time.

+ quick, cheap, easy. − high risk of sampling bias; hard to generalise.

Stratified

How: split the population into subgroups (strata), such as year groups, then pick randomly from each in the same proportion as in the population.

+ every subgroup fairly represented. − needs detailed information about the population; slow.

Random

How: every member of the target population has an equal chance of being picked, for example by computer from a full list.

+ reduces selection bias. − needs a complete list of the population.

Snowball

How: the first participants recruit others they know who fit the study.

+ reaches hard-to-reach groups. − participants tend to be similar; privacy concerns.

Worked example 8: a stratified sample, with numbers. A school has 200 Grade 11 students: 120 take the IB Diploma and 80 take another programme. A researcher wants a stratified sample of 30. Understand and apply
  1. Fraction of the population to sample: 30 out of 200 = 0.15 (15%).
  2. Apply it to each stratum: 120 × 0.15 = 18 IB students; 80 × 0.15 = 12 others.
  3. Check: 18 + 12 = 30, and 18 : 12 is the same ratio as 120 : 80 (3 : 2).
  4. Then choose the 18 at random from the IB list and the 12 at random from the other list.
Why this step? Why pick at random inside each stratum in step 4?

Picking the first 18 IB students you meet would let your own choice tilt the sample (bias). Random choice inside each stratum keeps it fair; without it, it is not stratified sampling.

Worked example 9: choose and justify a technique. A researcher wants to interview teenagers who look after a sick parent at home (young carers) about their stress. Understand and apply
  1. Is there a list of the population? No, so random and stratified sampling are impossible.
  2. Choose snowball sampling: find a few through a support group; each passes the study on to others they know.
  3. Justify: it reaches a hidden group, who may trust a study a friend recommends.
  4. Disadvantage: people from the same group and friendships are likely to be similar, so results may not generalise to all young carers.
Why this step? Why start by asking about a list?

Random and stratified sampling both need a full list of the population. Checking that first tells you which techniques are even possible.

Finish it. A school has 300 students: 180 girls and 120 boys. Stratified sample of 20. Fraction: 20 out of 300 = 1/15. Girls: 180 ÷ 15 = 12. Boys: ____. Check: ____.
Answer

Boys: 120 ÷ 15 = 8. Check: 12 + 8 = 20, and 12 : 8 = 3 : 2, the same ratio as 180 : 120. Then pick the 12 girls and 8 boys at random from each list.

Exam traps. (1) Random sampling is not random allocation. A true experiment needs random allocation; it can still use an opportunity sample. (2) "Stopping people at random in the street" is opportunity sampling. (3) For a disadvantage, say why the sample is unrepresentative, linked to the study.

8. Explain random allocation in a true experiment. Provide an example. [4 marks]

Show model answer

Random allocation means chance (a coin toss, drawing names) decides which condition each participant goes into. This matters because people differ in ways that could affect the DV, such as ability, motivation or tiredness. Chance spreads these differences evenly across the conditions, so they cannot explain a difference in the DV; this is why the guide says it is used "to ensure causation". 10. Explain the concept of causality in relation to the flashcard study (worked examples 4 and 5). [4 marks]

Show model answer

Causality is about whether one variable really causes a change in another; the guide says psychologists investigate the relationship between variables "with the goal of determining cause-and-effect relationships".
