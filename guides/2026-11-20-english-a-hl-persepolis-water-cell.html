How to read a comic page

A comic or graphic novel tells a story in boxes called panels. In English you read them left to right, top row first, like text. The white strip between two panels is the gutter.

Inside a panel there are two kinds of words: a speech balloon (with a little tail pointing at the speaker) holds what a character says; a caption (a box, often at the top) holds the narrator's words.

Every drawing is a choice: how close we are, where we look from, how big the panel is, what is black and what is white. In English class you analyse those choices the way you would analyse word choices in a poem.

Persepolis in a few lines

Persepolis is Marjane Satrapi's memoir in comic form: the true story of her own childhood in Iran around the 1979 revolution, drawn only in black and white.

Two voices tell it. The pictures show the child, Marji, as she was then. The captions are the grown-up Marjane looking back, who knows much more than the child did. Much of the book's meaning comes from the gap between the two.

In "The Water Cell" (pp. 18-25 in your class edition) Marji's parents are out protesting every day, and at home she learns things about her country and her own family that her school never told her.

Iran's rulers, only as much as you need

For many years Iran was ruled by kings of one family, the Qajar dynasty. In the 1920s an army officer, Reza Shah, took power and made himself king ("Shah" means king). His son, also called the Shah, ruled after him until the 1979 revolution, when huge protests ended the monarchy.

That is all the history the Water Cell questions need. Your book gives the rest.

Close or far, high or low: how a camera sees

Film makers and comic artists make two choices for every picture. How close is the camera? Far away shows a whole place; very close shows one eye. How high is the camera? Looking down on someone makes them seem small; looking up makes them seem big and strong.

Try it with your phone: photograph a friend from far, then close, then from a chair above them, then from the floor. Each photo makes you feel differently about the same person. That feeling is the effect you write about.

Want to see it explained another way? BBC Bitesize: Types of shot (Moving Image Arts)

What analysis means in English A

Analysis is not retelling the story. It answers three questions about one choice the writer or artist made:

  • What is the choice? (a close-up, a black background, a short caption)
  • What does it do to the reader? (we feel close to her, we feel she is trapped)
  • Why did the writer want that? (to show how a child meets violence)

A sentence that only names a technique earns very little. A sentence that goes from the technique to its effect to its purpose is what examiners reward.

Literary and non-literary texts

The IB sorts texts into two groups. Literary texts are works of literature: novels, poems, plays, short stories and graphic novels. Non-literary texts are everything else people read and see: adverts, cartoons, blogs, speeches, photographs, posters, news articles.

In Paper 1 you get two unseen non-literary texts (texts you have never seen before). Your class books, like Persepolis, are literary works and are used in other parts of the course.

Writing for an audience in French

In a French B writing task, three questions decide your text before you write a word: who reads it (here: your classmates), why (to persuade them to use phones less) and what kind of text (a speech or a blog post).

The way you speak to the reader is the register. To a group of classmates you write in a friendly but correct way and use vous, because you are talking to several people. A job application letter would be the wrong kind of text for this reader and this purpose, however good its French.

Session 3 · film codes worksheet

Shots and angles

How much do we see, and where are we looking from?

By the end of this section I can

A graphic novel artist works like a film director. For every panel she chooses how close the "camera" is (the shot type: how much we see) and how high it is (the angle: where we look from). These are two different choices.

The five shot types Learn

ShotWhat it showsUsual effect on the reader
Extreme close-up (ECU)One tiny detail fills the frame: an eye, a scar, one object.Intensity: you must look at that one thing.
Close-up (CU)A face or hands; the head fills most of the frame.Intimacy or tension: you feel close and read the emotion.
Medium shot (MS)From the waist or chest up: face and body language.Natural, like a conversation.
Long shot (LS)The whole body inside its surroundings.Context: you see where the character is.
Extreme long shot (ELS)The character is tiny in a huge setting.Scale, isolation or awe.

The three angles Learn

AngleWhere the camera isUsual effect
High angle (bird's-eye view on your worksheet)Above, looking down.The subject looks small, weak or vulnerable; the reader feels powerful.
Eye levelAt the subject's height.Neutral. It is the default, so when a panel breaks from eye level, ask why.
Low angle (worm's-eye view on your worksheet)Below, looking up.The subject looks powerful, threatening or heroic; the reader feels small.

The two choices combine: a low-angle close-up feels powerful and threatening; a high-angle extreme long shot (a tiny figure seen from above) feels isolated and vulnerable.

Worked example. A made-up panel: we look down on a small girl standing alone in the middle of a large, empty school yard.
  1. How much do we see? Her whole body and a lot of space around her: a long shot. Not ELS: she is still clearly visible, not a dot.
  2. Where is the camera? Above her, looking down: a high angle.
  3. Effect: the empty space and the downward view make her look small and alone, so the reader feels sorry for her.
  4. Exam sentence: "The high-angle long shot leaves the girl small in an empty yard, so the reader looks down on her isolation."
Why this step? Why decide the shot type and the angle separately (steps 1 and 2)?

Because they are two different choices with different effects. Here both point the same way (small, alone), which makes the analysis stronger: you can say the artist uses two techniques together.

Finish it. A made-up panel: we look up at a headteacher's shouting face, which fills the frame.
  1. Shot: a close-up (the face fills the frame).
  2. Angle: ...
  3. Effect on the reader: ...
Show the answer

Angle: low angle (we look up). Effect: the headteacher looks powerful and threatening, and the reader feels small, like a pupil being told off. Together: a low-angle close-up.

Exam traps. (1) "A low-angle shot" says nothing about how close we are. (2) Naming a shot earns little: the IB's Paper 1 criterion B describes a response that only identifies features as "descriptive". Say what the choice does. (3) Usual effects are a starting point: explain the effect in this panel.
Self-check: write two key words for each, then open
  1. A panel shows only a hand gripping a door handle. Which shot?
  2. Two people from the chest up, talking. Which shot?
  3. We look up at a soldier from his boots. Which angle, and what is the usual effect?
  4. "This is a high-angle shot, so it is a close-up." What is wrong?

Answers. 1: close-up (hands count as CU on your worksheet). 2: medium shot. 3: low angle; he looks powerful and the reader feels small. 4: angle and shot type are separate choices; a high-angle shot can be any distance.

Sessions 4 and 5 · time, motion and the role of the reader

Panels, gutters and closure

A comic controls time with boxes and gaps, and lets the reader fill the gaps.

By the end of this section I can

Controlling time (Session 4)

TechniqueWhat it can do
Panel size and shapeA bigger or wider panel makes a moment feel longer and more important; small panels feel quick.
Silent panel (no words)A pause: the reader stops and looks.
Frame (the border line) or none; bleed (the picture runs off the page edge)A frameless or bleeding image feels open, not held in one moment.
Gutter (the gap between panels)Time passes there; a wide or black gutter can feel like a longer or darker break.
Grid layoutA regular grid gives a steady rhythm; breaking it draws attention.
Motion lines, sound effects, speechShow speed and noise; speech takes time to read, so it slows a panel.
Overlapping balloonsA quick conversation where people interrupt.

Closure (Session 5) Learn

Your Session 5 slide quotes the comics writer Scott McCloud: closure is "observing the parts but perceiving the whole". Panels show separate moments, and the reader's mind joins them into one story. If panel 1 shows a raised hand and panel 2 shows someone crying, you "see" the slap that is never drawn.

The six panel transitions Learn

McCloud's six kinds of jump from one panel to the next. Class terms, not IB terms: the examiner rewards what the transition does, not the label.

TransitionWhat changes between the panels
Moment-to-momentAlmost nothing: the same thing a second later. Slow motion; asks the least of the reader.
Action-to-actionOne subject doing one thing after another. The most common kind.
Subject-to-subjectDifferent people or things in the same scene.
Scene-to-sceneA jump across a lot of time or space; the reader fills a big gap.
Aspect-to-aspectDifferent parts of one place, idea or mood; time seems to stand still.
Non-sequiturNo logical link at all; the reader still searches for one.
Worked example. A made-up sequence. Panel 1: a mother reads a letter. Panel 2: close-up of her hand dropping the letter. Panel 3: the empty kitchen at night.
  1. Panel 1 to 2: the same person, the next action: action-to-action.
  2. Panel 2 to 3: hours pass and she is gone: a jump in time, so scene-to-scene.
  3. Effect: the gutter hides what she did next. The reader must imagine her reaction, which makes the news feel too painful to show.
Why this step? Why is step 3 the one that earns marks?

Steps 1 and 2 only label. Step 3 says what the gap makes the reader do and feel, which is analysis of an authorial choice.

Finish it. A made-up sequence. Panel 1: soldiers knock at a door. Panel 2: a family hides under a table in the dark.
  1. Transition: the scene stays the same house, but we move from the soldiers to the family: ...
  2. Effect on the reader: ...
Show the answer

Subject-to-subject (different people, same scene). Effect: the gutter makes the reader imagine what happens at the door, so the fear grows; we share the family's view of danger they cannot see.

Self-check: write two key words for each, then open
  1. Why might an artist put one important moment in a very wide panel?
  2. Panel 1: a girl at her desk. Panel 2: her teacher at the board. Panel 3: the classmate next to her. Which transition?
  3. Explain closure in one sentence of your own.

Answers. 1: a wider panel makes the moment feel longer, so the reader lingers. 2: subject-to-subject. 3: for example, "the reader's mind joins separate panels into one story, filling in what is not drawn."

Session 7 · captions, combinations, black and white

Words, pictures and black and white

Words and pictures can repeat, add to or fight each other.

By the end of this section I can

A speech balloon holds what a character says (a thought balloon, what they think). A caption is a box of narration. In Persepolis the captions are the voice of the adult Marjane looking back, while the pictures show the child Marji: that gap is a key source of the book's perspective.

The seven word-picture combinations Learn

CombinationHow words and picture work together
Word-specificThe words tell it all; the picture only illustrates.
Picture-specificThe picture tells it all; the words are like a soundtrack ("Bang!").
Duo-specificBoth say the same thing.
AdditiveOne makes the other stronger or adds detail.
ParallelWords and picture follow different tracks and do not meet.
MontageThe words are drawn as part of the picture.
InterdependentTogether they say something neither could say alone. The most common kind.

Black and white: chiaroscuro Apply

Persepolis is drawn only in black and white, with big areas of solid black. Chiaroscuro (an art word, Italian for "light-dark") means strong contrast between light and dark areas. A symbol is a thing that stands for an idea; a motif is an image or idea that keeps coming back. Your cartoons guide gives the rule: analyse the choice, do not guess the feeling. Weak: "Black makes the reader sad." Strong: "Here, the solid black around the small figure isolates her."

Worked example. A made-up panel: a father laughing at a family dinner. Caption: "That was the last time I saw him laugh."
  1. Picture alone: a happy meal. Words alone: a sad memory. Together: we know something terrible is coming that the family does not. So: interdependent.
  2. Effect: the reader enjoys the happy scene and grieves at the same moment.
  3. Purpose: the adult narrator's knowledge hangs over the child's happy world.
Why this step? Why look at the picture and the words separately first (step 1)?

Only by asking what each says alone can you see what they say together, and that difference is what you analyse.

Finish it. A made-up panel: a small white figure in a doorway; everything else is solid black.
  1. Feature: chiaroscuro, strong contrast of white and black.
  2. What the white and the black could stand for here: ...
  3. Effect on the reader: ...
Show the answer

For example: the white figure stands for someone innocent or alone; the black for danger or the unknown pressing in. Effect: the reader feels how exposed and fragile the figure is. Any reading is fine if it comes from this panel.

Self-check: write two key words for each, then open
  1. A panel shows a burning building; the only words are "CRASH!" Which combination?
  2. Who "speaks" in a Persepolis caption, and why does that matter?
  3. Improve: "The picture is black and white, which is sad."

Answers. 1: picture-specific. 2: the adult Marjane looking back; the reader gets both the child's view (pictures) and the adult's understanding (captions). 3: for example, "The solid black around the white figure isolates her, suggesting innocence surrounded by a darkness she does not understand."

After marking: points that are easy to miss

Paper 1 skill · the analysis chain · ibo.org guide

From technique to paragraph

Never stop at naming the technique: say what it does and why.

By the end of this section I can

Model paragraph. A made-up panel: a high-angle long shot of a small boy alone in a large, empty classroom; the past-tense caption reads "Nobody told me why they had gone."

[Topic sentence] The artist uses a high-angle long shot to show a child left outside adult events. [Evidence and micro-analysis] Seen from above, the boy is small in the middle of an empty classroom, so the reader looks down on him and feels his isolation before reading a word. The past-tense caption, "Nobody told me why they had gone", adds an older narrator who now knows what the boy did not; this gap between the remembering voice and the confused child lets the reader share both views. [Synthesis] The panel therefore represents childhood as a time of being surrounded by events that no one explains, a question of perspective.

Why this step? Why does the synthesis name a concept (perspective)?

It lifts one panel to the big idea of the text. The Part 4 scheme gives 2 marks for linking the insight to representation or perspective.

Finish it. Same panel, your turn for one link of the chain.
  1. Spot it: the empty desks around the boy.
  2. Explain it: rows of empty desks fill most of the panel.
  3. Effect on the reader: ...
  4. Purpose: ...
Show the answer

Effect: the reader notices who is missing and feels the silence of the room. Purpose: the absence shows a loss the child cannot explain, so the reader, like him, must guess what happened.

Perspective and representation

  • Perspective: whose point of view a text shows, and how the reader's own view shapes the reading. The IB guide: "A text may offer a multiplicity of perspectives which may, or may not, reflect the views of its author."
  • Representation: how a text shows reality, and how its form (pictures, layout, black and white) shapes the meaning.
Exam trap. Retelling the story earns almost nothing, and a list of techniques is "descriptive" in the criterion B wording. Every technique you name needs its effect and its purpose.

The trap in this task. A lettre de motivation applies for a job or a place: it fits neither classmates nor the purpose of persuading them. Choose the discours (for example at a school assembly) or a school blog post. The IB Language B guide warns that a response that ignores context, purpose and audience may get 0 for criteria B and C, even if the French is good.

Self-check list before you hand it in

  1. Discours or blog, with its conventions at the start and at the end.
  2. Register suits classmates and stays the same (vous for the group).
  3. Both halves: at least two problems and at least two other things to do at break.
  4. Every idea has a detail or an example.
  5. Paragraphs, one main idea each, joined by connectors.
  6. Three cause connectors from 18 Sep, built correctly: parce que, comme, puisque, étant donné que + subject and verb; grâce à, à cause de, en raison de + noun.

1. A panel shows a girl's whole body standing in an empty street, with the houses around her. Which shot type?

Whole body plus surroundings is a long shot. A medium shot stops at the waist or chest; an extreme long shot would make her tiny in a vast setting.

2. The artist draws a soldier from below, looking up at him. This is

Camera below looking up is a low angle, which usually makes the subject look powerful. Vulnerable is the usual effect of a high angle.

3. Panel 1: a boy kicks a ball. Panel 2: the ball hits a window. Which transition?

One subject moving through actions: action-to-action. Moment-to-moment shows almost no change; scene-to-scene jumps across time or place.

4. A crying girl, with the caption "I was so sad." McCloud would call this word-picture combination

Words and picture send the same message: duo-specific. Interdependent means together they say something neither says alone.

8. Explain what closure is, and what the reader does in this made-up pair: panel 1, a man raises a stick over a dog; panel 2, an empty street with the sound effect "YELP!" [2]

Show model answer

Closure is the reader's mind joining separate panels into one story (1). Here the reader imagines the blow that is never drawn, so the violence feels worse for being hidden (1).

Checklist: a correct definition in your own words; what the reader fills in for these panels.

9. A made-up panel: a low-angle close-up of a soldier shouting. Identify the shot and angle, and explain one effect on the reader. [2]

Show model answer

A low-angle close-up (1). The face fills the frame and we look up at it, so the soldier seems huge and threatening and the reader feels small and powerless (1).

Checklist: both terms correct (shot and angle); an effect on the reader, not just "it shows the soldier".

10. A made-up advert: a tiny family sits under a giant smartphone; the slogan reads "Always connected." Analyse one visual choice and one verbal choice, and give the purpose. [3]

Show model answer

Scale: the giant phone towers over the tiny family, so the reader sees technology dominating them (1). The slogan "Always connected" sounds positive, but next to the image it becomes ironic: the family is connected to the phone, not to each other (1). Purpose: the advert criticises how screens push out real family contact (1).

Checklist: one visual choice with its effect; one verbal choice with its effect (here, irony); a purpose sentence.
