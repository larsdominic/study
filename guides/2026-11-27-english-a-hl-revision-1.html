Why start from memory

Rereading your notes feels like learning because the words look familiar. But in the exam nothing is in front of you. Pulling an idea out of your memory, even slowly and with gaps, makes it much easier to pull out next time.

So today every part starts with a blank page: you write what you remember, then you check. The gaps you find are not a failure; they show exactly what to learn this week.

One paragraph is not a whole Paper 1 answer

In the exam you write a full answer on each text: an introduction, several paragraphs and a conclusion. The examiner marks the whole answer with four criteria, A to D, 5 marks each.

Today you write one paragraph, so you cannot give it a real mark. Instead you use the IB's own words for each level as a mirror: which description sounds most like your paragraph?

Artificial intelligence in a few lines

Artificial intelligence (AI) means computer programs that do things we used to think only people could do: write essays, make pictures, hold a conversation. In 2026 many people use it every day.

People also argue about it: it could be used to cheat, to spread lies or to harm, and it needs huge buildings full of computers (datacentres) that use a lot of electricity and water. Today's cartoon comments on that debate.

French: a clause or a noun group

A clause has a subject and a conjugated verb: ils regardent leur téléphone (they look at their phone).

A noun group is a noun with its little words, and no verb: les écrans, l'arrivée des smartphones.

Some French connectors must be followed by a clause, others by a noun group. Choosing the wrong one is the most common connector mistake.

Still stuck on a word? Do not search for long. Write it in the Error log tab of the study sheet starting with "word:" (for example "word: composition") and carry on. It will be explained in the next guide.

Graphic Novels Sessions 4 and 5 · moved from 20 November

Panels, gutters and closure

A comic controls time with boxes and gaps, and lets the reader fill the gaps.

By the end of this section I can

Your Session 5 slide quotes the comics writer Scott McCloud: closure is "observing the parts but perceiving the whole". Learn word for word Panels show separate moments; the reader's mind joins them into one story across the gutter, the gap between panels. Time passes in the gutter, and what is never drawn there, the reader imagines.

The six panel transitions (McCloud, from class) Learn word for word

TransitionWhat changes between the panelsUsual effect
Moment-to-momentAlmost nothing: the same thing a second laterSlow motion, suspense
Action-to-actionOne subject, the next actionSteady story; the most common
Subject-to-subjectAnother person or thing, same sceneShows reactions; widens the view
Scene-to-sceneA jump across time or placeThe reader fills a big gap
Aspect-to-aspectDifferent parts of one place or moodTime seems to stand still
Non-sequiturNo logical linkThe reader still searches for one

Pace Understand and apply

  • Panel size: a big or wide panel makes a moment feel longer and more important; small panels feel quick.
  • Silent panel (no words): a pause; the reader stops and looks.
  • Grid: a regular grid gives a steady rhythm; breaking it draws attention.
Worked example. A made-up sequence. Panel 1: close-up of a girl's hand holding a phone; the screen says "Exam results: 9:00". Panel 2: the same hand, the screen now says 9:01, the thumb hovering. Panel 3: a wide, silent panel of her family round the kitchen table, all staring at her.
  1. Spot it: panels 1 to 2 change by one minute and one thumb: moment-to-moment. Panel 2 to 3 shows other people in the same scene: subject-to-subject.
  2. Explain it: the slow first transition stretches one minute over two panels; the third panel is the widest and has no words.
  3. Effect: the reader waits with her, then feels the silent pressure of the family. We never see the result: closure makes us imagine it.
  4. Purpose: shows how heavy family expectations feel to a teenager.
Why this step? Why name the transition before the effect?

The name is only the "spot it" and "explain it" links. Marks come from step 3: what the gap makes the reader do and feel. Naming first stops you guessing the effect without evidence.

Finish it. A made-up pair. Panel 1: a boy on a bridge drops a paper boat into a river. Panel 2: an extreme long shot of a tiny paper boat alone on a wide grey sea.
  1. Transition: a jump across time and place:  
  2. Effect on the reader:  
  3. Purpose:  
Answer

Scene-to-scene. Effect: the reader imagines the whole journey in the gutter, so the boat seems to have travelled impossibly far; the extreme long shot makes it small and alone. Purpose: for example, how small hopes or children travel into a huge, uncaring world. Any purpose that comes from these two panels is right.

Exam traps. (1) Moment-to-moment is almost no change; action-to-action is the next action. (2) Aspect-to-aspect stays in one place and mood; scene-to-scene jumps. (3) Transition names are class terms: the examiner rewards what the transition does to the reader, not the label.
Self-check: write two key words for each, then open
  1. Panel 1: a rainy window. Panel 2: an empty chair. Panel 3: a cold cup of tea. Which transition, and what does it do?
  2. Where in a comic does "missing" time pass?
  3. Why can a wide panel with no words slow the reader down?

Answers. 1: aspect-to-aspect; time stands still and the reader feels the mood (loneliness, absence). 2: in the gutter. 3: there is more to look at and nothing tells you to move on, so the moment lasts longer.

Your turn: an exam-style question

Made-up sequence. Panel 1: close-up of a mother's face, eyes on the front door. Panel 2: extreme close-up of the door handle starting to turn. Panel 3: a completely black panel, no words.
Question. Analyse how the sequence uses panel transitions and closure to create tension. [3]
Model answer (open after writing yours)

The sequence moves subject-to-subject, from the mother's watching face to the turning handle, so the reader sees both the fear and its cause [1]. The black, silent final panel shows nothing at all: through closure the reader must imagine who comes through the door [1]. Because the answer is withheld, the tension is left with the reader, which makes the threat feel larger than any drawing of it could [1].

Mark: 1 for a correctly named transition with what it shows; 1 for what the reader fills in through closure; 1 for the effect (tension) linked to the choice.

B: Analysis and evaluation

Level 3: "a generally appropriate analysis of textual features and/or authorial choices"

Level 5: "an insightful and convincing analysis"; "a very good evaluation of how such features and/or choices shape meaning"

My level: ☐ 1 ☐ 2 ☐ 3 ☐ 4 ☐ 5

C: Focus and organisation

Level 3: "adequately organized in a generally coherent manner"; "some focus"

My level: ☐ 1 ☐ 2 ☐ 3 ☐ 4 ☐ 5

Level 3: "clear and carefully chosen with an adequate degree of accuracy"; register "mostly appropriate"

Model paragraph on the cave cartoon (open after marking yours)
Steve Jones uses an analogy between the discovery of fire and today's artificial intelligence to suggest, with gentle humour, that every powerful invention brings fear of its misuse. [claim] Two cave dwellers crouch over a small campfire, the only warm yellow in a cold blue-grey cave, so the reader's eye goes straight to the flames as the source of both comfort and danger. [visual + colour, effect] The caption, presumably spoken by the man, "Imagine if this technology fell into the wrong hands...", borrows the modern language of tech warnings and places it in a Stone Age mouth, while his own hands are raised to the fire. [word and image together] This anachronism makes the reader laugh, yet the ellipsis leaves the sentence hanging, and the woman's stick, already resting in the flames, hints at how easily a flame becomes a torch or a weapon. [less obvious detail] The cartoon therefore invites readers to see AI as the newest chapter in a very old story, in which the fear of misuse is as old as invention itself. [purpose]

Why it reaches the top levels. A: it reads the implied message (AI) and a subtlety (the stick). B: every detail is followed by an effect, image and words are read together, and "with gentle humour" judges how it works. C: the claim answers the guiding question and the last sentence returns to it. D: precise terms (analogy, anachronism, ellipsis), effect verbs, formal register. 165 words.

Another fair reading: the cartoon laughs at people who panic about every new invention, since fire turned out to be useful. Either reading is right if the details support it: that is what criterion A rewards.

Notes on the backup cartoon (datacentre)

The nine cause connectors (18 Sep sheet) Learn word for word

+ clause (subject + verb)+ noun group
parce que (because), comme (as, starts the sentence), puisque (since, the reader already knows), étant donné que (given that), du fait que (owing to the fact that), sous prétexte que (claiming that, the reason is doubtful)grâce à (thanks to, good result), à cause de (because of, bad result), en raison de (owing to, neutral and formal)
Worked example: one idea, two structures.
  1. Clause: Beaucoup d'élèves dorment mal parce qu'ils regardent leur téléphone tard le soir.
  2. Noun: Beaucoup d'élèves dorment mal à cause des écrans le soir. (à cause de + les écrans = des écrans)
Why this step? Why did "que" become "qu'" and "de les" become "des"?

Before a vowel, que drops its e (qu'ils). De + les always joins into des, and à + les into aux (grâce aux activités).

Finish it. Fill the gaps with a connector from the table.
  1. Les élèves ne se parlent plus   leur téléphone.
  2.   club d'échecs, Lina a rencontré de nouveaux amis.
  3. Je sors pendant la pause   j'ai besoin de bouger.
Answer

1: à cause de (bad result, + noun). 2: Grâce au (good result; à + le = au). 3: parce que (+ clause).

Trap. Parce que + noun is the most common mistake: "parce que le téléphone" is wrong; write "à cause du téléphone" or "parce que le téléphone nous distrait".

Answers. 1: Comme. 2: sous prétexte qu'. 3: En raison (en raison de + le nombre = du nombre).
