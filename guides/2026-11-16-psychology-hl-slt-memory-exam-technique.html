How Paper 1 answers are marked

Psychology exams have no multiple choice. You write answers, and examiners mark them with markbands: short descriptions of what an answer at each level looks like. For a 4-mark question there are two bands, 1-2 marks and 3-4 marks.

The first word of the question, the command term, says what kind of answer gets the top band. Describe means give a detailed account. Explain means a detailed account plus reasons or causes. Answering "describe" when the question says "explain" keeps you in the lower band, even if every sentence is true.

Two kinds of learning you met in session 2

Classical conditioning: learning by linking two things that happen together. Pavlov's dogs drooled at a sound that always came just before food.

Operant conditioning: learning from what happens after you do something. A behaviour that is rewarded (reinforced) happens more; a behaviour that is punished happens less.

In both, the learner experiences things directly. Today's social learning theory adds a third way: learning by watching other people and what happens to them.

What a theory and a model are

A theory is an explanation of why or how something happens, which can be checked against evidence.

A model is a simplified picture of how something works, often drawn as boxes and arrows, like a map of the underground: it leaves out details so you can see the main route.

Neither is ever "proven". Researchers use them to make predictions ("if this is true, then people should forget a word within 20 seconds"), then run studies to see whether the predictions come true. Studies that fit support the model; studies that do not fit show it needs changing.

Memory in everyday words: in, kept, out

Every memory has three steps:

  • In: you notice something and take it in, for example a new classmate's name.
  • Kept: it is stored, for a few seconds or for years.
  • Out: you get it back when you need it, for example when you see her the next day. Getting it back is called retrieval.

Forgetting can happen at any step: you never really noticed, it was not kept, or you cannot find it when you need it (the name is "on the tip of your tongue").

Averages and putting numbers in order

An average is one number that sums up a whole set of numbers. Psychology uses three:

  • Mean: add them all and divide by how many. Scores 4, 6 and 8: 18 ÷ 3 = 6.
  • Median: write them in order from smallest to largest and take the middle one. For 3, 9, 5 that is 3, 5, 9, so the median is 5.
  • Mode: the one that appears most often. In 2, 3, 3, 7 the mode is 3.

Always sort the numbers first when you need the median.

Percentages and milliseconds

A percentage is "out of 100". 80% recalled means 80 out of every 100 items were remembered; under 10% means fewer than 10 out of 100.

A millisecond (ms) is one thousandth of a second. Reaction times are often around 400 to 500 ms: just under half a second. 1 240 ms is about one and a quarter seconds, which is very slow for a simple reaction task.

Worked example 1: a full PEEL 4-marker. "Describe operant conditioning with reference to one example. (4 marks)" Understand and apply
  1. Point. Operant conditioning is learning in which a behaviour becomes more or less likely because of its consequences: reinforcement makes it more likely and punishment makes it less likely.
  2. Evidence. A hypothetical example: a teacher gives a student a merit point every time she hands in her homework on time. Before this she was late about half the time; after a few weeks she is on time every week.
  3. Explain. Handing in homework on time is the behaviour. The merit point is a pleasant consequence added straight after it, so it is positive reinforcement. Because the behaviour is followed by the reward every time, the student links being on time with the merit point, and the behaviour becomes more frequent. If the merit points stopped, the behaviour could fade again (extinction).
  4. Link. So operant conditioning explains learning as a change in behaviour shaped by what follows it.
Why this step? Why does the Explain part name "positive reinforcement" and say what was added?

Because the 3-4 band needs the example to be explained, not only relevant. Naming each part of the example with its theory term (behaviour, consequence, positive reinforcement) is what turns a story into an explanation.

Finish it. "Describe classical conditioning with reference to one example. (4 marks)" The Point and Evidence are done. On paper, write the Explain and the Link using the starters.
  1. Point (done). Classical conditioning is learning by association: a neutral stimulus (NS) is repeatedly paired with an unconditioned stimulus (UCS), which causes a natural, unlearned response (UCR), until the NS alone causes a learned, conditioned response (CR).
  2. Evidence (done). Pavlov's dogs salivated when they were given food. He sounded a metronome just before giving them food, many times. Later the dogs salivated at the metronome alone.
  3. Explain (you). At first the metronome was a stimulus because . Food was the and salivating at food was the . After repeated pairing the metronome became the ...
  4. Link (you). Because Pavlov used dogs, (an animal-human link, as EX01 asks) ...
Show the answer

Explain: At first the metronome was a neutral stimulus because on its own it caused no salivation. Food was the unconditioned stimulus and salivating at food the unconditioned response, as no learning was needed. After repeated pairing the metronome became a conditioned stimulus, and salivating at it is a conditioned response, learned through association. Link: Because Pavlov used dogs, we must link it to humans: a child who has a painful injection may later feel fear at the sight of the nurse's uniform. Classical conditioning shows that learning can happen through association alone, without any reward.

Marking checklist for your timed classical conditioning answer

  • Point: learning by pairing a neutral stimulus with an unconditioned stimulus.
  • All five terms used correctly: NS, UCS, UCR, CS, CR. LC02: the examiner looks for "An example that is explained with regard to the neutral stimulus, unconditioned stimulus, and conditioned stimulus".
  • Example explained: each part of it named as NS, UCS, CS and so on.
  • Animal study? One sentence linking it to humans (EX01).
  • Link sentence; one example only; no evaluation.

All six ticked: 3-4 band. Example there but not explained term by term: 1-2 band.

Exam traps. (1) Naming a study is not explaining it: "Pavlov showed this with dogs" is a relevant example that is "not explained", so 1-2 marks at most. (2) Only your first example is credited (specimen markscheme), so a second example wastes time. (3) A 4-marker that runs to 15 minutes steals time from the essay.

The core idea

Your slides (LC09) put it in one line: "Humans learn not only through personal experience but also by observing models." Learn word for word (class definition)

  • Observational learning: learning by watching a model (a parent, friend, film character, influencer).
  • Vicarious reinforcement: "By watching the rewards and punishments others receive, we decide whether to imitate their behavior" (LC09). Vicarious means "through someone else"; seeing a model punished makes copying less likely.

In operant conditioning (session 2) the learner's own behaviour is rewarded; here the learner only watches.

Learning is not the same as performing. A child can learn a behaviour by watching and only do it weeks later, when there is a reason (EX03, question 10).

The four processes

Who gets copied? (LC09) Models we identify with (same age or gender), models we like, models who behave consistently, and models whose behaviour is rewarded.

The specimen markscheme (Paper 1, question 4) credits exactly these: vicarious reinforcement, self-efficacy, identification, the four processes, and liking the model.

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy: "the expectation of personal success in a task, based on the view that one is in control of one's actions and abilities" (LC09, class definition). In plain words: believing you can do it, which affects reproduction. Its four sources (LC09):

  • Mastery experiences: your own past successes (failures lower it).
  • Vicarious experiences: seeing someone like you succeed.
  • Social persuasion: being told you can do it.
  • Emotional and physical states: a good mood raises it, a low mood lowers it.

A study you can use as your example

Details are not marked in Paper 1 (p45): know which part of the theory it shows.

Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961): Bobo doll

72 children aged 3 to 6 watched an adult attack an inflatable Bobo doll, or a calm adult, or no adult. Those who saw the aggressive model copied the same hitting and words more than the others. Boys copied physical aggression more, especially from a male model.

Shows: observational learning (no reward to the child) and identification (same-sex model copied more).

Extra, not needed today

Bandura later widened social learning theory into "social cognitive theory", with the agentic perspective and (triadic) reciprocal determinism (LC09, LC10, LC11). The official guide names only social learning theory and the specimen markscheme does not ask for these: not needed for the IB exam. Self-efficacy is the one idea from that part you do need.

Coates et al. (2019), in LC09, is a second study: children who saw an influencer with unhealthy snacks ate more, useful for health questions. Social learning theory also comes back in two later contexts: "The role of social learning theory in understanding one or more health problems" (health and well-being, p29) and "The application of social learning theory to explain and change group behaviour(s)" (human relationships, p32).

Worked example 2: a full 6-mark application answer (IEA). "A primary school wants more children to eat the vegetables served at lunch. Explain how social learning theory could be used to achieve this goal. (6 marks)" For 6-markers EX01 uses IEA, two or three times: Identify a principle, Explain how it works, Apply it to the situation. Understand and apply
  1. Introduction. Social learning theory (Bandura) says people learn behaviour by observing models and are more likely to copy behaviour they see rewarded.
  2. I: attention and identification. E: children pay more attention to models they see as similar and like, and copy them more.   3. I: vicarious reinforcement. E: when observers see a model rewarded, their motivation to copy rises, even though they were not rewarded themselves. The multi-store model (Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1968)

Look at the picture at the top of the page. Memory is three separate stores, and information flows through them in one direction:

StoreCapacity (how much)Duration (how long)Gets there by
Sensory memoryvery large: everything the senses pick upabout a second or less for what you seeinput from the senses
Short-term memory (STM)7 ± 2 items (Miller, 1956); newer research suggests about 4 chunksabout 15-30 seconds without rehearsalattention
Long-term memory (LTM)no known limitdays to a lifetimerehearsal
  • Maintenance rehearsal: repeating something to keep it in STM, like saying a phone number over and over. In the model, rehearsal also passes information to LTM.
  • Chunking: grouping items so STM holds more. MTVCNNABCFOX is 12 letters; MTV CNN ABC FOX is 4 chunks (LC13).
  • Information leaves STM by decay (it fades) or displacement (new items push old ones out).

Evidence (LC13). Peterson and Peterson (1959): see worked example 3. The serial position effect (Glanzer and Cunitz, 1966): from a word list people recall the first words (rehearsed into LTM) and the last words (still in STM) better than the middle: two effects, two stores.

Worked example 3: a full PEEL 4-marker. "Explain one cognitive model with reference to one example. (4 marks)" Understand and apply
  1. Point. The multi-store model (Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1968) explains memory as three separate stores: sensory memory, short-term memory (STM) and long-term memory (LTM). Attention moves information into STM, and rehearsal moves it from STM into LTM.
  2. Evidence. Peterson and Peterson (1959) gave participants three-consonant trigrams, then made them count backwards in threes before recalling them after 3 to 18 seconds. Recall fell from about 80% after 3 seconds to under 10% after 18 seconds.
  3. Explain. Counting backwards stopped maintenance rehearsal, so the trigrams could not be kept in STM or passed on to LTM, and they were lost within seconds. This is what the model predicts: STM has a limited duration, and without rehearsal information never reaches LTM, a separate store where it could last for years.
  4. Link. So the model explains why we forget a new phone number seconds after hearing it unless we keep repeating it.
Why this step? Why does the Explain part start with what the counting did?

The counting task is the part of the example that connects to the theory: it removed rehearsal. Saying that first lets you tie the result (fast forgetting) to two parts of the model, limited STM duration and rehearsal as the route to LTM. That is what "the example is relevant and explained" (p45) means.

Levels of processing (Craik and Lockhart, 1972)

This model says what matters is how deeply you process information when you first meet it, not how many stores there are or how often you repeat it (LC13):

DepthTypeYou think aboutCraik and Tulving question
ShallowStructuralwhat it looks like"Is the word in capital letters?"
Phoneticwhat it sounds like"Does the word rhyme with 'weight'?"

The value of cognitive models

The specimen markscheme for Paper 1 question 3 gives the general points: models are "tested in empirical research studies"; "different studies are required to test different parts of the model"; and "Models are based on constructs that must be measured; it is important that these constructs are well operationalized."

Worked example 4: the value of the multi-store model. Understand and apply
  1. It makes testable predictions. If STM is a separate store with a short duration, blocking rehearsal should make information vanish within seconds. Peterson and Peterson tested exactly that, and it did.
  2. Different studies test different parts: STM duration (Peterson and Peterson), STM capacity (Miller), two stores (serial position effect).
  3. Constructs are operationalised. "Short-term memory" cannot be seen, so it is measured as the number of trigrams recalled correctly after a set number of seconds.
  4. Models are revised. Craik and Tulving showed that the type of processing matters, which the multi-store model ignores, so the model was challenged and improved: models change with evidence (LC14).
Why this step? Why is point 4 (a weakness) part of the model's value?

Because a model is valuable precisely when it can be tested and shown to be incomplete. A model that no study could challenge would explain nothing. The specimen markscheme also rewards saying what a study did not test.

HL: levels of measurement and choosing the right average

Before you calculate an average, ask: what kind of data is it, and what shape is it?

The official line (Table 3.4, p27; assessed at HL only, in Paper 3) Learn word for word: "Descriptive statistics: Analyse and interpret results of typical measures of central tendency (mean, median, mode) and measures of dispersion (range, standard deviation, semi-interquartile range)." Today: the three measures of central tendency (kinds of average); dispersion comes later. The four levels of measurement are class content (HL01), not named in the guide.

A true zero means "none of it": 0 ms reaction time is no time at all (ratio), but 0 °C is not "no temperature" (interval).

  • Mean: add all values, divide by how many. Uses every value, so one extreme value pulls it a long way.
  • Median: put the values in order, take the middle one. With an even number of values, the mean of the middle two.
  • Mode: the most frequent value or category (there may be none).

Skewness (guide glossary, p67-68): "The degree to which the distribution of data in a dataset is asymmetrical, as shown when graphed. A positively skewed distribution has a longer tail on the right side, while a negatively skewed distribution has a longer tail on the left side."

Worked example 5: one slow student. Reaction times (ms) of 8 students: 412, 398, 455, 430, 1 240, 405, 441, 420. Which average is typical? Understand and apply
  1. Level: reaction time has a true zero, so it is ratio data.
  2. Put in order: 398, 405, 412, 420, 430, 441, 455, 1 240.
  3. Mean: 4 201 ÷ 8 = 525.125, about 525 ms.
  4. Median: 8 values, so the mean of the 4th and 5th: (420 + 430) ÷ 2 = 425 ms. Mode: none, all values differ.
  5. Shape: 1 240 is far above the rest, an outlier, so the data are positively skewed; 7 of the 8 students were faster than the mean.
  6. Conclusion, Paper 3 style: "The median (425 ms) is the better measure of central tendency, because the outlier of 1 240 ms pulls the mean up to 525 ms, above 7 of the 8 values."
Why this step? Why check the shape (step 5) when ratio data "usually" use the mean?

Because the level tells you which averages are allowed, and the shape tells you which is fair. Ratio data allow the mean, but one outlier makes it untypical (HL01: "Check the level first, then check the shape of the graph"). Without the outlier the mean would be 423 ms, almost the same as the median.

8. Explain how social learning theory can account for the development of aggressive behaviour in children. (4 marks, 10 minutes)

Show model answer and checklist

Model answer (two developed points). Social learning theory says children learn aggression through observational learning: they attend to a model's aggressive behaviour and retain it, with no reward needed. In Bandura, Ross and Ross (1961), children aged 3 to 6 who watched an adult hit a Bobo doll later copied the same hits and words more than children who saw a calm model or none. This shows new aggressive acts were learned just by watching. Children also copy models they identify with: boys copied physical aggression more after a male model, so aggression is more likely to develop when children see it in people similar to them.

9. Describe the levels of processing model of memory with reference to one example. (4 marks, 6 minutes)

Show model answer and checklist

Model answer. The levels of processing model (Craik and Lockhart, 1972) says memory depends on how deeply information is processed when it is first met: shallow processing (structural, what it looks like, or phonetic, what it sounds like) gives weak memories, and deep semantic processing (what it means) gives strong ones. Craik and Tulving (1975) asked participants questions about words without warning them of a test: "Is it in capital letters?" (structural), "Does it rhyme with weight?" (phonetic) or "Is it a type of fish?" (semantic). In a later recognition test about 83% of semantic words were recognised but only about 16% of structural words. The semantic question made participants think about meaning, which is deep processing, so those words were remembered best. This shows that how we process information, not just repeating it, decides how well we remember it.

Checklist (Section A markband, p45): shallow and deep named with their types; one example; the example explained (which question is which level, and why semantic words were remembered best); a link. Example missing or not explained: 1-2 marks.
