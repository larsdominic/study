Atoms, electrons and covalent bonds

Everything is made of tiny atoms. Each atom has a small centre with electrons (tiny negative particles) around it. The electrons in the outermost layer, the outer shell, take part in bonding.

In a covalent bond two atoms share a pair of electrons. It is strong. A single bond (C-C) shares one pair; a double bond (C=C) shares two pairs.

Each kind of atom makes a fixed number of covalent bonds: hydrogen 1, oxygen 2, nitrogen 3, carbon 4. A double bond counts as two of them.

Reading a formula and counting atoms

A formula lists the atoms in one molecule. The small low number after a symbol counts that atom: C6H12O6 (glucose) has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 6 oxygen atoms. H2O (water) has 2 hydrogen and 1 oxygen.

Groups of atoms have short names: -OH is an oxygen joined to a hydrogen (a hydroxyl group); -COOH is an acid group; CH2OH is a carbon with two hydrogens and an -OH.

In ring diagrams, the corners of the ring are atoms (usually carbon), and the lines are covalent bonds.

Polar, non-polar and dissolving (from A1.1)

In some bonds the electrons are shared unevenly, so one end is slightly negative and the other slightly positive: the bond is polar. -OH groups are polar. Bonds between carbon and hydrogen are shared evenly: non-polar.

A hydrogen bond is a weak attraction between a slightly positive H and a slightly negative O (or N) nearby.

"Like dissolves like": water is polar, so it dissolves polar things (sugars, with many -OH groups) but not non-polar ones (oils, which are mostly C and H).

Cells, membranes and osmosis

Every cell is surrounded by a thin membrane that controls what goes in and out. Plant cells also have a stiff cell wall outside the membrane.

Osmosis is water moving across a membrane towards the side with more dissolved substances. So if a cell fills up with dissolved sugar, water rushes in and the cell swells (an animal cell can burst). Big molecules that do not dissolve do not cause this.

Energy from food: respiration

Cells release energy from food in respiration: glucose (or fat) is broken down with oxygen, giving carbon dioxide and water. Energy is measured in joules (J); 1 kJ = 1 000 J. "Oxidised" here means broken down with oxygen in respiration.

Building big molecules needs energy; cells get it from a small molecule called ATP, made in respiration.

Percentage change

Percentage change = (new value − starting value) ÷ starting value × 100.

Example: 50 grows to 60. Change 10; 10 ÷ 50 = 0.2; × 100 = a 20% increase. Always divide by the value you started with (usually the earlier one).

B1.1.1 to B1.1.4 · SL and HL

Carbon, building and breaking, simple sugars

Life builds its big molecules from small units, joined and split by two opposite reactions.

By the end of this section (the guide's statement titles in bold)

B1.1.1 Why carbon?

A carbon atom has four electrons in its outer shell, so it forms four covalent bonds (each bond is a shared pair of electrons).Learn The four can be four single bonds, or single bonds plus a double bond (a double bond uses two of the four). Carbon bonds to other carbon atoms and to other non-metals: mainly hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, sometimes phosphorus and sulfur. Covalent bonds are strong, so carbon compounds are stable.

Because carbon atoms join to each other, they can make unbranched chains (fatty acid tails), branched chains, single rings (glucose) and several rings joined together (steroids). That variety of shapes is why carbon compounds are the basis of life.

For context: scientists everywhere use the same unit prefixes (kilo, centi, milli, micro, nano) because they agreed on them internationally.

B1.1.2 and B1.1.3 Condensation and hydrolysis

A monomer is one small unit; a polymer (a macromolecule) is a long chain of monomers.

MonomerPolymerBond made
monosaccharide (e.g. glucose)polysaccharide (e.g. starch)glycosidic bond
amino acidpolypeptide (protein)peptide bond
nucleotidenucleic acid (DNA, RNA)covalent sugar-phosphate bond

Condensation: two molecules are joined by a new covalent bond, and a molecule of water is released. An -OH from one molecule and an -H from the other leave as H2O.Learn Building a polymer needs energy (in cells it comes from ATP). Fats are also made this way (ester bonds), but they are not polymers.

Hydrolysis: water is used to break a covalent bond, splitting a polymer into monomers. The water molecule is split: its -H goes to one monomer and its -OH to the other ("hydro" means water, "lysis" means splitting).Learn Digestion in the gut is hydrolysis, sped up by enzymes: starch to glucose, proteins to amino acids.

Worked example: formula of a condensation product. Two glucose molecules (C6H12O6) join to make maltose, a disaccharide (two sugar units). What is the formula of maltose?
  1. Add up the atoms of both molecules: C 6 + 6 = 12, H 12 + 12 = 24, O 6 + 6 = 12, so C12H24O12.
  2. One bond is made, so one H2O is lost: take away 2 H and 1 O.
  3. Maltose is C12H22O11.
Why this step? (step 2)

Every condensation releases one water molecule. A chain of n monomers has n − 1 bonds, so it loses n − 1 waters.

Finish it. Five glucose molecules join in a chain. Total atoms: C 30, H 60, O 30. Number of bonds = 4, so 4 H2O are lost: H 60 − 8 = ?, O 30 − 4 = ?. Write the formula.
Answer

C30H52O26.

Exam trap. Count the bonds, not the molecules: three monomers need two condensations, so two waters. Hydrolysis is the reverse: it adds water, it does not release it.

B1.1.4 Monosaccharides

A monosaccharide is a single sugar unit. Most have 5 carbons (pentoses, C5H10O5, for example ribose) or 6 carbons (hexoses, C6H12O6, for example glucose and fructose). In cells they are mostly rings: carbons plus one oxygen atom in the ring, with -OH and -H groups above and below it.Learn

To recognise one in a diagram, count every carbon atom, in the ring and in the side chains: 5 means pentose, 6 means hexose. Do not just count the corners of the ring.

Draw glucose like this: a ring of five carbons and one oxygen, carbons numbered 1 to 6 clockwise from the right, carbon 6 in the CH2OH group. The only difference: on carbon 1, the -OH is below the ring in α-glucose and above it in β-glucose.Learn Going round from carbon 1, α-glucose has its -OH groups down, down, up, down.

Two pentoses you met in A1.2: ribose (in RNA) and deoxyribose (in DNA). Deoxyribose has H instead of OH on carbon 2.

Glucose: properties and uses.Learn

PropertySo glucose is used
soluble in water (its -OH groups form hydrogen bonds with water)transported dissolved in blood plasma
easy to transport (small and soluble)carried to every cell that needs it
chemically stablekept in cells, or stored after joining into starch or glycogen

Worked example: pentose or hexose? A ring diagram shows a ring of four carbons and one oxygen, with a CH2OH group on two of the ring carbons.
  1. Carbons in the ring: 4.
  2. Carbons in side chains: each CH2OH has one, so 2.
  3. Total 6, so it is a hexose, even though the ring has only five corners (this one is fructose).
Why this step? (step 2)

A hexose is defined by its six carbon atoms, wherever they are. Side-chain carbons count too.

Finish it. You can draw α-glucose. To draw β-glucose, copy α-glucose and change one thing on one carbon. Which carbon, and what do you change?
Answer

Carbon 1: swap its -H and -OH, so the -OH points up, above the ring.

Exam trap. When asked to identify a sugar, be specific: "α-glucose", not just "glucose". In a drawing, copy the ring shape you are given and put every -H and -OH on the correct side.
Self-check A (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. How many water molecules are released when 10 amino acids join into one chain?
  2. Which reaction digests starch in the gut, and what does it use?
  3. Why can carbon form branched chains?
  4. Which carbon is different in α-glucose and β-glucose?

Answers. 1. Nine (9 peptide bonds, one water each). 2. Hydrolysis; it uses water (and enzymes). 3. It forms four bonds, so one carbon can bond to three or four other carbons. 4. Carbon 1: -OH below the ring in α, above in β.

B1.1.5 to B1.1.7 · SL and HL

Polysaccharides and glycoproteins

Same monomer, different jobs: the type of glucose and the way the chain is built decide what a polysaccharide is for.

By the end of this section (the guide's statement titles in bold)

B1.1.5 Starch and glycogen: energy stores

Both are polymers of α-glucose.Learn

  • Starch (plants) is a mixture of two molecules. Amylose: α-glucose joined by 1,4 glycosidic bonds (carbon 1 of one glucose to carbon 4 of the next), unbranched, coiled into a helix. Amylopectin: 1,4 bonds plus some 1,6 bonds, which start side branches.
  • Glycogen (animals, stored in liver and muscle cells): like amylopectin but much more branched.

Why they are good stores (each feature, SO its benefit):Learn

  • Coiled and branched, so compact: a lot of glucose fits in a small space.
  • Very large molecules, so insoluble: they do not dissolve, so they do not draw water into the cell by osmosis.
  • Glucose is easily added or removed at the chain ends (condensation to store, hydrolysis to release). Branches give many ends, so glycogen can release glucose fast when an animal needs energy.

B1.1.6 Cellulose: a structural polysaccharide

Cellulose is made of β-glucose joined by 1,4 bonds. Because the -OH on carbon 1 is above the ring, each glucose is flipped compared with the one next to it (alternating orientation). That keeps the chain straight, not coiled.Learn Straight chains lie side by side and form many hydrogen bonds between their -OH groups, making bundles (microfibrils). These are very strong when pulled (high tensile strength), so the cell wall stops a plant cell bursting when it takes in water.

Worked example: a 3-mark "explain" answer. "Explain how the structure of cellulose is related to its function in plant cell walls." One marking point per line:
  1. Cellulose is made of β-glucose, with each glucose flipped relative to its neighbour, SO the chains are straight.
  2. Straight chains lie parallel, SO many hydrogen bonds form between neighbouring chains, making bundles (microfibrils).
  3. The bundles have high tensile strength, SO the cell wall resists the pressure of water and stops the cell bursting.
Why this step? (the word SO)

In an "explain" question a feature alone scores nothing. The mark comes from linking the feature to what it does. "SO" forces that link.

Finish it. "Explain why a potato cell that stores glucose as starch does not swell up by osmosis. [2]" First half: "Starch is a very large molecule, SO it is ..."
Answer

... insoluble; SO it does not change the water concentration inside the cell and does not draw water in by osmosis (dissolved glucose would).

Exam trap. Amylose is unbranched (coiled); amylopectin and glycogen are branched. Cellulose is never branched or coiled. "Insoluble" or "compact" alone get no mark in an "explain" question: add what it does.

B1.1.7 Glycoproteins and cell–cell recognition

A glycoprotein is a protein in the cell membrane with a short carbohydrate chain attached, sticking out of the outside surface of the cell.Learn Other cells and the immune system recognise the shape of these chains: this is cell–cell recognition, and it is how the body tells its own cells ("self") from foreign ones.

Example: ABO blood groups. Red blood cells carry these chains as antigens. Group O has the basic chain only. Group A has one extra sugar added to it; group B has a different extra sugar; group AB has both. If someone receives blood with an antigen their own cells do not have, their antibodies bind to it and the red cells clump together.

Self-check B (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. Which of starch, glycogen and cellulose is the most branched?
  2. Which form of glucose is in cellulose, and which in starch?
  3. What holds cellulose chains together in bundles?
  4. How does the antigen of blood group O differ from group A?

Answers. 1. Glycogen. 2. β-glucose in cellulose, α-glucose in starch. 3. Hydrogen bonds between neighbouring chains. 4. O has the basic chain only; A has one extra sugar on it.

B1.1.8 to B1.1.10 · SL and HL

Lipids: triglycerides, phospholipids and fatty acids

Lipids are defined by one property: they do not mix with water.

By the end of this section (the guide's statement titles in bold)

B1.1.8 Lipids are hydrophobic

Lipids dissolve in non-polar solvents (such as hexane, a petrol-like liquid) but only very slightly in water.Learn They are mostly carbon and hydrogen, with few oxygen atoms and so few polar groups to form hydrogen bonds with water. The main groups: fats (solid at room temperature, about 20 °C), oils (liquid at room temperature), waxes and steroids.

B1.1.9 Building triglycerides and phospholipids

Glycerol is a small molecule with three -OH groups. A fatty acid is a long hydrocarbon chain (only C and H) with an acid group, -COOH, at one end. Condensation between an -OH of glycerol and the -COOH of a fatty acid makes an ester bond and releases one water.Learn

  • Triglyceride: glycerol + three fatty acids: three condensations, three ester bonds, three waters released.
  • Phospholipid: glycerol + two fatty acids + one phosphate group in place of the third fatty acid.
Finish it. Glycerol is C3H8O3; palmitic acid is C16H32O2. One glycerol and three palmitic acids make a triglyceride. Total atoms: C 3 + 48 = 51, H 8 + 96 = 104, O 3 + 6 = 9. Three waters are lost: H 104 − 6 = ?, O 9 − 3 = ?.
Answer

C51H98O6.

B1.1.10 Saturated and unsaturated fatty acids

  • Saturated: no C=C double bonds; every carbon holds as many hydrogen atoms as it can. Straight chain.Learn
  • Monounsaturated: exactly one C=C double bond: one kink.
  • Polyunsaturated: two or more C=C double bonds.

Melting point. Straight saturated chains pack closely together, so more energy is needed to separate them: higher melting point. Kinks stop unsaturated chains packing closely: lower melting point. So the more C=C bonds, the lower the melting point.Learn For example, stearic acid (18 carbons, no C=C) melts at about 69 °C, oleic acid (18 carbons, one C=C) at about 13 °C (textbook values, not from your class files; only an illustration, not to learn).

Plants and endotherms. Plants store energy mainly as oils, rich in unsaturated fatty acids; they stay liquid even when the plant is cold (as in seeds). Endotherms (birds and mammals, which keep their body warm, near 37 °C in humans) store more fats, richer in saturated fatty acids: these are solid at room temperature but soft at body temperature.

For context: "trans fats" in pack question 11 are unsaturated fatty acids with straight chains that behave like saturated ones. Their chemistry is not needed for the IB exam.

Worked example: percentage change (data questions ask this often). A seed contains 4.8 g of oil. After 6 days of growing it contains 1.2 g. Calculate the percentage decrease. (Invented numbers for practice.)
  1. Change: 4.8 − 1.2 = 3.6 g.
  2. Divide by the starting value: 3.6 ÷ 4.8 = 0.75.
  3. Multiply by 100: 75% decrease.
Why this step? (step 2)

A percentage change is always measured against where you started. Dividing by the final value (1.2) would give 300%, which is wrong.

Finish it. The blubber of a seal is 40 mm thick in autumn and 52 mm thick in winter. Change: 52 − 40 = 12 mm. Divide by the starting value and multiply by 100.
Answer

12 ÷ 40 × 100 = 30% increase.

Exam trap. The bond in fats is an ester bond (not glycosidic, not peptide). A phospholipid has two fatty acids, not three. "Monounsaturated" means one C=C double bond, not "one hydrogen missing".
Self-check C (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. Name the bond between glycerol and a fatty acid.
  2. How many water molecules are released when one phospholipid forms from glycerol, two fatty acids and a phosphate?
  3. A fatty acid has three C=C bonds. What type is it?
  4. Why are oils liquid at room temperature?

Answers. 1. Ester bond. 2. Three (three condensations: two fatty acids and the phosphate). 3. Polyunsaturated. 4. Their unsaturated fatty acids have kinks, so they cannot pack closely: low melting point.

B1.1.11 to B1.1.13 · SL and HL

Fat stores, the bilayer and steroids

Being hydrophobic makes lipids good stores, good insulators, and the barrier around every cell.

By the end of this section (the guide's statement titles in bold)

B1.1.11 Triglycerides in adipose tissue

Adipose tissue is fat tissue: cells full of triglyceride droplets, under the skin and around organs.

  • Energy store: triglycerides release about 38 kJ per gram in respiration, carbohydrates about 17 kJ per gram (summary notes): about twice as much, so the same energy is stored in less body mass.Learn They are insoluble, so they have no osmotic effect on cells.
  • Thermal insulation: fat conducts heat poorly, so a layer under the skin slows heat loss. Endotherms keep their body warmer than their surroundings, so the colder the habitat, the thicker the layer. Seals and whales in polar water have thick blubber.

B1.1.12 The phospholipid bilayer

A phospholipid is amphipathic: one part is hydrophilic (the phosphate head, which is charged and polar) and one part is hydrophobic (the two fatty acid tails).Learn In water, phospholipids arrange themselves into a double layer: heads face the water on both sides, tails point inwards, away from water. Nothing has to build it; it forms by itself.

Worked example: a 3-mark "explain" answer. "Explain why phospholipids form a bilayer in water."
  1. Phospholipids are amphipathic: a hydrophilic phosphate head and two hydrophobic fatty acid tails.
  2. The heads are attracted to water, SO they face the water on both sides.
  3. The tails are not attracted to water, SO they point inwards, towards each other, forming a hydrophobic core.
Why this step? (step 1)

The word "amphipathic" with both halves named is usually the first marking point. Everything else follows from it.

B1.1.13 Steroids

How to recognise a steroid in a diagram: four rings fused together, three rings of six carbons and one ring of five, with only a few -OH or C=O groups.Learn Examples: the sex hormones oestradiol and testosterone.

Steroids are mostly carbon and hydrogen, so they are non-polar (hydrophobic). They dissolve in the hydrophobic core of the bilayer and pass straight through it by diffusion, without a protein channel. So steroid hormones can enter their target cells. For context: inside, they bind to receptors and change which genes are used.

Finish it. "Explain why ringed seals living in Arctic water have a thick layer of blubber. [2]" First half: "Blubber is adipose tissue, which conducts heat poorly, SO ..."
Answer

... it reduces heat loss from the body to the very cold water; a seal keeps its body much warmer than the water, so it needs a thick layer (it also stores energy).

Exam trap. The heads are hydrophilic and the tails hydrophobic, never the other way round. To identify a steroid, point to the four fused rings; do not confuse it with a phospholipid (head and two tails) or a triglyceride (three tails).
Self-check D (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. What does "amphipathic" mean?
  2. Which part of a phospholipid faces the water?
  3. How do you recognise a steroid in a diagram?
  4. Give two reasons why triglycerides are good long-term energy stores.

Answers. 1. Having a hydrophilic part and a hydrophobic part in one molecule. 2. The phosphate head. 3. Four fused rings: three of six carbons, one of five. 4. About twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate (less mass to carry); insoluble, so no osmotic effect.

Paper 1A style (multiple choice), then 3 short written items

Quick quiz

Decide on your answer before you click. Each question explains the trap after you answer. Then write the three short answers on paper and mark them with the model answers.

1. Three α-glucose molecules (C6H12O6) are joined in a chain by condensation. What is the formula of the product?

Three molecules, two bonds, so two waters lost: C18H36O18 − 2 H2O = C18H32O16. Option 1 loses no water; option 2 loses only one; option 4 loses three (one per molecule instead of one per bond).

2. Which description always identifies a hexose?

Hexose means six carbons. Fructose is a hexose with a five-cornered ring, so counting corners fails. Sugar rings always contain one oxygen. Two joined rings is a disaccharide.

3. Which property of glucose allows it to be transported dissolved in blood plasma?

Transport in plasma needs solubility, which comes from the polar -OH groups. Stability suits storage; energy yield suits respiration. Glucose is polar, not non-polar.

4. Why do cellulose molecules form straight chains?

Alternating (flipped) β-glucose keeps the chain straight. α-glucose with 1,4 bonds gives the coiled amylose; 1,6 bonds give branches (amylopectin, glycogen). Hydrogen bonds link neighbouring cellulose chains; they do not coil them.

5. A triglyceride is hydrolysed into glycerol and fatty acids. Which bonds are broken, and what happens to water?

Hydrolysis uses water, one molecule per bond broken, and the triglyceride has three ester bonds. Glycosidic bonds are in carbohydrates. Releasing water is condensation. Option 4 describes part of a phospholipid.

6. Fatty acid X has 18 carbon atoms and no C=C double bonds. Fatty acid Y has 18 carbon atoms and two C=C double bonds. Which statement is correct?

Two or more C=C means polyunsaturated. Its kinks stop the chains packing closely, so Y melts at a lower temperature. X has no C=C: saturated, straight, higher melting point.

7. Which feature in a molecular diagram shows that a molecule is a steroid?

Four fused rings is the steroid shape. Option 1 is a phospholipid; option 2 a polysaccharide; option 4 is neither.

9. Outline how glycoproteins allow cells to be recognised, using ABO blood groups as an example. [3]

  • other cells / the immune system recognise the shape of the carbohydrate chain (self or foreign);
