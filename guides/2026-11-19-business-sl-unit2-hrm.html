People in a business

A business has owners (for a company, its shareholders), managers who run it day to day, and staff who do the work.

  • An employee has a contract with the business: regular pay, set hours, rights such as holidays and sick pay.
  • A self-employed worker works for themselves and sells their work to the business, task by task, with no employee rights.

Unit 2 is about how a business plans, organises, leads and motivates these people. Business calls them its human resources, because people are a resource, like money or machines, that must be managed well.

Pay, costs and profit

Money in: revenue = price × number sold. Money out: costs, such as materials, rent and pay. Profit = revenue − costs.

Staff pay is a cost. A wage is usually paid per hour or per item and often weekly; a salary is a fixed amount per year, paid monthly. A cleaner might earn a wage of $12 an hour; an accountant a salary of $48 000 a year, which is $4 000 a month.

That is why HR decisions are also money decisions: hiring, training and pay rises raise costs today, and the business hopes they raise output or quality, and so profit, later.

Who is in charge of whom

In most organisations people report to someone: their boss gives them work and checks it. In a school, teachers report to a head of department, who reports to the principal.

Bosses have authority: the right to give instructions and make decisions. The people who report to a manager are their subordinates.

Drawing all these links as boxes and lines gives an organisation chart: the top box is the most senior person, and each row below is one level. Section 2.2 uses this picture all the time, so try drawing your own school's chart.

Why people work

People work for money, but not only for money. Think of a part-time job: the pay matters, but so do friendly colleagues, being trusted with something important, being praised and learning new skills.

When people want to work well, managers say they are motivated. Motivated staff produce more, make fewer mistakes and stay longer, which saves the business money. When people stop caring, they are demotivated: they do the minimum, take more days off or leave.

Section 2.4 gives three theories that explain what motivates people at work, and the rewards a business can use.

Introduction to human resource management

The right number of people, with the right skills, at the right time, ready to accept change.

By the end of this section I can

HRM covers planning, hiring, training, pay, welfare and staff leaving (retirement, dismissal, redundancy).

Why staff resist change, and what HR can do

Four usual reasons: self-interest (fear of losing a job, pay, status or team); misunderstanding and mistrust (not told why, so they imagine the worst); a different view (they honestly think the change is wrong); low tolerance of change (fear of the unknown or of not coping).

StrategyBest whenDrawback
Education and communication: explain why, earlyRumoursSlow
Participation and involvement: staff help plan itCommitment neededSlow
Facilitation and support: training, time to adjustStaff fear not copingCostly
Negotiation and agreement: a bonus, redundancy payA group will clearly loseExpensive
Manipulation and co-option: give a resister a role, share selected factsOther methods too slowUnethical; trust lost
Coercion: force it throughUrgent, survivalStaff demotivated or leave

Also: change in stages, and a fitting leadership style (2.3). For context only: the list is usually credited to Kotter and Schlesinger; the names are not needed for the IB exam.

Worked example: recommend a strategy (AO3). A hospital will replace paper patient notes with tablets in six months. Nurses complain nobody asked them; several older nurses say they "are not good with computers".
  1. Find the causes in the case: "nobody asked" (misunderstanding, mistrust) and "not good with computers" (low tolerance, fear of not coping).
  2. Strategy 1, participation: a group of nurses tests the tablets and helps design the screens, so the system fits a shift and nurses feel ownership.
  3. Strategy 2, facilitation and support: training in work time and a helper nurse on each ward.
  4. Weigh it: both cost time and money, but six months is enough. Coercion would be quicker but could push experienced nurses to leave.
Why this step? Why find the causes before choosing a strategy? (think, then open)

A strategy only works if it removes the cause: training does not help nurses angry at not being asked. Examiners reward a strategy linked to the case.

Finish it. A school will move all homework to an online system in three months. Teachers say they were not asked, and setting it up needs several evenings of extra unpaid work.
  1. Cause 1: "not asked", so misunderstanding and mistrust. Strategy: participation, for example a group of teachers chooses the settings.
  2. Cause 2: ... Strategy: ... Weigh it: ...
Answer

Cause 2: the extra unpaid work is self-interest (they lose free time). Strategy: negotiation (paid time or cover lessons for setup) or facilitation (training and setup time inside the school day). Weigh: costs money, but three months is enough time, and forcing it (coercion) would hurt goodwill.

Exam traps. (1) Gig workers are not "part-time employees": most are self-employed and paid per task. (2) Flexi-time is choosing when you work; part-time is working fewer hours. (3) Naming a strategy without linking it to the cause in the case and giving its drawback.
Self-check 2.1 (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. Internal or external: a falling birth rate; a plan to open a factory; a new minimum wage law; buying warehouse robots?
  2. Occupational versus geographical mobility?
  3. One reason call-centre staff might resist working from home three days a week.
  4. When does coercion fit, and what is its risk?

Answers. 1: external, internal, external, internal. 2: moving between jobs or careers; moving between places. 3: loss of team and social contact (self-interest), or fear of not coping with the technology (low tolerance). 4: urgent change, for example survival; risk: lost trust, good staff leave.

2.2

Organisational structure

Who reports to whom, who decides, and how many people each manager looks after.

By the end of this section I can

Delegation Learn
A manager passing authority for a task or decision to someone lower down; the manager stays responsible for the result.
Span of control Learn
The number of people who report directly to one manager.
Levels of the hierarchy Learn
The number of layers of authority from top to bottom.
Chain of command Learn
The line through which instructions pass down and reports pass up.
Bureaucracy Learn
Many formal rules, procedures and paperwork, with decisions passing through several levels: good control, slow and inflexible.
Centralisation / decentralisation Learn
Most decisions made by a few people at the top / authority passed down to lower levels or local branches.
Delayering Learn
Removing one or more levels of the hierarchy, usually middle managers.
Matrix structure Learn
Staff from different departments work in project teams, so each reports to two managers: their department manager and a project manager.

An organisation chart shows jobs as boxes and who reports to whom as lines. A wide span means fewer managers, fewer levels (flat) and a shorter chain: faster messages, but managers must delegate. A narrow span allows close supervision but needs more levels (tall).

Chart LearnFeaturesSuits / drawback
Flat (horizontal)Few levels, wide spansSmall or creative firms / managers overloaded
Tall (vertical)Many levels, narrow spans, often bureaucraticWork needing close control / slow, costly
By functionDepartments: marketing, finance, HR, operationsOne main product / departments do not talk
By productA division for each productVery different products / functions copied, costly
By regionA division for each area, run locallySeveral countries / regions drift from the firm's aims
Worked example 1: draw and read a chart (AO4). Kai Foods: a managing director (MD) and four department heads (operations, marketing, finance, HR). Operations has three supervisors with 8 staff each.
  1. Draw from the top: MD; four heads; three supervisors under operations; "8 staff" under each (the figure). Departments are functions, so it is a chart by function.
  2. Levels: MD, heads, supervisors, staff = 4.
  3. Spans: MD 4; operations manager 3; each supervisor 8.
  4. People in operations: 1 + 3 + 3 × 8 = 28.
Why this step? Why is the MD's span 4 and not 31? (think, then open)

Span of control counts only the people who report directly to that manager: the four department heads. Everyone further down reports to someone else.

Finish it. Lumo Toys: an MD and three heads (design, sales, finance). The sales head has 4 team leaders, each with 5 sales staff.
  1. Levels: MD, heads, team leaders, staff = 4.
  2. Span of the MD, of the sales head, of each team leader: ... People in sales: ...
Answer

Spans: MD 3, sales head 4, team leader 5. People in sales: 1 + 4 + 4 × 5 = 25.

Worked example 2: delayering in numbers. Bloom Books: a chief executive, 3 area directors, 6 regional managers (2 each), 24 store managers (4 each), then store staff. It removes the regional managers ($70 000 a year each).
  1. Levels: 5 before, 4 after.
  2. Each director's span: 2 before; 24 ÷ 3 = 8 after.
  3. Saving: 6 × $70 000 = $420 000 a year (less one-off redundancy pay).
  4. Weigh it: shorter chain, faster decisions; but directors may be overloaded, and staff may fear more cuts (resistance, 2.1).
Why this step? Why mention redundancy pay in step 3? (think, then open)

Removing 6 jobs costs redundancy pay once. Spotting a cost the headline number leaves out earns evaluation marks.

Finish it. A bank has a CEO, 4 directors, 12 area managers (3 each) and 48 branch managers (4 each). It removes the area managers ($60 000 each).
  1. Levels (counting branch staff): 5 before, 4 after.
  2. Each director's new span: ... Yearly saving: ...
Answer

48 ÷ 4 = 12 branch managers per director; 12 × $60 000 = $720 000 a year.

Matrix structure

A matrix suits firms with many projects (engineering, software): skills mix and teams form fast. But two bosses can give conflicting instructions, and coordination is costly.

Which structure fits when the outside world changes? (AO3)

External changeMay fit / risk
RecessionDelayering, more central control to cut costs / overload, low morale
Fast technology change, new rivalsFlatter, decentralised or matrix for speed and ideas / less control

Exam traps. (1) Counting everyone below a manager as their span. (2) Delayering removes levels, not just any staff. (3) Delegation is one manager passing a task down; decentralisation is the whole firm moving decisions away from the top.

2.3

Leadership and management

Managers make sure things get done; leaders make people want to do them.

By the end of this section I can

Management Learn
Getting things done through people and resources to meet objectives: planning, organising, directing, coordinating and controlling. Focus on tasks, targets, the short term; authority from the job title.
Leadership Learn
Setting a direction and inspiring people to follow it willingly. Focus on people, vision and change; influence from trust and example.

One person can be both.

Worked example: a 4-mark "explain". Mira leads 12 experienced software designers building an app due in 6 months. She plans weekly team votes on design choices. Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of a democratic style for Mira's team. [4]
  1. Marks: 2 + 2, each a point plus why, tied to the case.
  2. Advantage: the 12 designers are experienced, so votes bring in expert ideas, and staff who chose a design are more committed to it.
  3. Disadvantage: weekly votes among 12 people take time and can end in disagreement, slowing a project with a fixed deadline.
Why this step? Why repeat "12 experienced" and "6 months" from the case? (think, then open)

Without case facts the answer is general theory and gets only the first mark of each pair.

Finish it. Raj runs a fast-food outlet where most of the 20 staff are 16 to 18 and in their first job. He gives clear orders and checks every task. Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of his autocratic style. [4]
  1. Advantage: new young staff need clear instructions, so orders and checks keep service fast and food safe.
  2. Disadvantage: ...
Answer

Disadvantage: staff have no say, so they may feel bored and undervalued, and young staff in a first job may leave quickly; Raj must then keep training new staff, which costs time and money.

Exam traps. (1) Paternalistic and autocratic both decide alone; paternalistic acts out of care for staff. (2) Laissez-faire is not "no leader": the leader still sets goals. (3) Situational is not a sixth fixed style: it means choosing among the others.
Self-check 2.3 (write the style for each, then open)
  1. Which style fits: (a) a factory fire; (b) a lab of experienced scientists; (c) a bakery owner who decides everything herself but treats staff like family?
  2. One difference between management and leadership.

Answers. 1: (a) autocratic; (b) laissez-faire or democratic; (c) paternalistic. 2: for example, managers focus on tasks and targets, leaders on people and vision.

2.4

Motivation and demotivation

What makes people want to work well, and what makes them stop.

By the end of this section I can

Motivated staff produce more and stay longer; demotivation shows as low output, absence and staff leaving.

Taylor: scientific management

Workers are motivated mainly by money: find the one most efficient way to do each job, train workers in it, supervise closely and pay by output (piece rate). Evaluate: raises output in repetitive work, but ignores social needs, and speed can beat quality.

Maslow: hierarchy of needs

At work: physiological = enough pay, breaks; safety = job security, safe conditions; social = teamwork, friendly colleagues; esteem = praise, a job title, promotion; self-actualisation = challenging, creative work. Evaluate: easy to apply; but people differ, and levels are hard to measure.

Herzberg: motivation-hygiene theory

Hygiene factors Learn are around the job (pay, conditions, security, supervision, policy, colleagues): poor ones cause dissatisfaction, good ones only remove it. Motivators Learn are in the job (achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, promotion, growth): they create motivation, for example through job enrichment. Many books call it the two-factor theory. Evaluate: explains why a pay rise gives only a short boost; but pay does motivate some workers (sales staff on commission).

Worked example 1: pay sums. Ana earns $15 an hour for 38 hours; Ben earns $0.40 per shirt and makes 1 500; Chloe earns 5% commission on $40 000 of sales.
  1. Ana, time rate: 15 × 38 = $570.
  2. Ben, piece rate: 0.40 × 1 500 = $600.
  3. Chloe, commission: 0.05 × 40 000 = $2 000.
Why this step? Why write 5% as 0.05? (think, then open)

5% means 5 out of 100, which is 0.05. Multiplying by 5 instead of 0.05 gives an answer 100 times too big.

Finish it. Dev earns $14 an hour for 20 hours; Eli earns $0.25 per unit and makes 1 200; Fay earns 4% commission on $30 000.
  1. Dev: 14 × 20 = $280.
  2. Eli: ... Fay: ...
Answer

Eli: 0.25 × 1 200 = $300. Fay: 0.04 × 30 000 = $1 200.

Worked example 2: Herzberg on a case. A hotel gave reception staff a 5% pay rise. Six months later they still say the work is repetitive and managers never praise them. Explain why the rise did not motivate, and suggest one non-financial reward. [4]
  1. Pay is a hygiene factor: the rise removed some dissatisfaction but cannot motivate on its own.
  2. The complaints are missing motivators: the work itself and recognition.
  3. Suggest job enrichment: experienced receptionists handle VIP guests and train new staff, plus regular praise.
Why this step? Why name which factor each complaint is? (think, then open)

Sorting case facts into hygiene factors and motivators is what "applying" the theory means.

Finish it. A warehouse pays well and is safe, but pickers do the same route all day and are never asked for ideas.
  1. Pay and safety are hygiene factors, already good, so they remove dissatisfaction only.
  2. Missing motivators: ... One reward: ...
Answer

Missing: the work itself (repetitive) and responsibility (never asked). Reward: job rotation between routes and tasks, or empowerment, letting teams plan their own routes.

Exam traps. (1) Herzberg does not say pay is unimportant: poor pay causes dissatisfaction. (2) Enrichment (more responsibility) is not enlargement (more tasks at the same level).

Unit 2, 2.1 to 2.4

Quiz: 7 multiple choice + 3 written

Real papers are not multiple choice; these let the page mark you. Written items are exam style: write on paper, then mark with the model answer. For any miss: close the page, redo that section's self-check, write the rule in the Error log.

1. Which is an external factor in HR planning?

An ageing population is demographic change, outside the firm. The others are the firm's own decisions, so internal.

2. Staff wrongly believe new software means job cuts, because managers sent one email with no explanation. Which strategy tackles the cause most directly?

The cause is misunderstanding, so explaining fixes it cheaply. Coercion confirms their fears; a bonus is costly for a rumour; manipulation destroys trust.

3. An MD has 4 department heads; each head has 6 staff. The span of control of a head is

Span counts direct reports: 6. 4 is the MD's span, 24 all staff below the heads, 3 the number of levels.

4. Delayering most likely leads to

Fewer managers means each looks after more people, and instructions pass through fewer levels.

5. A family-firm owner decides everything herself, always with her staff's welfare in mind. Her style is

Deciding alone out of care for staff is paternalistic. The trap is autocratic, which decides alone without that care.

Written 2 (2.2). FreshMart has 40 stores. It plans to remove its 8 area managers, so store managers report straight to its 2 regional directors. Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of this delayering for FreshMart. [4]

Show model answer and checklist

Advantage: a shorter chain of command, so decisions reach stores faster, and 8 salaries are saved. Disadvantage: each director's span rises from 4 to 40 ÷ 2 = 20 store managers, so directors may be overloaded and stores get less support.
