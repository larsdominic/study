Looking at your own work honestly

It is hard to see mistakes in your own writing: you read what you meant, not what you wrote. Two tricks help.

  • Read your paragraph one sentence at a time and ask of each: what does this sentence do? Describe, name, explain, judge?
  • Compare with a model sentence by sentence, not as a whole.

A habit is a mistake that comes back every time. Once you can name it, you can catch it.

What an infographic is

An infographic (information + graphic) shows facts as a picture: big numbers, small icons, colours and arrows, with very few words. You see posters like this in schools, hospitals and on social media.

It looks neutral, like a list of facts, but someone chose every number, colour and arrow. In Paper 1 you analyse those choices exactly as you did for cartoons.

Numbers in plain words

"6 out of 10" means 60%: of every 10 people, 6. A proportion is this kind of part of a whole.

"An estimated 1 million" means experts calculated it carefully from data; it is not an exact count of every person.

You do not need to check or argue with the numbers in Paper 1. You analyse how they are shown: how big, what colour, next to what.

Who made it: the WHO

The World Health Organization (WHO) is the United Nations agency for health: countries work together through it on things like vaccines, diseases and healthy living. It has regional offices; today's infographic comes from its office for Europe.

Who made a text matters: a health agency's logo makes readers trust the message more than an unknown website's would.

Block 2 · your paragraphs against the Horsey model

Your three habits that lose marks

A habit is a mistake you make every time. Find it once, fix it for good.

By the end of this section I can

Eight habits that cost marks Understand and apply

1 Describing

Sign: Sentences stop at what is there

Costs: B: "descriptive"; "reliant on description"

Fix: After every detail: so, which suggests

2 Naming only

Sign: "This is juxtaposition." and nothing after

Costs: B: "little relevant analysis"

Fix: Technique + effect verb + reader

3 No claim

Sign: First sentence retells the text

Costs: C: "little focus"

Fix: First sentence answers the guiding question

4 Literal only

Sign: No target, no hidden message

Costs: A: "understanding of the literal meaning" without "implications"

Fix: Name the target and what is implied

5 Guessing feelings

Sign: "Red makes the reader angry."

Costs: B (and your class guide)

Fix: "Here, ... suggests ... because"

6 Vague evidence

Sign: "The picture shows stuff about money."

Costs: A: references "rarely appropriate"

Fix: One precise detail or short quote per point

7 Chatty language

Sign: "really big", "kind of", "the guy"

Costs: D: register not "appropriate"

Fix: Formal register, exact terms

8 No judgement

Sign: Never says how well it works

Costs: B: levels 4 and 5 need "evaluation"

Fix: One sentence on how well a choice works

The Horsey checklist (from 13 November)

  • Thesis answers the guiding question (C).
  • Every piece of evidence is followed by what it does (B).
  • Image and words read together; one less obvious detail (B).
  • The implied message and how the reader is positioned (A).
  • A judgement of how well it works (B); terms used accurately, formal register (D).
Worked example: diagnosing a paragraph. A made-up student paragraph on the ship cartoon from 13 November:
(1) In the cartoon there is a big gold ship and a small wooden boat. (2) The ship is really big and the boat is small. (3) This is juxtaposition. (4) The speech bubble says "We're all in the same boat!" which is ironic. (5) The cartoonist wants to show that rich people are bad.
  1. Sentence 1: retells the picture, so there is no claim: habit 3.
  2. Sentence 2: still describing (habit 1), and "really big" is chatty (habit 7).
  3. Sentences 3 and 4: name a technique and stop: habit 2.
  4. Sentence 5: a target at last, but vague and judged with a feeling word ("bad"): habits 4 and 7.
  5. Three biggest habits: describing, naming only, no claim. One fix for sentence 3: "The juxtaposition of the towering gold ship and the tiny boat lets the reader see the inequality before reading a word."
Why this step? Why pick only three habits?

Three rules fit on a sticky note and in your head under exam pressure. Fixing the three most frequent ones lifts more marks than half-fixing eight.

A new text type · same method

Infographics: numbers, pictures and paths

Paper 1 can give you any non-literary text type. The chain works on all of them.

By the end of this section I can

The official guide's list of non-literary text types includes "Advertisement", "Cartoon" and "Infographic"; the two Paper 1 passages always come from two different types. An infographic turns information into a picture you can read in seconds. Its purpose is usually to inform, and often also to persuade or to call for action. The terms below are common teaching terms, not from your class files.

ConventionWhat it isWhat it can do
Title or headlineThe biggest words, at the topSets the message before any detail
StatisticsNumbers in large, bold typeShocks or convinces; sounds objective
PictogramA row of small icons standing for a numberMakes a proportion visible at a glance
Icon vs symbolIcon: a simple picture of the thing itself (a bus for transport). Symbol: a thing standing for an idea (a heart for love)Icons make it quick and universal; symbols add emotion
Colour codingOne colour per section or ideaGroups information; guides the eye
Reading pathLines, arrows or numbers that lead the eyeControls the order, so the text ends where the creator wants
ImperativesVerbs that give orders: "Use the stairs"Speaks directly to the reader; calls for action
Logo and sourceWho made it, often at the bottomGives authority, so the reader trusts it
Worked example. An infographic invented for practice (the numbers are not real data): title "Your school run, rethought"; under it "3 OUT OF 4 car trips to school are under 2 km" next to four car icons, three of them red; a green path winds from a house icon to a school icon past a walker, a bike and a bus; at the bottom, "Walk it. Bike it. Bus it." and a city council logo.
  1. Spot it: the four car icons, three in red, beside "3 OUT OF 4".
  2. Explain it: a pictogram with colour coding turns the statistic into a picture.
  3. Effect: the reader sees the proportion at a glance, and the red marks most car trips as a problem.
  4. Purpose: to persuade parents that most short car trips to school are unnecessary.
Why this step? Why analyse the icons and not just quote the number?

Quoting "3 out of 4" only repeats the text (description). The marks come from how the creator shows the number: the red, the row of cars, the size.

  3. Effect:  
  4. Purpose:  
Answer

Effect: the short, rhythmic orders are easy to remember, and the green path makes the alternatives look natural and simple, so the reader feels the change is easy. Purpose: a call to action that turns the shocking number above into something the family can do tomorrow.

Exam traps. (1) Do not argue with the data ("I don't think that is true"): analyse how it is presented. (2) "Colourful and eye-catching" is description: say which colour does what, here. (3) An infographic still has a creator, an audience and a purpose: name them in your first sentence.

Answers. 1: icon (a picture of the thing itself). 2: a pictogram with colour coding; it shows the proportion, 3 out of 10, at a glance. 3: an imperative.

Model paragraph on the WHO infographic (open after marking yours)
The WHO infographic combines alarming statistics with a clear reading path to persuade readers that exercise can fit into every part of ordinary life. [claim] It opens with its biggest numbers, "6 OUT OF 10" beside a row of ten small figures and "1 MILLION DEATHS" in bold capitals, so the reader feels the size of the problem before meeting any advice. [statistics + pictogram, effect] Below, four coloured lines, labelled "AT HOME", "AT SCHOOL", "AT WORK" and "IN THE COMMUNITY", branch out like routes on a transport map, and each stop offers a small instruction: "Use the stairs, not the elevators". [colour coding + imperative] Because the actions are short and everyday, change seems easy rather than a sacrifice. [effect] Every line then runs down into one final sentence in capitals, "REGULAR PHYSICAL ACTIVITY THROUGHOUT THE LIFE-COURSE ENABLES PEOPLE TO LIVE BETTER AND LONGER LIVES", so all paths lead the eye to the same conclusion. [reading path] Together with the WHO logo, this simple design makes a public-health message feel both trustworthy and achievable. [purpose + judgement]

Why it works. A: it names the purpose (persuade) and the implied message (activity is easy). B: every detail has an effect; image and words are read together; the last sentence judges how well the design works. C: the claim answers the guiding question. D: precise terms (pictogram, imperative, reading path), formal register.

Notes on the backup infographic (road safety)

The huge red "186 300" is the first thing the eye meets, so the scale of child deaths lands before any explanation. Rows of small child figures make the number human. The cartoon-like children with a ball and a bike in the title area show who is at risk, and the yellow and black stripes recall road-works warnings. "#SaveKidsLives" turns the reader into a campaigner. Purpose: to make road safety for children feel urgent and preventable.
