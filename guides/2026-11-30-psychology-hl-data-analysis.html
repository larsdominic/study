What Paper 3 is

Paper 3 is the extra exam that only HL students sit: 1 hour 45 minutes, 30 marks, 30% of your final grade. You get a short booklet of sources: graphs, tables of results and summaries of interviews, all about one claim (a statement such as "technology in school harms students' mental health").

There are four questions. The first three each look at one source; the last one asks how far all the sources together support the claim. You never collect data yourself: everything you need is printed in the booklet. Examiners mark each answer against a short description of what a 1-mark, 3-mark or 6-mark answer looks like.

Three kinds of average

Take five test scores: 3, 4, 4, 6, 8.

  • Mean: add them (25) and divide by how many (5): 5.
  • Median: put them in order and take the middle one: 4.
  • Mode: the one that appears most often: 4.

All three try to say what a typical score is. Now change the 8 into 28. The mean jumps to 9, but the median stays 4. One unusual score can drag the mean a long way, which is why psychologists often report the median as well.

Same average, different spread

Two classes both have a mean test score of 15 out of 20.

  • Class A: 14, 15, 15, 16. Everyone scored about the same.
  • Class B: 5, 15, 20, 20. Some did very badly, some perfectly.

The average hides this difference. A measure of spread shows it. The simplest is the range: highest minus lowest (2 for class A, 15 for class B). In Paper 3 you always look at the average and the spread before you say what a result means.

Numbers or words: two kinds of data

Quantitative data are numbers: scores, times, counts. They are easy to compare: group A recalled 12 words, group B 8.

Qualitative data are words: what people said in an interview or a focus group (a small group discussion led by a researcher). The researcher reads the written-out conversations and groups similar ideas into themes, such as "feeling lonely".

Paper 3 uses both. Question 3 is always about the words kind: can we trust the themes, and do they apply to other people?

A link is not a cause

Students who spend more hours on their phones may get lower grades. That is a link (a correlation). It does not show that phones cause lower grades: struggling students might turn to their phones more, or tiredness might cause both.

Only a study where the researcher changes one thing on purpose and compares groups can suggest a cause. In Paper 3, always ask: was anything changed on purpose, or were things just measured?

Reading a graph before believing it

Before you look at the bars, read the edges of the graph:

  • the title: what is it really about?
  • the axes: what is measured, in what units, and does the scale start at 0?
  • the key: which colour is which group?
  • what is missing: how many people were asked? when?

A scale that starts at 6.6 instead of 0 can make a small difference look huge. Spotting things like that is exactly Paper 3 question 1.

Descriptive statistics

An average says what is typical. A measure of spread says how much people differ. Paper 3 wants both.

By the end of this section I can

StatisticWhat it is
Meanadd all scores, divide by how many; pulled by one extreme score
Medianmiddle score in order (even number: halfway between the middle two); not pulled by extremes
Modemost frequent score or category
Rangehighest − lowest; one outlier makes it huge
Standard deviation (SD)roughly the typical distance of a score from the mean
Semi-interquartile range (SIQR)(Q3 − Q1) ÷ 2: half the width of the middle 50%; ignores extremes

Class rule (HL01): categories: mode; ordered ratings (1 to 5): median; numbers, symmetrical: mean; numbers with skew or outliers: median. Q1 and Q3 are a quarter and three quarters of the way up the ordered scores.

Worked example 1: five statistics on a made-up data set. Understand and apply

Made up: words recalled (out of 20), in order.

ConditionScores (n = 7)MeanMedianModeRangeSD
Phone on desk6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 189.4388123.99
Phone away9, 10, 11, 12, 12, 13, 1411.57121251.72

  2. Median: the 4th of 7 scores. Desk 8, away 12. Mode: desk 8, away 12 (each appears twice).
  3. Range: desk 18 − 6 = 12; away 14 − 9 = 5. SD (from a calculator or the source): 3.99 and 1.72.

Why this step? Why recalculate without the 18? (write two key words, then open)

To show how much one outlier moves the mean and spread; if one score changes the picture this much, report the median and say why.

Worked example 2: read a box and whisker plot. Understand and apply

Made up: reading scores (out of 30), notifications on or off. Five numbers: lowest, Q1 (box left edge), median (red line), Q3 (box right edge), highest.

  1. Typical score: median 14 (off) against 10 (on). But 14 lies inside the "on" box (8 to 15), so the middle halves overlap a lot.
  2. SIQR: on (15 − 8) ÷ 2 = 3.5; off (16 − 12) ÷ 2 = 2. Range: on 20, off 10. The "on" scores vary more.
  3. Shape: "on" has 2 from Q1 to the median but 5 from the median to Q3, and whiskers of 4 (left) and 9 (right): positively skewed (guide glossary: "a longer tail on the right side"), so its mean is probably above its median. "Off" is symmetrical (2 and 2, 3 and 3).
Why this step? Why compare the median with the other group's box? (write two key words, then open)

Overlap: if one median sits inside the other group's box, many people scored alike, so the difference is less clear-cut. The specimen markscheme uses this check.

Finish it. Made up: box plots of anxiety scores. Group X: lowest 5, Q1 9, median 11, Q3 13, highest 17. Group Y: lowest 6, Q1 7, median 8, Q3 12, highest 20.
  1. Done for you: medians 11 (X) and 8 (Y). SIQR of X = (13 − 9) ÷ 2 = 2.
  2. Your turn: SIQR of Y, the two ranges, and which group is skewed, which way, and how you can tell.
Answer

SIQR Y = (12 − 7) ÷ 2 = 2.5. Ranges: X 12, Y 14. Y is positively skewed: 1 then 4 inside the box, whiskers 1 (left) and 8 (right). X is symmetrical (2 and 2, 4 and 4).

Exam traps. (1) A big range can come from one outlier: check the SD or SIQR. (2) A high SD is not "unreliable data": it means people differed. (3) Mean well above median: a few high scores pull it up (positive skew). (4) Always quote the numbers: "median 14 against 10", not "higher".

Paper 3 question 2 · data analysis [6]

Question 2: analyse and state a conclusion

"Analyse": "Break down in order to bring out the essential elements or structure." Then a conclusion tied to the numbers.

By the end of this section I can

Recipe: (1) what is measured and what a high score means; (2) typical scores compared, with the size of the gap; (3) spread compared; (4) shape, outliers, overlap; (5) a conclusion in the claim's words, with the key numbers and how sure you can be.

Worked example 3: Q2 on worked example 1. Made-up question: "Analyse the findings and state a conclusion linked to the claim that phones in the classroom harm learning." Understand and apply
  1. The source compares words recalled out of 20 by 7 students with the phone on the desk and 7 with it in another room; higher is better recall.
  2. The phone-away group recalled more on both averages: mean 11.57 against 9.43 (2.14 words more), median 12 against 8 (4 more).
  3. The desk group was far more spread out: SD 3.99 against 1.72, range 12 against 5.
  4. That spread comes mostly from one outlier (18). Without it the desk mean is 8.0, so the median is the fairer comparison. Six of the seven desk scores are below the away median of 12.
  5. So, in this sample, students whose phone was in another room recalled more words (median 12 against 8), which supports the claim. The conclusion is tentative: 7 per group, one outlier, and no test of whether the gap could be chance.
Why this step? Why end with "tentative"? (write two key words, then open)

The top band needs a conclusion "explicitly linked to the findings": saying how far the numbers carry it (small groups, an outlier) is part of that link.

Finish it. Made up: motivation scores (out of 40) after four weeks, 20 students per group. App with points and badges: mean 27.4, median 28, SD 3.1. No app: mean 24.9, median 25, SD 6.8. Claim: "technology increases students' motivation".
  1. Done for you: the app group scored higher on both averages: mean 27.4 against 24.9 (2.5 more), median 28 against 25 (3 more).
  2. Your turn: one sentence on spread, then the conclusion sentence.
Answer

Spread: the no-app SD (6.8) is more than twice the app SD (3.1): app scores were much more alike. Conclusion: so the app group was somewhat more motivated (median 28 against 25), which supports the claim, but the gap is small next to the no-app spread, and 20 per group for four weeks is a limited test.

Exam traps. (1) Listing numbers is not analysing: which is higher, by how much, why it matters. (2) Write the conclusion as its own last sentence, starting "So", in the claim's words. (3) Never "proves". (4) Specimen Q2 (done on 21 Nov): the markscheme prints the ranges as 14 and 12, but 25 − 12 = 13. It looks like a misprint; trust your own subtraction.
Class homework HL02 · the class task for Q2

Bandura vs Charlton: same theory, different data

The sheet's golden rule: "point to the number". Every claim uses a figure from the tables.

By the end of this section I can

Study A (Bandura, Ross and Ross, 1961): a laboratory experiment. Study B (Charlton et al., 2000): playgrounds on St Helena before and after TV arrived. Use only the numbers on the sheet.

Watch the unit. In Study A one number stands for one child. In Study B it is mean acts per 30 minutes of video, with different children in 1994 and 2000 and no count of children given.
Model points (open after your own sentences)

Describe

  • A: 22 of the 24 children who saw the aggressive model (92%) copied its aggressive acts; about 70% of children in the other two groups scored zero.
  • A, girls' mallet aggression: 18.0 (aggressive model), 13.1 (no model), 0.5 (quiet model): the quiet model lowered it more (12.6) than the aggressive one raised it (4.9).
  • B: only 9 of 64 comparisons (14%) changed noticeably. Antisocial: 2 of 32, both decreases. Prosocial: 7 of 32 (5 down, 2 up).
  • B: one prosocial behaviour among pairs of girls fell from 13.67 to 5.75 acts per 30 minutes, a drop of 7.92 (about 58%).

Question

  • Each A subgroup (sex of child × sex of model) had only 6 children. With about 70% zeros in two groups, a mean describes few real children.
  • A's observers could often tell the group, because aggressive-condition children were easy to spot: possible researcher bias, although agreement was close (correlations in the .90s).
  • B cannot link any child to how much TV they watched.

Compare

  • Learning by watching a model: A is stronger (control group, matched groups, 22 of 24 copied). Design limit: a laboratory and a Bobo doll, minutes after the model.
  • What happens when TV arrives in a real community: B is stronger, and antisocial behaviour hardly changed (2 of 32, both down).
  • No contradiction: A counts copies of specific acts by each child straight after a model; B counts general playground behaviour per 30 minutes, years later. Both can be right.

Worked example 6: a Q4 plan. Made-up claim: "Phones in the classroom harm students' learning." Source A: worked example 1 (median 12 words with phone away, 8 on the desk). Source B: a survey of 200 students, correlation coefficient −0.31 between daily phone hours and grades. Source C: focus groups: "looking things up quickly helps", "notifications pull my attention away". Understand and apply
  1. Opening: the sources partly support the claim, but none shows clearly that phones cause worse learning in real lessons.
  2. A supports it and is the only one that can suggest cause, because the researcher changed where the phone was. Limit: 7 per group, one outlier, a word list, not a lesson.
  3. B: more phone time goes with lower grades, but −0.31 is fairly weak and cannot show cause: struggling students may use phones more (bidirectional ambiguity).
  4. C gives both sides: phones help and distract. It shows how students experience it, not how much.
  5. Conclusion: limited support. The strongest source is small and artificial; the others do not show cause, and C shows benefits.
Why this step? Why give each source its limitation? (write two key words, then open)

A limitation says how much weight a source carries, so the conclusion follows from the weights, not from counting sources.

Finish it: paragraph frame for source B. Fill the blanks on paper.

"Source B shows that ______ (number) between ______ and ______. This supports the claim because ______. However, ______ (limitation), so source B ______ (how much weight)."

Answer

"Source B shows a correlation of −0.31 between daily phone hours and grades. This supports the claim because heavier phone users tended to have lower grades. However, it is fairly weak and cannot show which causes which, so source B adds only weak support."

Exam traps. (1) Fewer than three sources cannot reach the top band. (2) Summaries without links to the claim are "descriptive" (4-6 band). (3) The conclusion must be "consistent with the arguments presented".
Paper 3 question 1 · interpretation of graphs [3]

Question 1: one issue that limits interpretation

"Explain": "Give a detailed account including reasons or causes." Name the issue, show it in the graph, say what it does to the conclusion.

By the end of this section I can

Issues the specimen markscheme accepted: y-axis not starting at zero; no sample size; an undefined category; something missing (no spread, people counted twice); a title that does not match the claim.

Worked example 4: a 3-mark answer. Made-up source: mean hours of sleep with and without a phone in the bedroom. Understand and apply
  1. Identify (1 mark): the y-axis starts at 6.6 hours instead of 0.
  2. Describe (2): so the "no phone" bar looks 2.5 times as tall (0.5 against 0.2 above the axis), although the real difference is 7.1 − 6.8 = 0.3 hours, about 18 minutes or 4.4% more sleep.
  3. Explain (3): a reader may conclude that a phone in the bedroom badly cuts sleep, when the graph supports only a small difference; with no sample size or spread we cannot even tell whether 18 minutes is more than normal night-to-night variation.
Why this step? Why calculate the 2.5 and the 4.4%? (write two key words, then open)

They compare the size of the distortion with the size of the real effect: that is the reason the issue limits interpretation.

Finish it. Made up: a bar chart titled "Online learning and loneliness" shows 41% of students lonely in 2021 against 23% in 2019. No sample sizes are given.
  1. Done for you (1 mark): the graph does not give the number of students asked in each year.
  2. Your turn: describe (2) and explain (3).
Answer

Describe: 41% could be 41 of 100 students or 4 of 10, and the two years may have asked different groups. Explain: so the 18-point rise could be chance or a change in who was asked; the graph cannot show that loneliness rose, let alone that online learning caused it.

Exam traps. (1) "The sample is too small" with no link to this graph stays at 1 mark. (2) Two issues: only the first is marked. (3) Q1 is about interpreting the data, not a general weakness of the design.
Class files HL03, HL04, HL05 · the class task for Q1

The Clancy cards: the space between data and conclusion

HL05: "A number is an observation. A verdict is an interpretation."

By the end of this section I can

The class used a real, distressing court case in which the defence argued postpartum psychosis (a rare, severe mental illness after giving birth). For Paper 3 only the skill matters: reading each number for what it really measured.

  • D, in the data: what exactly was measured, how many people, when, where, who.
  • R, in the reader: which comparison you choose; noticing only what fits (confirmation bias); being swayed by the first number or its framing (anchoring and framing).
  • C, in the context: your role, what the source wanted to do (inform or persuade), what was left out.

Card 3 is a D example: reoffending figures run from about 2% (4 of 177, Connecticut, closely monitored release) to 36% (Quebec, over nine years). They used different measures (rearrested, charged again), follow-up times, places and years: different numbers, not one number measured badly.

Model three-sentence limitation for the 35% figure (open after yours)

(1) The 35% comes from one state, Oregon, whose review board, according to the source's own title, releases people found not guilty by reason of insanity more quickly than nearly every other state. (2) Under closely monitored release in Connecticut only about 2% (4 of 177) were rearrested, so the rate seems to depend heavily on how a state supervises people after release, not only on the people themselves. (3) So the 35% cannot be used as a general reoffending risk for all insanity acquittees, let alone for one mother in Massachusetts; it describes what happened under Oregon's quicker release system.

Other good single limitations: "charged again" is not the same as convicted; the source is an investigative news article (ProPublica, 2018), not a peer-reviewed study.

Exam traps. (1) Different figures from different studies do not prove one is wrong: first check whether they measured the same thing. (2) "4% risk" and "96% do not" are the same number framed two ways: describe it neutrally.

Question 3: credibility, bias or transferability

Always about a qualitative source. The paper asks one of three set questions.

By the end of this section I can

The three possible questions (guide p49) Learn word for word: "Discuss how the researcher could improve the credibility of the findings." / "Discuss how the researcher could avoid bias." / "To what extent are the findings transferable to other populations or contexts?"

  • Credibility Learn (guide glossary): "The degree to which the research gives a true picture of what is being investigated and the results represent the perceptions and opinions of the research participants."
  • Transferability Learn: "the extent to which findings from a study can be applied or generalized to other contexts or settings beyond the specific study's participants or conditions."

Strategies the specimen markscheme rewards: triangulation (another method, other data sources, or a second researcher); member checking (participants confirm or correct the themes); peer review of methods and themes; being open about conflicts of interest and own views (reflexivity). Transferability depends on how similar the new people and setting are (age, culture, school system, access to technology, time) and on whether the researcher described the sample and setting fully.

Worked example 5: transferability. Understand and apply
Made-up source. A teacher interviewed 6 Grade 11 students at her own international school in Singapore about using an AI chatbot for homework, and analysed the transcripts alone. Themes: "it saves time on hard questions"; "I worry about being accused of cheating"; "I feel less proud of my work".

"To what extent are the findings transferable to other populations or contexts?" [6]

  1. Sample: only 6 students from one class at one international school, who probably all have a device and fast internet, so "saves time" may not transfer to schools where students share devices.
  2. Context: the worry about "being accused of cheating" depends on this school's AI rules; where AI is allowed for homework, the theme may vanish. The tools also change fast.
  3. Balance: the findings could transfer to similar schools with similar rules, if the researcher describes her sample and school in enough detail for readers to judge the match.
  4. Judgement: transferable only to a limited extent; interviews at several kinds of school would show which themes are shared.
Why this step? Why quote "6 students" and "accused of cheating"? (write two key words, then open)

The 5-6 band needs "explicit" reference to the source: quoting details shows it.

Finish it. Made up: a researcher who works for a fitness-tracker company ran two focus groups with 5 members each of one sports club about using fitness trackers, and coded the transcripts herself. Question: "Discuss how the researcher could avoid bias." [6]
  1. Done for you: researcher bias. She works for a tracker company, so she may notice positive comments more. She should state this interest (reflexivity) and have an independent second researcher code the transcripts too.
  2. Your turn: one point about participant bias in the focus groups, and a judgement.
Answer

Participant bias: club members may say what sounds sporty or follow one strong voice. She could have everyone write their views before the discussion and add one-to-one interviews to compare. Judgement: this reduces bias but cannot remove it, because she still chose the questions and the club.

Exam traps. (1) "Discuss" or "to what extent": more than one point and a judgement. (2) Credibility is not reliability or sample size: it is whether the themes show what participants meant. (3) A strategy with no link to the source ("use triangulation") stays in the 1-2 band.

Written items (on paper, then open the model)

W1 [3] Made-up source: a bar chart shows that 22% of students who used an AI tutor reported high anxiety, against 31% of students who did not. Students chose for themselves whether to use the tutor. Explain one issue that limits the interpretation of the data.

Show model answer

Students chose whether to use the tutor, so the groups may have differed before (identify): calmer students may have been keener to try a new tool (describe). So the 9-point gap could come from who chose the tutor, not the tutor itself; the graph cannot show that it lowers anxiety (explain).

Markband: 1 issue identified; 2 clearly described; 3 explained (what it does to the conclusion). Only the first issue counts.

W2 [2] Made-up: sleep before a test (hours), phone in bedroom: median 6.5, SIQR 1.5; no phone: median 7.5, SIQR 0.5. State one conclusion and support it with the numbers.

Show model answer

Students without a phone in the bedroom typically slept longer, median 7.5 against 6.5 hours (1 mark), and their sleep was more consistent, SIQR 0.5 against 1.5 (1 mark).

Checklist: a clear conclusion; both medians or both SIQRs quoted; no "proves".

W3 [2] A researcher interviewed 8 nurses about night shifts and found the theme "feeling invisible to managers". Explain one way she could improve the credibility of this finding.

Show model answer

Member checking: show the nurses the theme "feeling invisible to managers" and ask whether it matches what they meant (1 mark), so it represents their views, not her reading of them (1 mark). An explained triangulation point also earns both marks.

Checklist: strategy named; linked to this source; says how it makes the finding truer to the participants.
