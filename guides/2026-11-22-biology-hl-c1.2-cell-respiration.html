Energy stored in molecules

Food molecules such as glucose hold chemical energy in the bonds between their atoms. When a big molecule is broken down into small ones (here carbon dioxide and water), energy is released.

A cell cannot release all of glucose's energy in one go: it would be wasted as heat. So it breaks glucose down in many small steps and catches the energy in small packets, as ATP. Think of changing a large banknote into coins you can actually spend.

Energy is never made or destroyed, only moved from one form or molecule to another. "Releasing energy" means moving it out of a molecule so the cell can use it.

Cells, organelles and the mitochondrion

A cell is surrounded by a thin membrane. Inside is the watery cytoplasm, with small structures called organelles, each with a job.

The mitochondrion (plural mitochondria) is the organelle where most respiration happens. It has two membranes: a smooth outer one and a folded inner one. The fluid inside the inner membrane is the matrix.

Muscle cells have many mitochondria because they need a lot of energy. Bacteria have none, and red blood cells lose theirs.

Enzymes and pathways

An enzyme is a protein that speeds up one particular reaction. Respiration is a pathway: a chain of reactions where the product of one step is the starting molecule of the next, and each step has its own enzyme.

Because every step needs an enzyme, anything that affects enzymes (temperature, pH, the amount of starting molecule) also changes how fast respiration goes. You learnt this in C1.1 Enzymes and metabolism.

Glucose, fatty acids and counting carbons

Glucose is a sugar with the formula C6H12O6: 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 6 oxygen atoms. Biologists label molecules by their number of carbon atoms: glucose is 6C, pyruvate is 3C, an acetyl group is 2C.

Counting carbons is a useful check: carbon atoms are never lost, so the 6 carbons of one glucose must end up somewhere (in two 3C pyruvates, and finally in 6 carbon dioxide molecules).

Fatty acids are long chains of carbon and hydrogen with very little oxygen. They come from fats (B1.1).

Oxidation and reduction

In biology, oxidation means a molecule loses electrons; reduction means it gains electrons. A memory trick: OIL RIG, Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.

A hydrogen atom is one proton plus one electron. So when hydrogen atoms are taken off a molecule, it loses electrons: it is oxidised. The molecule that takes the hydrogen gains electrons: it is reduced.

The two always happen together, because the electrons have to go somewhere.

Gas, pressure and volume

Gas particles move about and push on the walls of their container: this push is pressure. If gas is removed from a closed tube (for example oxygen used up by living things), there are fewer particles, so the pressure inside falls.

A liquid in a tube connected to it then gets pushed towards the side with the lower pressure. Warming a gas makes it expand and push harder, which is why temperature must be kept constant when you measure gas changes.

Circles, volumes and units

The radius r of a circle is the distance from the centre to the edge; the diameter is twice that, right across. The area of a circle is π r2 (π ≈ 3.14; use the π key on your calculator).

A thin tube is a cylinder: its volume is the area of its end times its length, π r2 × length.

Units: 1 cm = 10 mm. mm3 is a cubic millimetre, a tiny volume. A power of −1 means "per": mm3 min−1 is cubic millimetres per minute, and g−1 is per gram.

C1.2.1 to C1.2.5 · SL and HL

ATP and the two kinds of respiration

Cells cannot use the energy in glucose directly. Respiration moves it into ATP, and ATP hands it out, a little at a time, wherever it is needed.

By the end of this section I can

C1.2.1 ATP, the energy currency

ATP is adenosine triphosphate, a nucleotide: adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups.Learn Why it suits the job (your notes):

  • hydrolysis of one ATP releases a small, manageable amount of energy, enough for one task;
  • it is soluble, so it moves easily through the cytoplasm;
  • it cannot cross the plasma membrane, so the energy stays in the cell that made it.

ATP is not a long-term store: a cell holds little ATP and recycles it constantly. Fats, starch and glycogen are the stores.

C1.2.2 What ATP is used for

  • Active transport: pumping ions or molecules across a membrane against their concentration gradient, for example the sodium-potassium pump.Learn
  • Anabolism: building macromolecules by condensation: proteins, DNA and RNA, starch and glycogen.
  • Movement: muscle contraction, cilia and flagella, chromosomes moving in cell division.

C1.2.3 ATP and ADP

ATP + water → ADP + phosphate   releases energy

ADP + phosphate → ATP + water   needs energy

ADP is adenosine diphosphate. Hydrolysis of the third phosphate releases energy; adding it back (phosphorylation) needs energy, which comes from respiration.Learn No kilojoule values are needed. Phosphate is often written Pi.

C1.2.4 Cell respiration

Cell respiration is the controlled release of energy from organic (carbon) compounds in cells, to produce ATP.Learn The main respiratory substrates are glucose and fatty acids; other carbon compounds, such as amino acids, can also be used. It is a system: many steps, each with its own enzyme.

Exam trap: respiration is not breathing. Gas exchange is oxygen and carbon dioxide moving between an organism and its surroundings. Cell respiration is chemical reactions inside every cell. Write "cell respiration", never "breathing". And ATP distributes energy; do not say it "stores" it.

Muscles use anaerobic respiration when they need ATP faster than oxygen can arrive: it is quick but short, because lactate builds up.

Worked example: a 4-mark comparison. "Distinguish between aerobic and anaerobic cell respiration in humans."
  1. Aerobic needs oxygen; anaerobic does not.
  2. Aerobic produces carbon dioxide and water; anaerobic produces lactate and no carbon dioxide.
  3. Aerobic gives a much larger ATP yield per glucose; anaerobic gives only 2.
  4. Aerobic happens in the cytoplasm and mitochondria; anaerobic only in the cytoplasm.
Why this step? Why does every line mention both kinds?

"Distinguish" asks for differences. A line about only one kind ("anaerobic makes lactate") does not show a difference, so it is only half a mark point.

C1.2.6 · SL and HL

The rate of cell respiration

You cannot see respiration, but you can measure the oxygen it uses up.

By the end of this section I can

Variables that change the rate

  • Temperature, pH and glucose concentration: respiration is enzyme-controlled.
  • Oxygen: more oxygen, more aerobic respiration.
  • Size and activity: per gram, small or active organisms respire faster.

How a respirometer works

  1. The organisms take in oxygen and give out carbon dioxide.Learn
  2. KOH or soda lime (an alkali) absorbs the carbon dioxide, so only the oxygen uptake changes the gas volume.
  3. The gas volume and the pressure in the tube fall.
  4. The coloured fluid moves towards the organisms. Distance moved in a set time shows the volume of oxygen used.

Good practice: a water bath keeps temperature constant (warm gas expands); a control tube with glass beads of the same volume shows movement not caused by respiration; let the apparatus settle first; weigh the organisms so different ones can be compared; repeat (your notes: at least five times). Use organisms that are not harmed, such as seeds, and keep them away from the alkali.

Calculating the rate

volume of oxygen = π r2 dLearn

rate = volume ÷ time, for example mm3 min−1Learn

r is the inside radius of the capillary tube (half the diameter), d the distance the fluid moved, both in mm. To compare organisms of different size, also divide by mass: mm3 g−1 min−1.

Worked example. 5.0 g of germinating peas; capillary internal diameter 1.0 mm; the fluid moves 2.4 cm in 10 minutes. Calculate the rate of oxygen uptake per gram.
  1. Radius = 1.0 ÷ 2 = 0.5 mm.
  2. Distance: 2.4 cm = 24 mm.
  3. Volume = π × 0.52 × 24 = π × 0.25 × 24 = 18.85 mm3.
  4. Rate = 18.85 ÷ 10 = 1.885, so 1.9 mm3 min−1 (2 significant figures, like the data).
  5. Per gram: 1.885 ÷ 5.0 = 0.377, so 0.38 mm3 g−1 min−1.
Why this step? Why halve the diameter first?

The formula needs the radius. Using the diameter gives π × 1.02 × 24 = 75.4 mm3, four times too big, because the radius is squared.

Finish it 2. Capillary internal radius 0.4 mm; the fluid moves 1.5 cm in 5 minutes. Rate in mm3 min−1?
  1. Distance = 1.5 cm = 15 mm.
  2. Volume = π × 0.42 × 15 = ...
  3. Rate = ...
Show the answer

Volume = π × 0.16 × 15 = 7.54 mm3. Rate = 7.54 ÷ 5 = 1.51, so 1.5 mm3 min−1.

Exam trap. Diameter instead of radius; cm mixed with mm; no units or no "per minute". Show every line of working: the mark scheme gives a mark for working alone.
Self-check 2 (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. Woodlice A (0.60 g) take in 3.6 mm3 of oxygen per minute; woodlice B (2.4 g) take in 7.2 mm3. Which respires faster per gram?
  2. Why a water bath, and why glass beads in the control tube?
  3. Why does the fluid hardly move if there is no KOH?

Answers. 1: A: 3.6 ÷ 0.60 = 6.0; B: 7.2 ÷ 2.4 = 3.0 mm3 g−1 min−1. A is twice as fast per gram. 2: temperature changes make gas expand or contract; the beads fill the same volume, so the control shows movement not caused by respiration. 3: in aerobic respiration of glucose, the volume of carbon dioxide given out equals the volume of oxygen taken in (6 O2 in, 6 CO2 out), so the gas volume hardly changes.

NAD, glycolysis and anaerobic respiration HL

Respiration takes hydrogen off glucose step by step. NAD carries it away; what happens to NAD decides whether the cell can go on.

C1.2.7 Oxidation, reduction and NAD

Oxidation is loss of electrons; reduction is gain of electrons.Learn They always happen together (a redox reaction). In respiration, substrates are oxidised by removing hydrogen (each hydrogen atom carries an electron): dehydrogenation. The coenzyme NAD accepts the hydrogen and is reduced, becoming reduced NAD (NADH).Learn

NAD + 2H → reduced NAD

Reduced NAD carries the hydrogen to the electron transport chain, where it is oxidised back to NAD.

C1.2.8 Glycolysis

In the cytoplasm, no oxygen needed, the first stage of both kinds of respiration. Many steps, each with a different enzyme:Learn

  1. Phosphorylation: 2 ATP add two phosphates to glucose (6C).
  2. Lysis: it splits into two 3-carbon sugars (triose phosphate).
  3. Oxidation: hydrogen removed from each, picked up by NAD: 2 reduced NAD.
  4. ATP formation: each becomes pyruvate (3C), making 2 ATP each: 4 ATP.

No other names of in-between molecules are needed.

Worked example: net ATP from glycolysis.
  1. ATP used: 2.
  2. ATP made: 2 per 3-carbon sugar × 2 sugars = 4.
  3. Net = 4 − 2 = 2 ATP per glucose, plus 2 reduced NAD and 2 pyruvate.
Why this step? Why subtract the 2 ATP?

"Net" means what the cell gains after paying back what it used. Your notes list forgetting this as a common error.

Finish it 3. A muscle cell respires 5 glucose molecules anaerobically. How many ATP are made, used and gained?
  1. Made: 4 per glucose × 5 = 20.
  2. Used: ...
  3. Net gain: ...
Show the answer

Used: 2 × 5 = 10. Net: 20 − 10 = 10 ATP (2 per glucose). Turning pyruvate into lactate adds no ATP.

C1.2.9 Why make lactate?

Glycolysis turns NAD into reduced NAD, and a cell has little NAD. Without oxygen the electron transport chain cannot turn it back. So muscle cells give the hydrogen from reduced NAD to pyruvate: pyruvate is reduced to lactate and NAD is regenerated, so glycolysis can go on making its net 2 ATP per glucose.Learn

C1.2.10 Yeast

Yeast runs the same glycolysis but regenerates NAD differently: pyruvate loses carbon dioxide and the rest is reduced to ethanol.Learn

glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide   (yeast)

  • Baking: carbon dioxide bubbles make dough rise; the ethanol evaporates in the oven.
  • Brewing: ethanol is the alcohol; fermentation stops when ethanol reaches a level toxic to the yeast.
Exam trap. Humans and yeast differ only after pyruvate. Glycolysis, the 2 ATP and the reason (regenerating NAD) are the same.
Self-check 3 HL (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. When NAD picks up hydrogen from a 3-carbon sugar, which is oxidised and which reduced?
  2. The four events of glycolysis, in order.
  3. Why would a muscle cell without oxygen soon make no ATP if it could not make lactate?

Answers. 1: the sugar loses hydrogen, so it is oxidised; NAD gains it, so it is reduced. 2: phosphorylation, lysis, oxidation, ATP formation. 3: NAD would all become reduced NAD with no way back, so glycolysis would stop.

C1.2.11 to C1.2.17 · HL only

Inside the mitochondrion HL

With oxygen, every carbon of glucose leaves as carbon dioxide, every hydrogen goes to the electron transport chain, and that energy makes most of the ATP.

By the end of this section I can

The mitochondrion

Two membranes. The inner membrane is folded into cristae (large surface area for the chain and ATP synthase). Between the membranes is the narrow intermembrane space, where protons are pumped. Inside is the matrix, with the enzymes of the link reaction and the Krebs cycle.Learn

C1.2.11 The link reaction

Pyruvate (3C) enters the matrix. For each pyruvate:Learn

  1. Decarboxylation: carbon dioxide is removed (3C to 2C).
  2. Oxidation: hydrogen is removed; NAD is reduced.
  3. The 2-carbon acetyl group joins coenzyme A: acetyl CoA carries it to the Krebs cycle.

Fatty acids are also broken into acetyl groups that join here, as acetyl CoA.

C1.2.12 The Krebs cycle

In the matrix. Acetyl (2C) joins oxaloacetate (4C) to make citrate (6C). Citrate is changed step by step back into oxaloacetate. Each turn has:Learn

  • two decarboxylations (2 carbon dioxide);
  • four oxidations (dehydrogenations): 3 reduced NAD and 1 reduced FAD (FAD is a second hydrogen carrier);
  • 1 ATP.

Citrate and oxaloacetate are the only names you need.

Worked example: one glucose, after glycolysis. One glucose gives 2 pyruvate, so 2 link reactions and 2 turns.
  1. Krebs: 2 × 2 = 4 CO2, 2 × 3 = 6 reduced NAD, 2 × 1 = 2 reduced FAD, 2 × 1 = 2 ATP (as in your notes).
  2. Add the link reaction (2 CO2, 2 reduced NAD): CO2 = 2 + 4 = 6, matching C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O.
  3. Reduced NAD in all: 2 (glycolysis) + 2 (link) + 6 (Krebs) = 10, plus 2 reduced FAD.
Why this step? Why multiply by 2?

Glycolysis splits one glucose into two pyruvates, so everything after it happens twice per glucose.

Finish it 4. Count for one pyruvate through the link reaction and one Krebs turn.
  1. Carbon dioxide: 1 (link) + 2 (Krebs) = 3.
  2. Reduced NAD: ...
  3. ATP made directly: ...
Show the answer

Reduced NAD: 1 + 3 = 4 (plus 1 reduced FAD). ATP: 1. Check: 3 CO2 for a 3-carbon pyruvate.

C1.2.13 and C1.2.14 Electron transport chain and proton gradient

  1. Reduced NAD (from glycolysis, link reaction and Krebs cycle) gives a pair of electrons to the first carrier protein in the inner membrane, and becomes NAD again.Learn
  2. Electrons pass from carrier to carrier, losing energy at each step.
  3. That energy pumps protons (H+) from the matrix into the intermembrane space.
  4. High proton concentration outside, low in the matrix: a proton gradient.Learn

C1.2.15 Chemiosmosis

Protons can only cross the inner membrane through ATP synthase. They flow down their gradient from the intermembrane space into the matrix, through ATP synthase: chemiosmosis. ATP synthase uses this energy to phosphorylate ADP to ATP. This is oxidative phosphorylation.Learn

Exam trap. Say protons flow through ATP synthase, not just "through the membrane". Pumped into the intermembrane space; flow back into the matrix. Give exact places: "on the inner membrane (cristae)", "in the matrix", never just "in the mitochondrion".

C1.2.16 Oxygen

Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor: it takes the electrons from the last carrier plus protons from the matrix, forming water.Learn This keeps electrons flowing. Without oxygen the chain stops, no gradient is made, reduced NAD is not turned back into NAD, and the link reaction and Krebs cycle stop too.

C1.2.17 Lipids against carbohydrates

  • Lipids release more energy per gram (your notes: about twice as much), because they contain less oxygen and more hydrogen and carbon to oxidise.Learn
  • Fatty acids are broken into 2C acetyl groups that enter as acetyl CoA; they do not go through glycolysis.
  • So only carbohydrate can be respired anaerobically: lipids need the Krebs cycle and the chain, so oxygen.

  1. What is decarboxylation, and in which two stages does it happen?
  2. A poison blocks ATP synthase but not the carriers. What happens to the proton concentration in the intermembrane space, and to ATP production?

Quick quiz

Decide before you click. Questions 5 to 7 and written 9 and 10 are HL. Got one wrong? Close the page, redo that section's self-check, and write the rule in the Error log.

1. Which property makes ATP suitable for handing out energy inside a cell?

Small amount per molecule, right for one task. ATP is not a long-term store (fats and glycogen are), it cannot cross the plasma membrane, and it is a nucleotide, not a 6-carbon sugar.

2. Which statement about anaerobic respiration in human muscle is correct?

Cytoplasm only, lactate, no carbon dioxide (that is yeast). Glucose only, and only 2 ATP.

3. A capillary tube has an internal radius of 0.5 mm. The fluid moves 18 mm in 6 minutes. What is the rate of oxygen uptake?

π × 0.52 × 18 = 14.14 mm3; ÷ 6 = 2.36, so 2.4. 9.4 uses the diameter (1.0) as r; 14.1 is the volume, not divided by time; 0.75 forgets π.

4. Why does a respirometer have a control tube with glass beads of the same volume as the organisms?

The control has everything except living things. KOH or soda lime absorbs carbon dioxide; the water bath keeps the temperature constant.

5. (HL) What is the net yield of glycolysis from one glucose?

4 made, 2 used: net 2, with 2 reduced NAD. Glycolysis releases no carbon dioxide. 30 to 38 is all of aerobic respiration.

6. (HL) During chemiosmosis in a mitochondrion, protons move

The chain pumps protons out into the intermembrane space; in chemiosmosis they flow back into the matrix through ATP synthase. They cannot pass through the phospholipids.

7. (HL) Why can fatty acids not be used in anaerobic respiration?

9. (HL) Outline the role of NAD in glycolysis and in anaerobic respiration in humans. [3]

Show model answer

Checklist, 1 mark each: NAD accepts hydrogen (is reduced) when triose phosphate is oxidised in glycolysis; reduced NAD is oxidised back to NAD when pyruvate is reduced to lactate; this regenerates NAD so glycolysis can continue (producing 2 ATP per glucose).
