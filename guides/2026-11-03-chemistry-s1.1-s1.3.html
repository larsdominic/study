SL, HL and AHL: what the labels mean

IB Chemistry is taught at two levels: SL (Standard Level) and HL (Higher Level). You take HL, so everything on this page is for you.

  • Parts with no label are for everyone, SL and HL.
  • Parts marked HL are the extra parts that only HL students learn and are tested on.
  • AHL means Additional Higher Level: the IB's own name for those extra HL parts. The class "AHL answer sheet" covers them.

Paper 1A is the multiple-choice part of the exam. The quiz at the end of this page is written in that style.

Atoms, elements and the periodic table

Everything is made of tiny atoms. An atom has a very small nucleus in the centre, made of protons and neutrons, with electrons moving around it.

An element is a substance with only one kind of atom. There are 118 of them, listed in the periodic table in order of their number of protons, the atomic number Z: hydrogen 1, helium 2, ... calcium 20, iron 26.

  • Rows are called periods (1 to 7).
  • Columns are called groups (1 to 18). Elements in the same group behave in similar ways.

Keep a periodic table open next to this page: you need it to look up Z.

Charges and ions

Protons are positive (+), electrons are negative (−), neutrons have no charge. Opposite charges attract and like charges repel. That attraction keeps the electrons around the nucleus.

An atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so it has no overall charge. If it loses electrons it becomes a positive ion; if it gains electrons it becomes a negative ion. The number of protons never changes.

Reading formulas and equations

A formula shows which atoms are in a substance. Small low numbers (subscripts) count atoms: H2O has 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom; Cl2 is a molecule of 2 chlorine atoms. No number means 1.

Small raised signs (superscripts) after a symbol show charge: Na+, O2−. A raised number in front, like 37Cl, is the mass number of one isotope.

An equation shows a change: starting substances, an arrow, then what forms. The letters in brackets are state symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water. NaCl(s) → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq) means solid salt dissolving in water to give separate ions.

Energy: moving and stored

Energy is measured in joules (J); 1 kJ = 1000 J. Two kinds come up today:

  • Kinetic energy is the energy of movement: Ek = ½mv², where m is mass and v is speed. If two particles have the same kinetic energy, the lighter one must be moving faster.
  • Potential energy is stored energy. Particles that attract each other gain potential energy when they are pulled apart, like a stretched rubber band.

Energy taken in is absorbed; energy given out is released. In an atom, an electron further from the nucleus has more energy, like a ball higher up a staircase: it can only sit on a step, never between steps.

Light as a wave

Light travels as a wave. Two numbers tell you about a wave:

  • Wavelength (λ, "lambda"): the distance from one peak to the next. For light it is tiny and given in nanometres (1 nm = 10−9 m). Red is about 700 nm, violet about 400 nm.
  • Frequency (f): how many waves pass a point each second, in hertz (Hz).

All light travels at the same speed, c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1, and c = λf. So a shorter wavelength means a higher frequency, and a higher frequency means more energy. Visible light is a small part of the electromagnetic spectrum: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays (longest wavelength to shortest).

Standard form and units

Very large and very small numbers are written in standard form: a number from 1 to 10 multiplied by a power of ten.

  • 6.02 × 1023 = 602 000 000 000 000 000 000 000
  • 9.116 × 10−8 = 0.000 000 091 16

To multiply, multiply the front numbers and add the powers: (2 × 103) × (3 × 105) = 6 × 108. To divide, divide the front numbers and subtract the powers. On a calculator use the ×10x (or EXP) key, not × 1 0 ^.

A power of −1 on a unit means "per": m s−1 is metres per second, mol−1 is per mole. J s means joules multiplied by seconds.

Electron shells: the Grade 9 and 10 picture

Before IB, electrons are drawn in circles (shells) around the nucleus. For the first 20 elements the shells fill 2, then 8, then 8, then the rest: sodium (11 electrons) is 2, 8, 1 and calcium (20) is 2, 8, 8, 2.

The electrons in the outermost shell decide how an element reacts. For groups 1 and 2 their number equals the group number: sodium has 1 outer electron and is in group 1.

S1.3 adds detail to this picture: each shell (now called a main energy level, n) is split into sublevels (s, p, d, f), and each sublevel into orbitals. Sodium's 2, 8, 1 becomes 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹: the same 2, then 2 + 6 = 8, then 1.

Averages and weighted averages

A normal average adds the values and divides by how many there are. A weighted average lets the more common values count more.

Example: a bag has 3 marbles of 10 g and 1 marble of 14 g. The average marble mass is (3 × 10 + 1 × 14) ÷ 4 = 11 g. It is closer to 10 g because most of the marbles are 10 g.

With percentages: 75% at 10 g and 25% at 14 g gives (75 × 10 + 25 × 14) ÷ 100 = 11 g, the same answer. This is exactly how relative atomic mass (Ar) is worked out from isotope masses and their percentage abundances.

1.1.3 Temperature and kinetic energy

Temperature in kelvin is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles. At 0 K (absolute zero) particles have minimum energy. The kelvin and the Celsius degree are the same size, so a temperature change is the same number in both scales; only the zero point differs.

T (K) = T (°C) + 273

25 °C = 298 K. 373 K = 100 °C. A rise of 20 °C is a rise of 20 K.

Because average kinetic energy depends only on temperature, two different gases at the same temperature have the same average kinetic energy. Since Ek = ½mv², the lighter particles must be moving faster on average. This is why hydrogen effuses faster than oxygen at the same temperature.

Exam trap. "Explain why the temperature stays constant during boiling" wants: energy is used to break intermolecular forces, so potential energy increases while average kinetic energy, and therefore temperature, stays the same.

1.2.1 Protons, neutrons, electrons and the nuclear symbol

An atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus of protons and neutrons (together called nucleons). Electrons occupy the space outside the nucleus. Almost all the mass is in the nucleus; almost all the volume is the electron region.

ParticleRelative massRelative chargeWhere
Proton1+1nucleus
Neutron10nucleus
Electron1/1836 (negligible)−1outside the nucleus
Worked example. 64Cu2+: Z = 29, so 29 protons; 64 − 29 = 35 neutrons; 29 − 2 = 27 electrons. 19F−: 9 protons, 10 neutrons, 10 electrons.

1.2.2 Isotopes and relative atomic mass

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A). They have the same chemical properties because chemical behaviour depends on the number and arrangement of electrons, which is identical. They have slightly different physical properties that depend on mass: density, rate of diffusion, boiling and melting point.

The relative atomic mass Ar is the weighted average mass of the atoms of an element on a scale where one atom of 12C is exactly 12. It is weighted by abundance, which is why it is usually not a whole number.

Ar = Σ (isotope mass × % abundance) ÷ 100
Worked example (from the S1.2 handout). Chlorine is 75.77% 35Cl and 24.23% 37Cl.
Ar = (35 × 75.77 + 37 × 24.23) ÷ 100 = (2652.0 + 896.5) ÷ 100 = 35.48 (to 4 significant figures with these masses; the data booklet value 35.45 uses exact isotopic masses).
Worked example, reversed: find the abundances. Lithium has Ar = 6.94 and isotopes of mass 6 and 7. Let the percentage of 7Li be x.
  1. 6(100 − x) + 7x = 6.94 × 100 = 694
  2. 600 − 6x + 7x = 694, so x = 94
  3. 7Li = 94%, 6Li = 6% (with whole-number masses; the exact masses give about 92.4% and 7.6%)
Quick check: the answer must sit closer to the mass of the more abundant isotope. 6.94 is close to 7, so 7Li must dominate. Yes. The AHL answer sheet does the same for chlorine (question 2(ii)).
Exam traps. (1) Ar is a weighted average, never a mass number; "chlorine atoms have mass 35.45" is wrong, no single atom does. (2) If the abundances are given as detector currents or ratios that do not add to 100, divide by their total instead of 100. (3) Keep 4 significant figures in the working; round only at the end.

1.2.3 Mass spectra HL

A mass spectrometer ionises a sample and sorts the ions by mass-to-charge ratio (m/z). You are not assessed on how the instrument works, only on reading the spectrum: each peak is an isotope (for a 1+ ion, m/z equals the isotope mass) and the peak height is its relative abundance.

Worked example (S1.2 handout, copper). Peak A at m/z 63 has height 5.2, peak B at 65 has height 2.3. The heights do not add to 100, so divide by their sum.
Ar(Cu) = (63 × 5.2 + 65 × 2.3) ÷ (5.2 + 2.3) = (327.6 + 149.5) ÷ 7.5 = 63.6
How to answer "explain the peak at m/z = 74". Two marks: (1) it is a Cl2+ molecule made of two 37Cl atoms; (2) its abundance is low because 37Cl is the less abundant isotope, so the probability of two 37Cl atoms in one molecule is small.

  2. Why do 35Cl and 37Cl react identically with sodium but diffuse at different rates?
  3. Boron is 19.9% 10B and 80.1% 11B. Calculate Ar.
  4. (HL) A mass spectrum of an element shows peaks at m/z 24 (79%), 25 (10%) and 26 (11%). Identify the element and calculate Ar.

1.3.1 Light, energy and emission spectra

Light is electromagnetic radiation. Across the spectrum, from radio waves to gamma rays, shorter wavelength means higher frequency and higher energy. Within visible light, red has the longest wavelength and lowest energy, violet the shortest wavelength and highest energy.

c = λf     E = hf     so   E = hc / λ

A continuous spectrum (white light through a prism) contains every wavelength with no gaps. A line emission spectrum contains only certain wavelengths, seen as separate coloured lines on a black background. Each element has its own line spectrum, which is how elements are identified in stars and in flame tests.

How the lines arise: an electron absorbs energy (heat, electricity) and is promoted to a higher energy level, an excited state. When it falls back to a lower level it emits a photon whose energy equals the difference between the two levels, E = hf. Because only certain levels exist, only certain photon energies, and so only certain frequencies, are emitted.

1.3.2 The hydrogen spectrum as evidence for energy levels

Why this is evidence for energy levels: if electrons could have any energy, every transition would be possible and the spectrum would be continuous. The fact that only discrete lines appear means the electron can only have certain fixed energies. The convergence of the lines shows that the levels themselves converge at higher energy.

Exam phrasing that scores. "Electrons in excited states fall to lower energy levels and emit photons of energy equal to the difference between the levels. Since the energy levels are discrete, only certain frequencies are emitted, giving a line spectrum. The lines converge because the energy levels converge." Four ideas, four marks.

Sublevel capacities: s holds 2, p holds 6, d holds 10, f holds 14. Within one main level the energy order is s < p < d < f. An orbital is a region of space where there is a high probability (about 90%) of finding an electron; each orbital holds at most two electrons. Orbitals in the same sublevel have the same energy and are called degenerate. The period number of an element equals the main energy level of its outermost electrons.

1.3.5 Writing electron configurations

Three rules decide where electrons go:

  1. Aufbau principle: electrons fill the lowest energy sublevel available first. Order up to Z = 36: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p. Note that 4s fills before 3d because it is lower in energy for the neutral atom.
  2. Pauli exclusion principle: an orbital holds at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spins (drawn as up and down arrows).
  3. Hund's rule: in degenerate orbitals (the three p or five d), electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before any orbital gets a second electron.

Ions

Negative ions: add electrons to the next available orbital. O2− is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, the same as neon. Positive ions of main-group elements: remove from the outermost sublevel. Al3+ is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶.

Transition metal ions lose their 4s electrons first, even though 4s filled first. Once the 3d orbitals are occupied they drop below 4s in energy. So Fe is [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ but Fe2+ is [Ar] 3d⁶ and Fe3+ is [Ar] 3d⁵. V2+ is [Ar] 3d³. Cu+ is [Ar] 3d¹⁰.

The three errors examiners see most. (1) Filling 3d before 4s for atoms, or removing 3d before 4s for ions. (2) Forgetting Cr and Cu. (3) Orbital diagrams that pair electrons too early, breaking Hund's rule. Check every configuration by adding the superscripts: they must equal the number of electrons.

Reading the periodic table as a filling map: the s block is groups 1 and 2 (plus He), the p block is groups 13 to 18, the d block is the transition metals, and the period number is the n of the s and p sublevels being filled. For a p-block element, the number of p electrons equals the group number minus 12.

1.3.6 Ionisation energy from the convergence limit, and the trends HL

In the hydrogen spectrum the lines of the series ending at n = 1 get closer together until they merge at the convergence limit. That limit corresponds to an electron falling from n = ∞, so the same energy, read the other way, is the energy needed to remove the electron from n = 1 completely: the first ionisation energy.

Definition to learn word for word: the first ionisation energy is the minimum energy required to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions. Equation: M(g) → M+(g) + e−. The state symbols (g) are compulsory.

Worked example (handout). The convergence limit of hydrogen is at 91.16 nm. Find the first ionisation energy in kJ mol−1.
  1. Convert to metres: 91.16 nm = 9.116 × 10−8 m.
  2. f = c ÷ λ = 3.00 × 108 ÷ 9.116 × 10−8 = 3.29 × 1015 Hz.
  3. E = hf = 6.63 × 10−34 × 3.29 × 1015 = 2.18 × 10−18 J. This is for one atom.
  4. Per mole: 2.18 × 10−18 × 6.02 × 1023 = 1.31 × 106 J mol−1 = 1.31 × 10³ kJ mol−1.
If the question gives a frequency instead, skip step 2. Sodium: convergence limit 1.24 × 1015 Hz gives E = 8.22 × 10−19 J per atom, 495 kJ mol−1.

Trends in first ionisation energy

Three factors control how strongly the outer electron is held: the nuclear charge, the distance from the nucleus, and the shielding by inner electrons.

  • Across a period, IE increases. Nuclear charge increases by one proton each step, the outer electrons are in the same main level so shielding is about the same, so the electrons are pulled closer and held more strongly.
  • Down a group, IE decreases. The outer electron is in a higher main level, further from the nucleus and shielded by more inner shells; the extra nuclear charge is cancelled by the extra shielding.
  • Two dips across a period. Group 13 is lower than group 2 (B < Be, Al < Mg): the electron removed comes from a p sublevel, which is higher in energy and slightly more shielded than the s. Group 16 is lower than group 15 (O < N, S < P): the electron removed is the first paired one in a p orbital, and the repulsion between the pair makes it easier to remove.

1.3.7 Successive ionisation energies HL

Removing electrons one after another from the same atom gives successive ionisation energies. Each one is larger than the last, because the electron is being removed from an increasingly positive ion with less electron-electron repulsion. A large jump appears when the next electron has to come from an inner main energy level: closer to the nucleus and much less shielded.

The position of the first big jump gives the group: the number of electrons removed before the jump equals the number of outer electrons.

Worked example. An element has successive IEs (kJ mol−1) 578, 1817, 2745, 11578, 14831. The first big jump is between the 3rd and 4th values, so three electrons are in the outer level: group 13. (It is aluminium.)

Ten questions, Paper 1A style

Quick quiz

Click an answer. Wrong answers explain the trap. Write any you miss into the Error log.

1. Which statement about a compound is correct?

A compound has a fixed ratio of chemically bonded atoms and properties different from its elements. Variable composition and physical separation describe mixtures.

2. During boiling, the temperature of a pure liquid stays constant because

Energy is absorbed and goes into potential energy (separating particles). Average kinetic energy, and so temperature, is unchanged.

3. Which temperature change equals a change of 15 K?

A kelvin and a Celsius degree are the same size, so a change of 15 K is a change of 15 °C. Only absolute temperatures need the 273 shift.

4. How many neutrons and electrons are in 56Fe3+? (Z = 26)

Neutrons = 56 − 26 = 30. A 3+ ion has lost three electrons: 26 − 3 = 23. Protons never change when an ion forms.

5. Isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties because they have the same

Chemistry is decided by the electrons. Isotopes differ in neutrons and therefore in mass and the physical properties that depend on it.

6. An element is 60.0% isotope mass 69 and 40.0% isotope mass 71. Its relative atomic mass is

(69 × 60 + 71 × 40) ÷ 100 = (4140 + 2840) ÷ 100 = 69.8. The answer must lie closer to the more abundant isotope. (This is gallium.)

7. In the hydrogen emission spectrum, the lines converge at high frequency because

Levels converge at higher energy, so transitions from high n differ less and less in energy; at the limit the electron is removed (ionisation).

8. The condensed electron configuration of Cr (Z = 24) is

Chromium is one of the two exceptions you must know: a half-filled d sublevel with one 4s electron. Copper is the other: [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰.

9. Which orbital diagram for the 2p sublevel of carbon is correct?

Hund's rule: the two p electrons go into separate degenerate orbitals with parallel spins. Pairing early (option 1) and opposite spins in separate orbitals (option 2) both lose the mark.

10. (HL) Which element has successive ionisation energies of 738, 1451, 7733, 10540 kJ mol−1?

The big jump is between the 2nd and 3rd values, so two outer electrons: group 2. The values match magnesium.
