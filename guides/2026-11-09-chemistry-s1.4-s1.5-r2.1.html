Counting particles by mass: the mole

Guiding question: how do we quantify matter on the atomic scale?

By the end of this section I can

1.4.1 The mole and the Avogadro constant

The mole (mol) is the SI unit of amount of substance. One mole contains exactly NA elementary entities. An elementary entity is whatever you are counting: an atom, a molecule, an ion, an electron, or a specified group of particles such as a formula unit of NaCl. Always say which.

NA = 6.02 × 1023 mol−1 In booklet
N = n × NA (N = number of particles, n = amount in mol) Learn
Worked example 1. How many hydrogen atoms are there in 0.0500 mol of methane, CH4?
  1. Molecules first: N(CH4) = 0.0500 × 6.02 × 1023 = 3.01 × 1022 molecules.
  2. Each molecule has 4 H atoms: N(H) = 4 × 3.01 × 1022 = 12.04 × 1022 = 1.20 × 1023 H atoms. (When 12.04 × 1022 becomes 1.204 × 1023, the power goes up by one: check it every time.)
Going the other way: 1.50 × 1024 water molecules is n = 1.50 × 1024 ÷ 6.02 × 1023 = 2.49 mol.
Exam trap. Read which entity is asked: molecules, atoms of one element, all atoms, or ions. 0.0500 mol of CH4 has 3.01 × 1022 molecules but five times as many atoms in total.

1.4.2 Relative atomic mass and relative formula mass

Masses of atoms are compared on a scale relative to carbon-12 (one 12C atom has a relative mass of exactly 12). Ar is the weighted average relative mass of an element's atoms; Mr is the sum of the Ar values of all the atoms in the formula. Both are ratios, so they have no units. Use the data booklet Ar values to two decimal places.

Worked example 2. Mr of calcium nitrate, Ca(NO3)2, and of ammonium sulfate, (NH4)2SO4.
  1. Ca(NO3)2: the bracket doubles everything inside it, so 1 Ca, 2 N, 6 O. 40.08 + 2(14.01 + 3 × 16.00) = 40.08 + 2 × 62.01 = 164.10.
  2. (NH4)2SO4: 2 N, 8 H, 1 S, 4 O. 2(14.01 + 4 × 1.01) + 32.07 + 4 × 16.00 = 36.10 + 32.07 + 64.00 = 132.17.

1.4.3 Molar mass and n = m/M

The molar mass M is the mass of one mole, in g mol−1. It has the same number as Mr (water: Mr = 18.02, M = 18.02 g mol−1).

n = mM   (n in mol, m in g, M in g mol−1) In booklet
Worked example 3.
  1. Mass of 0.150 mol of (NH4)2SO4: m = n × M = 0.150 × 132.17 = 19.83 g, so 19.8 g (3 significant figures, like the data).
  2. Amount in 5.00 g of CaCO3: M = 40.08 + 12.01 + 48.00 = 100.09 g mol−1; n = 5.00 ÷ 100.09 = 0.0500 mol.
  3. Mass of one water molecule: one mole (6.02 × 1023 molecules) has mass 18.02 g, so one molecule has 18.02 ÷ 6.02 × 1023 = 2.99 × 10−23 g.

1.4.4 Empirical and molecular formulas

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound. The molecular formula is the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule, and is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula (H2O2 is (HO)2; glucose C6H12O6 is (CH2O)6). Ionic and giant structures have only an empirical formula.

Percentage by mass from a formula Learn: % element = (number of atoms × Ar) ÷ Mr × 100. Example: % N in NH4NO3 (Mr = 80.06) = 2 × 14.01 ÷ 80.06 × 100 = 35.0%.

1.4.5 Molar concentration

Concentration is the amount of solute per volume of solution (not per volume of solvent). The guide requires square brackets for molar concentration: [NaCl] = 0.10 mol dm−3. You must use both units, mol dm−3 and g dm−3, and convert between them.

n = CV   (C in mol dm−3, V in dm3) In booklet
g dm−3 = mol dm−3 × M Learn   1 dm3 = 1000 cm3 In booklet
Worked example 5. 2.00 g of NaOH is dissolved in water and made up to 250 cm3 of solution.
  1. M(NaOH) = 22.99 + 16.00 + 1.01 = 40.00 g mol−1; n = 2.00 ÷ 40.00 = 0.0500 mol.
  2. V = 250 cm3 = 0.250 dm3; [NaOH] = 0.0500 ÷ 0.250 = 0.200 mol dm−3.
  3. In g dm−3: 2.00 g ÷ 0.250 dm3 = 8.00 g dm−3 (check: 0.200 × 40.00 = 8.00).
  4. Dilution: 10.0 cm3 of 2.00 mol dm−3 made up to 250 cm3. The moles do not change: n = 2.00 × 0.0100 = 0.0200 mol, so the new concentration is 0.0200 ÷ 0.250 = 0.0800 mol dm−3.
Exam trap. Convert cm3 to dm3 (÷ 1000) before using n = CV. And for ions, count them: [Cl−] in 0.10 mol dm−3 MgCl2 is 0.20 mol dm−3, because each MgCl2 gives two Cl− ions.

1.4.6 Avogadro's law

Avogadro's law Learn: equal volumes of all gases, measured at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules. So for gases at the same T and P, the volume ratio is the mole ratio of the equation, and you can work in cm3 without converting to moles.

Worked example 6. 20 cm3 of propane is burned in 150 cm3 of oxygen. All volumes are measured at room temperature and pressure, where water is a liquid. Find the volume and composition of the gas at the end.
  1. Equation: C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) → 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(l).
  2. 20 cm3 of propane needs 5 × 20 = 100 cm3 of O2. Oxygen is in excess: 150 − 100 = 50 cm3 left over.
  3. CO2 formed: 3 × 20 = 60 cm3. The water is liquid, so its volume is negligible.
  4. Final gas: 50 cm3 O2 + 60 cm3 CO2 = 110 cm3.
Exam trap. Volume ratio = mole ratio only for gases, at the same temperature and pressure. Check the state symbol of water: H2O(l) adds no volume, H2O(g) does. And remember the leftover excess reactant in the final volume.

Structure 1.5

Ideal gases

Guiding question: how does the model of ideal gas behaviour help us to predict the behaviour of real gases?

By the end of this section I can

1.5.1 The ideal gas model

An ideal gas is a model with these assumptions Learn:

  • the particles are in constant, random motion;
  • the particles have negligible volume compared with the volume of the container;
  • there are no intermolecular forces between the particles;
  • all collisions are elastic: no total kinetic energy is lost when particles collide.

1.5.2 Real gases

Real gases deviate from the model, most at low temperature and high pressure. No calculations are needed, only the explanation:

  • High pressure: the particles are pushed close together, so their own volume is no longer negligible compared with the container, and the attractions between them start to matter.
  • Low temperature: the particles move slowly, so the attractions between them have a bigger effect (the gas is closer to condensing).
  • Larger or more polar molecules deviate more: bigger particles take up more of the volume, and polar molecules attract each other more strongly.

A real gas behaves most like an ideal gas at high temperature and low pressure. Small non-polar particles such as helium come closest.

Exam trap. "Explain why a real gas deviates at high pressure" needs two points: the volume of the particles is no longer negligible, and the intermolecular forces are no longer negligible because the particles are close together. "Particles collide more" scores nothing.

1.5.3 Pressure, volume, temperature and the molar volume

For a fixed mass (fixed n) of ideal gas, each pair of variables is linked when the third is kept constant. Temperature must always be in kelvin: T (K) = T (°C) + 273.15 In booklet. Adding 273, as the handouts, the answer sheets and this page do, is accepted; at most it moves the last significant figure (quiz question 7 gives 3.32 or 3.33 dm3, the same option). The names of the individual gas laws are not assessed.

At a given temperature and pressure, one mole of any ideal gas has the same volume, the molar volume Vm. At STP:

Vm = 22.7 dm3 mol−1 at STP In booklet
STP = 273.15 K (0 °C) and 100 kPa In booklet   n = VVm Learn
Worked example 7. What volume does 4.40 g of CO2 occupy at STP?
  1. M(CO2) = 12.01 + 32.00 = 44.01 g mol−1; n = 4.40 ÷ 44.01 = 0.1000 mol.
  2. V = n × Vm = 0.1000 × 22.7 = 2.27 dm3.

1.5.4 The ideal gas equation and the combined gas law

PV = nRT   R = 8.31 J K−1 mol−1 In booklet
P1V1T1 = P2V2T2 In booklet

The guide says SI units only for PV = nRT Learn: P in pascals (Pa), V in cubic metres (m3), T in kelvin. Conversions: 1 kPa = 103 Pa; 1 m3 = 103 dm3 = 106 cm3, so cm3 × 10−6 = m3 and dm3 × 10−3 = m3. In the combined gas law the units only have to match on both sides, but T must still be in kelvin. Check: PV = nRT with n = 1, T = 273 K and P = 100 000 Pa gives V = 8.31 × 273 ÷ 100 000 = 0.0227 m3 = 22.7 dm3, the molar volume at STP.

Worked example 8 (combined gas law). 150 cm3 of gas at 27 °C and 101 kPa is heated to 87 °C and compressed to 202 kPa. Find the new volume.
  1. Kelvin: T1 = 300 K, T2 = 360 K.
  2. Rearrange: V2 = V1 × P1P2 × T2T1 = 150 × 101202 × 360300 = 90.0 cm3.
  3. Sense check: the pressure doubles (halves V) and the temperature rises by a factor of 1.2 (V × 1.2), so 150 × 0.5 × 1.2 = 90.
Worked example 9 (molar mass from PV = nRT). 0.500 g of a gas occupies 193 cm3 at 25 °C and 1.00 × 105 Pa. Find its molar mass and suggest what it is.
  1. SI units: V = 193 × 10−6 = 1.93 × 10−4 m3; T = 298 K; P = 1.00 × 105 Pa.
  2. n = PV ÷ RT = (1.00 × 105 × 1.93 × 10−4) ÷ (8.31 × 298) = 19.3 ÷ 2476 = 7.79 × 10−3 mol.
  3. M = m ÷ n = 0.500 ÷ 7.79 × 10−3 = 64.2 g mol−1, close to SO2 (64.07). (In one line: M = mRT ÷ PV.)
Exam trap. The three classic slips: temperature left in °C (27 °C is 300 K, and doubling 20 °C to 40 °C does not double anything); volume left in cm3 or dm3 with P in Pa; pressure in kPa with V in m3. Write the converted values in a list before substituting.

2.1.1 Writing and balancing equations

A balanced equation shows the mole ratio of reactants and products. Write correct formulas first, then balance only by changing the numbers in front (never the subscripts). Add state symbols: (s), (l), (g), (aq).

Worked example 10. Butane burns completely in oxygen.
  1. Skeleton: C4H10 + O2 → CO2 + H2O.
  2. Carbon: 4CO2. Hydrogen: 10 H, so 5H2O.
  3. Oxygen on the right: 8 + 5 = 13 atoms, so 132O2. Double everything to clear the fraction.
  4. 2C4H10(g) + 13O2(g) → 8CO2(g) + 10H2O(l). Count: C 8 = 8, H 20 = 20, O 26 = 26.
Order that works for most combustions: C, then H, then O last (oxygen appears alone as O2).

2.1.2 Reacting masses, volumes and concentrations

Every calculation follows the bridge in the picture at the top Learn:

  1. Moles of what you know: n = m/M, n = CV, n = V/Vm or n = PV/RT.
  2. Cross the bridge: n(B) = n(A) × ba, where A is the substance you know, B the one you want, and a and b are their coefficients in the equation.
  3. Convert to what the question asks: mass, gas volume or concentration.
Worked example 11. 0.486 g of magnesium reacts with hydrochloric acid: Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g). (a) What volume of hydrogen forms at STP? (b) What volume of 0.100 mol dm−3 HCl reacts exactly with the magnesium?
  1. n(Mg) = 0.486 ÷ 24.31 = 0.0200 mol.
  2. (a) Ratio Mg : H2 = 1 : 1, so n(H2) = 0.0200 mol; V = 0.0200 × 22.7 = 0.454 dm3 = 454 cm3.
  3. (b) Ratio Mg : HCl = 1 : 2, so n(HCl) = 0.0400 mol; V = n ÷ C = 0.0400 ÷ 0.100 = 0.400 dm3 = 400 cm3.
Worked example 12 (two solutions). 25.0 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 Na2CO3 reacts exactly with 22.40 cm3 of hydrochloric acid: Na2CO3(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). Find [HCl].
  1. n(Na2CO3) = 0.0250 × 0.100 = 2.50 × 10−3 mol.
  2. n(HCl) = 2 × 2.50 × 10−3 = 5.00 × 10−3 mol.
  3. [HCl] = 5.00 × 10−3 ÷ 0.02240 = 0.223 mol dm−3.
This is the calculation behind a titration; titration practice comes in a later session.
Exam trap. The ratio goes the right way round: wanted over given. For 2HCl per Mg you multiply moles of Mg by 2 to get HCl, and divide moles of HCl by 2 to get Mg. Write the ratio as words ("2 HCl for every 1 Mg") before you multiply.

2.1.3 The limiting reactant

The limiting reactant is used up first and decides the most product you can make, the theoretical yield. Every other reactant is in excess. Test Learn: divide the moles of each reactant by its coefficient in the equation; the smallest result is the limiting reactant. The experimental yield is what you actually obtain.

Worked example 13. 10.0 g of aluminium is heated with 35.0 g of chlorine: 2Al(s) + 3Cl2(g) → 2AlCl3(s). Find the limiting reactant, the theoretical yield, and the mass of excess reactant left.
  1. n(Al) = 10.0 ÷ 26.98 = 0.3706 mol; n(Cl2) = 35.0 ÷ 70.90 = 0.4937 mol.
  2. Divide by coefficients: Al 0.3706 ÷ 2 = 0.1853; Cl2 0.4937 ÷ 3 = 0.1646. Chlorine is smaller, so Cl2 is limiting, even though there are more moles of it.
  3. n(AlCl3) = 0.4937 × 23 = 0.3291 mol; M(AlCl3) = 26.98 + 3 × 35.45 = 133.33; theoretical yield = 0.3291 × 133.33 = 43.9 g.
  4. Al used = 0.3291 mol × 26.98 = 8.88 g, so 1.12 g of Al is left over.

2.1.4 Percentage yield

% yield = experimental yieldtheoretical yield × 100 Learn

Continuing worked example 13: if 38.5 g of AlCl3 is collected, % yield = 38.5 ÷ 43.9 × 100 = 87.7% (from unrounded 43.88 g). Use the same units top and bottom (both g or both mol).

Why the experimental yield is usually lower: the reaction is incomplete or reversible, side reactions make other products, and product is lost when transferring, filtering or purifying. Why it can come out higher than 100%: the product is still wet or contains impurities. A result above 100% always means an error, never a better reaction.

2.1.5 Atom economy

atom economy = molar mass of desired producttotal molar mass of all reactants × 100 In booklet

Use the coefficients of the balanced equation (2 mol of product counts twice). Atom economy measures how much of the starting material ends up in the product you want, so it is a measure of efficiency in green chemistry. The higher the atom economy, the less waste: the two are inversely related. Atom economy comes from the equation alone, so it does not depend on the yield: a reaction can have a 100% yield and still a low atom economy.

Worked example 14. Two routes to ethanol, C2H5OH (M = 46.08).
  1. Fermentation: C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2. Atom economy = 2 × 46.08 ÷ 180.18 × 100 = 51.1%. Nearly half the mass leaves as CO2.
  2. Hydration of ethene: C2H4 + H2O → C2H5OH. Reactants 28.06 + 18.02 = 46.08, all of it in the product: 100%. Any reaction with a single product (an addition reaction) has 100% atom economy.
Exam trap. In atom economy, include every reactant with its coefficient on the bottom, and only the desired product (with its coefficient) on top. Do not use the mass of products on the bottom, and do not confuse atom economy with percentage yield.

  3. 4.00 g of H2 and 16.0 g of O2 react: 2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(l). Which is limiting, and what mass of water can form?

Quick quiz (Paper 1A style)

Click an answer. Use a calculator and the values on this page, as you would the data booklet. Wrong answers explain the trap. Write any you miss into the Error log.

1. How many atoms in total are there in 0.100 mol of ammonia, NH3?

Each NH3 has 4 atoms: 4 × 0.100 × 6.02 × 1023 = 2.41 × 1023. A counts molecules only; C counts only the hydrogen atoms; D has the right digits but loses a power of ten.

2. What is the relative formula mass of magnesium nitrate, Mg(NO3)2?

24.31 + 2(14.01 + 48.00) = 148.33, with no units. A ignores the bracket (MgNO3); B doubles the oxygens but not the nitrogen; D is Mg(NO2)2.

3. A hydrocarbon is 85.6% carbon by mass and has a molar mass of 56.12 g mol−1. What is its molecular formula?

C 85.6 ÷ 12.01 = 7.13, H 14.4 ÷ 1.01 = 14.26: ratio 1 : 2, so the empirical formula is CH2 (M = 14.03). 56.12 ÷ 14.03 = 4, so C4H8. A stops at the empirical formula; C (58.14) and D (42.09) do not match the molar mass.

4. 50.0 cm3 of 0.200 mol dm−3 MgCl2(aq) is diluted with water to 250.0 cm3. What is the concentration of chloride ions in the diluted solution?

n(MgCl2) = 0.0500 × 0.200 = 0.0100 mol, so n(Cl−) = 0.0200 mol in 0.2500 dm3: 0.0800 mol dm−3. A forgets that each MgCl2 gives two Cl−; C forgets the dilution; D dilutes the wrong way (multiplies by 5).

5. 30 cm3 of methane is burned in 100 cm3 of oxygen: CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l). All volumes are measured at the same room temperature and pressure. What is the total volume of gas at the end?

30 cm3 CH4 uses 60 cm3 O2, leaving 40 cm3 O2, and makes 30 cm3 CO2: 40 + 30 = 70 cm3. A forgets the CO2; B counts the liquid water as 60 cm3 of gas; C counts CO2 and water but forgets the excess oxygen.

6. Under which conditions does a real gas behave most like an ideal gas?

Low pressure: particles far apart, so their volume and their attractions are negligible. High temperature: particles move fast, so attractions have little effect. C is the worst case: this is where real gases deviate most.

7. What volume does 0.200 mol of an ideal gas occupy at 27 °C and 150 kPa? (R = 8.31 J K−1 mol−1)

V = nRT ÷ P = 0.200 × 8.31 × 300 ÷ 150 000 = 3.32 × 10−3 m3 = 3.32 dm3. A uses 27 instead of 300 K; C uses P in kPa but treats the answer as m3; D uses 22.7 dm3 mol−1, which only applies at STP.

8. A fixed mass of gas has a volume of 2.0 dm3 at 300 K. The pressure is doubled and the temperature is raised to 450 K. What is the new volume?

V2 = 2.0 × 12 × 450300 = 1.5 dm3. A divides by the temperature factor instead of multiplying; B doubles the volume when the pressure doubles; D ignores the pressure change.

9. 0.30 mol of N2 and 0.60 mol of H2 react: N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g). 5.11 g of NH3 (M = 17.04 g mol−1) is obtained. What is the percentage yield?

Divide by coefficients: N2 0.30, H2 0.60 ÷ 3 = 0.20, so H2 is limiting. Theoretical NH3 = 0.60 × 23 = 0.40 mol = 6.82 g; 5.11 ÷ 6.82 × 100 = 75.0%. B treats N2 as limiting because it has fewer moles; C divides theoretical by experimental; D forgets the 2 in 2NH3. A yield over 100% is a warning sign.

10. Oxygen can be made by heating potassium chlorate: 2KClO3(s) → 2KCl(s) + 3O2(g). M(KClO3) = 122.55 g mol−1, M(O2) = 32.00 g mol−1. What is the atom economy for making oxygen?

3 × 32.00 ÷ (2 × 122.55) × 100 = 96.00 ÷ 245.10 × 100 = 39.2%. A ignores the 3 in front of O2; B is the share of the unwanted product KCl; C ignores both coefficients.
