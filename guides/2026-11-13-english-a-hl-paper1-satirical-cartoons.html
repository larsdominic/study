Literary and non-literary texts

Literary texts are novels, poems and plays, like Purple Hibiscus. Non-literary texts are everyday texts made to inform, persuade or entertain: adverts, speeches, blogs, photographs, cartoons.

Paper 1 uses only non-literary texts you have never seen, so you cannot learn answers by heart. You learn a method for any text. Today you practise it on cartoons.

What it shows and what it means

Every cartoon has two layers. Literal: what is there ("a man waters a small plant in front of smoking chimneys"). Implied: what you are meant to understand ("companies make small green gestures to hide big pollution").

A friend texting "Great, another Monday" literally praises Monday but implies the opposite. Marks come from explaining the implied layer and the details that lead you there.

Irony: when words and reality do not match

Irony is a gap between what is said or expected and what is true. Saying "lovely weather" in a storm is verbal irony. Cartoonists use it all the time: the speech bubble says one thing, the picture shows the opposite. Spot the gap and say what it reveals about the speaker. If you miss irony, you may think a cartoon agrees with the very thing it attacks.

Analysing is not retelling

Retelling: "Two women sit in a café and one shows a photo." Anyone can see that.

Analysing: "The phone sits exactly between the friends, so the device comes between them, suggesting technology divides people."

Test: after each piece of evidence, does your sentence go on with so, because or which suggests? If it stops at the evidence, it is still retelling.

Revision guide Step 1 · Know the form

Satire and the editorial cartoon

A satirical cartoon makes you laugh at something so that you judge it.

By the end of this section I can

Your workbook leaves these empty for you to research. Start here, then use your own words.

  • Satire: humour, irony or exaggeration used to criticise, usually the powerful. Mocking only laughs; satire laughs to show a fault.
  • Editorial cartoon: a newspaper opinion-page cartoon on a news issue, giving the cartoonist's view. A comic strip mainly amuses.
  • Horatian satire: gentle, amused; invites us to laugh, even at ourselves.
  • Juvenalian satire: harsh, angry; attacks serious harm (war, corruption).
  • Target: who or what is criticised. Aiming at the powerless can become bullying.
  • Context: what a reader must know for the joke to work.
Worked example: audience, purpose and target. Your revision guide's practice cartoon (described in words): a huge gold ship towers over a tiny wooden boat. A speech bubble says "We're all in the same boat!"
  1. Context: leaders in a crisis often say "we're all in the same boat", meaning everyone suffers equally.
  2. Target: rich, powerful people (the ship) who use that phrase.
  3. Purpose (the guide's words): "Exposes empty talk of unity".
  4. Audience: newspaper readers who have heard leaders say this.
Why this step? Why find the context first?

Without knowing that leaders use the phrase, the bubble is just a joke about boats. The context tells you who is speaking, and so who the target is.

Finish it. A cartoon shows a head teacher on a stage under a banner "Wellbeing Week!", handing one tiny cupcake to a long queue of exhausted students carrying huge piles of homework.
  1. Context: schools run wellbeing events while workloads stay high.
  2. Target:  
  3. Purpose:  
Answer

Target: school leaders who offer small treats instead of less work. Purpose: exposes "wellbeing" events as a token gesture. Gentle, so closer to Horatian.

Exam trap. "The cartoon is about money" is a topic, not a purpose. A purpose says what the cartoonist does to the target: exposes, ridicules, warns, criticises.
Self-check: write two key words for each, then open
  1. Gentle teasing of parents who film every school concert: Horatian or Juvenalian?
  2. A furious attack on a leader who allowed an oil spill: Horatian or Juvenalian?

Answers. 1: Horatian (gentle, laughs at us). 2: Juvenalian (angry, serious harm).

Effect verbs (from your guide) Learn word for word

Use them in place of "shows": exposes (shows something hidden), ridicules (makes it look foolish), undermines (weakens a claim), invites (encourages the reader to), positions (puts the reader on a side), implicates (shows someone is to blame), provokes (stirs a strong reaction), disarms (makes the reader drop their guard), reinforces (makes an idea stronger), challenges (questions an accepted idea).

Worked example: name it, then say what it does. A politician stands in floodwater up to his chest, smiling. Caption: "A bit of rain."
  1. Spot it: the caption "A bit of rain" under a flood.
  2. Explain it: understatement, placed next to a disaster (juxtaposition).
  3. Effect: the gap ridicules the politician and invites us to see him as out of touch.
Why this step? Why add a verb after naming the technique?

Naming earns little. The verb says what the choice does to the reader and the target, which is what Criterion B rewards.

  2. Explain it: understatement and verbal irony.

Answer

For example: the calm words undermine the boss and expose how bankers play down disasters they caused. Any fitting effect verb is right.

Revision guide Step 4

Colour

Colour carries meaning only in context.

By the end of this section I can

Avoid: "Red makes the reader angry." Write: "Here, red suggests danger because ..."Revision guide, Step 4 Learn word for word
  • Warm and cool: reds, oranges, yellows come forward (energy, heat, danger); blues, greens, purples sit back (calm, cold, distance).
  • Saturation: how strong or dull a colour is. Bright grabs attention; dull drains life.
  • Accent colour: one strong colour against grey pulls the eye to one thing.
  • Black and white: a newspaper tradition and a choice: serious, stark.
Worked example: weak to strong (the guide's "Spot the difference").
  1. Weak: "The cartoonist uses gold, which is a nice colour and makes the ship stand out."
  2. Strong: "The rich gold of the hull contrasts with the grey-blue sea, linking comfort to the ship and exposure to those below."
Why this step? What makes the second one strong?

It ties the colour to a contrast in this cartoon and to its point (who is safe, who is exposed), instead of a personal feeling ("nice").

Finish it. A grey city; one child holds a single red balloon. Complete: "The dull grey city makes the red balloon the only   in the frame, so  ; here, the red suggests  ."
Answer

"... the only accent colour in the frame, so the eye goes straight to it; here, the red suggests the hope or play that the rest of the city has lost." Any answer that names the contrast, says "here" and gives a reason is right.

Exam trap. "Blue is a calm colour" is a guess about every reader everywhere. Say what the blue does in this frame.
Extra, not needed today: why colour meaning changes

Your guide quotes research: many colour-feeling links are shared across countries but change with language and place, and context changes meaning (blue is good on a first-place ribbon but bad on meat). For context only.

Revision guide Step 5 · Build your analysis

The chain: from notes to a paragraph

Spot it, explain it, give the effect, link it to the purpose.

By the end of this section I can

Build every point in this order: spot it, explain it, effect on reader, purpose. Learn word for word Your revision guide's Step 5 table shows it for the ship cartoon.

1. Model paragraph

The cartoon (invented, described in words). A smiling businessman, drawn bigger than anything else, waters one tiny seedling in a pot labelled "OUR GREEN PROMISE". Behind him, factory chimneys pour black smoke into a grey sky. The seedling is the only green thing. He says: "We're going green!"
Guiding question: How does the cartoonist use visual and verbal features to criticise the company?
The cartoonist uses scale and an accent colour to expose the gap between the company's green image and its real impact. [claim] The businessman, drawn larger than anything else, kneels proudly over a single seedling, while behind him a wall of chimneys pours black smoke into a grey sky. [spot it] Because the seedling is the only touch of bright green in the frame, the reader's eye is drawn to it first and then pulled back to the smoke, so the gesture looks tiny and almost pointless. [explain + effect] His cheerful slogan, "We're going green!", becomes a pun: the only thing going green is one plant. [words with image] This irony ridicules companies that advertise small eco-friendly gestures to hide much larger pollution, and invites the reader to question such slogans. [purpose]
Why this step? Why does the claim come first?

It answers the guiding question straight away (Criterion C), so every sentence after it is evidence for one argument, not a list.

2. Paragraph frame: finish it on the ship cartoon

Finish it. Copy and fill the blanks on paper.

The cartoonist uses   and   to (effect verb)  . The huge gold ship  , while the tiny wooden boat  . Because  , the reader  . The speech bubble "We're all in the same boat!" becomes   because  . This (effect verb) leaders who  , and invites the reader to  .

Answer

The cartoonist uses scale and an ironic idiom to expose the false unity of the powerful. The huge gold ship towers over the frame, while the tiny wooden boat is pushed into its shadow. Because the gap in size is the first thing we see, the reader grasps the inequality before reading a word. The speech bubble "We're all in the same boat!" becomes verbal irony because the picture shows two very different boats. This undermines leaders who use the language of unity to hide inequality, and invites the reader to distrust such phrases.

Model summary in 5 sentences (open after writing yours)
  1. La fondation Art Fund, qui aide les musées britanniques, a sollicité le King's College pour mesurer scientifiquement l'effet de l'art sur le stress.
