What a business is for

A business exists to provide goods (things you can touch) or services (things done for you) that people want, and customers pay for them. If more money comes in than goes out, the owners earn a reward for their effort and their risk.

Not every organisation is after money for its owners. State schools, public hospitals and charities also use people, money and buildings to provide something. The differences are who owns them and what happens to any money left over: that is section 1.2.

Every business has the same four jobs to do: look after the people (human resources), the money (finance), the customers (marketing) and making the product (operations).

Revenue, costs and profit

Three money words you will use in every unit:

  • Revenue: all the money from sales. Price × number sold.
  • Costs: all the money paid out: materials, wages, rent, electricity, interest on loans. The everyday costs of running the business are its operating costs.
  • Profit = revenue − costs. If costs are bigger, it is a loss.

Example: a juice stall sells 500 cups at $3. Revenue = 500 × $3 = $1 500. Fruit, cups, a helper's pay and the pitch cost $1 100. Profit = 1 500 − 1 100 = $400.

Revenue is not profit. A firm with high sales can still lose money if its costs are even higher, and profit often falls in a recession, when customers spend less.

Owning a company: shares and shareholders

A company's ownership is split into equal parts called shares. Whoever holds shares is a shareholder: an owner of part of the company. Hold 10 of its 100 shares and you own 10% of it.

  • Shareholders usually get one vote per share, for example to choose the board of directors who make the big decisions for them.
  • Part of the profit may be paid out to them as a dividend.
  • Shares can be sold to someone else. The price of one share (the share price) rises when people expect the company to do well.

Selling new shares is a way for a company to raise money without borrowing it.

Borrowing, debts and who pays

A business often needs money before it earns any. It can use the owners' savings, sell shares, or take a loan: borrowed money that must be paid back, plus interest, an extra charge each year (for example 8% of what is owed).

Money still owed is a debt. Things the business owns, such as machines, a van or cash, are its assets. If a business closes owing money, its assets are sold to pay the debts.

The big question in 1.2: if that is not enough, does the owner have to pay the rest from their own pocket? That is what liability is about.

Government, laws and taxes

The government (also called the state) makes laws, collects taxes and uses the tax money for public services such as state schools and roads. Organisations it owns or controls are the public sector; everything owned by private people or firms is the private sector.

Laws set the minimum a business must do: keep workers safe, limit pollution, pay tax on its profit. Governments also try to attract businesses, for example with grants (money that is not paid back) or lower taxes, because businesses create jobs.

Choosing to do more than the law requires is corporate social responsibility (1.3).

How Business exam questions work

Most IB Business questions are about a case study: a text about one business. The examiner wants you to use business ideas on that business, not in general.

  • The command term (the instruction word: define, explain, evaluate...) tells you how deep to go. Tap any command term on this page to see its official meaning.
  • The marks tell you how much to write: "explain two challenges [4 marks]" means two points worth 2 marks each.
  • Every point needs a fact or number from the case, then "this means... so...".

Long answers (10 marks) are marked with markbands: the examiner picks the level that best fits the whole answer.

1.1

What is a business?

Resources go in, something people want comes out, and value is added on the way.

By the end of this section I can

The nature of business

A business takes inputs (land, labour, capital and enterprise), changes them in a process, and sells the output: a good such as a phone or a service such as a haircut. Every business has four functions, Units 2 to 5 of your course: human resources, finance and accounts, marketing, and operations.

Added value Learn
Selling price minus the cost of bought-in materials. A café sells a coffee for $4.00 and pays $1.10 for beans, milk and cup: added value $2.90. Wages, rent and profit come out of it, so it is not profit.
Entrepreneur Learn
A person who spots a business opportunity, brings the resources together to act on it and accepts the risk that it may fail.
Intrapreneur Learn
An employee who acts like an entrepreneur inside an existing organisation, using its money rather than their own.

The four sectors of the economy

SectorWhat it doesExamples
Primary LearnTakes natural resources from land or seafarming, fishing, mining
Secondary LearnManufacturing and constructioncar factories, bakeries, builders
Tertiary LearnServices to people and businessesshops, banks, hotels, hospitals
Quaternary LearnKnowledge services: information, ICT, researchsoftware and data firms, research labs

Entrepreneurship, and starting up

Start-up challenges: raising finance (banks see a new firm as risky), cash flow (costs arrive before customers do), no brand yet, established competitors, little experience. Opportunities: a gap in the market, selling online before renting a shop, government grants, being small and flexible.

Worked example: a 4-mark "explain" answer. Noor, 24, plans a bike-repair stall at a train station. She has $3 000 of savings; the stall costs $9 000. A chain store two streets away also does repairs. Explain two challenges Noor may face. [4 marks]
  1. Read the marks: two challenges for 4 marks means 2 + 2: one mark for naming each challenge, one for explaining it with the case.
  2. Challenge 1, finance. Noor has only $3 000 of the $9 000, so she must borrow $6 000. With no trading record, a bank may refuse or charge a high interest rate, which raises her costs from the first month.
  3. Challenge 2, competition. The chain store nearby is already known to cyclists and may charge less because it buys parts in bulk, so Noor may struggle to win customers at first.
Why this step? (step 1: write two key words, then open)

The marks give the shape: "lack of finance" alone gets only the naming mark; the second needs the case ($6 000 gap) and its effect on Noor.

Finish it. Bilal plans a noodle food truck near a university. He has saved $8 000; the truck costs $20 000. Two noodle shops are already on the same street. Explain two challenges Bilal may face. [4 marks]
  1. Challenge 1, finance. Bilal must borrow $12 000 of the $20 000. As a new business a bank may refuse him or charge high interest, raising his costs.
  2. Challenge 2, competition. The two noodle shops on the same street ________, so ________.
Answer

Challenge 2: the two noodle shops already have regular student customers, so Bilal may sell little at first and need low prices, cutting his profit while he repays the loan.

Exam traps. (1) A bank is tertiary, not quaternary (knowledge work). (2) Added value is not profit.
Self-check 1.1 (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. Place each in its sector: an oil platform, an oil refinery, a petrol station, a firm writing software to forecast oil prices.
  2. A baker pays $0.80 for the inputs of one loaf and sells it for $3.00. Added value per loaf?
  3. Entrepreneur or intrapreneur: what is the difference?

Answers. 1: primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary. 2: 3.00 − 0.80 = $2.20. 3: an entrepreneur risks their own resources; an intrapreneur is an employee using the organisation's resources.

Extra, not needed today: sectoral change and the business plan

As a country develops, jobs move from primary to secondary (industrialisation) and later to tertiary and quaternary (deindustrialisation). A business plan (a toolkit tool) sets out the idea, target market, costs, finance needed and forecasts; banks usually ask for one before they lend.

Worked example: limited liability in numbers. A business closes owing $80 000. Selling everything it owns raises $30 000. Compare Ana, a sole trader, with Ben, who paid $20 000 for shares in a privately held company in the same position.
  1. Debt left after selling the assets: 80 000 − 30 000 = $50 000.
  2. Ana has unlimited liability: she must find the $50 000 from her own savings or home.
  3. Ben has limited liability: he loses his $20 000 and nothing more; the lenders bear the rest.
Why this step? (step 1: write two key words, then open)

Liability is about the debt the business's own assets cannot cover: find it first, then ask who pays.

Finish it: recommend a legal form. Lina and Marco, two designers, want to open a studio costing $40 000. Lina's parents will invest $15 000 but want a say and will not risk more than that.
  1. Two owners rules out a sole trader; outside investors who want a say point to a company rather than a partnership.
  2. The parents will risk no more than $15 000, so they need ________ liability.
  3. Private or public company? ________, because ________.
Answer

2: limited liability, so a company. 3: privately held, because $40 000 is a small sum and they want to control who owns shares.

Exam traps. (1) A publicly held company is in the private sector. (2) "Non-profit" means no profit goes to owners, not that it earns no money.
Self-check 1.2 (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. A state-owned railway and a publicly held airline: which sector is each in?
  2. Two reasons a successful sole trader might become a privately held company.
  3. How does voting in a cooperative differ from voting in a publicly held company?

Answers. 1: railway public, airline private. 2: limited liability; more capital from selling shares privately. 3: cooperative, one member one vote; company, one vote per share.

Partners usually sign a deed of partnership on sharing profits and decisions. In the UK a privately held company is "Ltd" and a publicly held one "plc". The first sale of shares to the public is an initial public offering (IPO).

1.3

Business objectives

Where the business wants to be, and the targets that get it there.

By the end of this section I can

Vision statement Learn
What the organisation hopes to become in the long term: its ideal future. Inspiring, not measurable.
Mission statement Learn
The organisation's purpose now: what it does, for whom, and what it values.
Strategic objective Learn
A long-term target for the whole organisation, set by senior managers.
Tactical objective Learn
A shorter-term target for one department or area that helps reach a strategic objective. Good objectives are SMART: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time-bound.
Corporate social responsibility (CSR) Learn
Acting in the interests of society and the environment beyond what the law requires.

Common objectives. Growth (more sales, outlets or market share). Profit (rewards owners, funds investment). Protecting shareholder value (keeping the share price and dividends up so investors stay). Ethical objectives (fair wages in the supply chain, honest marketing, lower emissions) even when the law does not demand it.

Worked example: from vision down to tactics. Pedal, a bike-sharing firm in one city with 400 bikes.
  1. Vision (ideal future): "A city where nobody needs a car for a short trip."
  2. Mission (purpose now): "Clean, cheap bikes within five minutes' walk of every home in the city."
  3. Strategic (whole firm, long term): "Grow to 2 000 bikes and 30% of short city trips by 2030."
  4. Tactical (one area, shorter term): "Cut workshop repair time from 5 days to 2 days by June."
Why this step? (step 4: write two key words, then open)

A tactical objective must serve the strategic one: faster repairs keep more bikes on the road, which helps win the 30% share.

Finish it. Sunbake, a family bakery with 3 shops.
  1. Vision: "To be the bakery every family in the region thinks of first."
  2. Mission: "Fresh bread every morning, baked with local flour, at prices families can afford."
  3. Strategic: ________ (whole firm, a number, a year).
  4. Tactical: ________ (one shop or department, a number, a month).
Answer

For example, strategic: "Open 5 more shops in the region by 2029." Tactical: "Raise sales at the station shop by 10% by the end of March."

Extra, not needed today: why objectives change

Internal reasons: a new owner or chief executive, the firm's stage of growth, its finances. External reasons: a recession, new competitors, new laws, changing customer attitudes. Conflict. A gain for one group is often a cost for another: higher wages please employees but cut shareholders' profit. Some decisions help several groups at once: well-trained staff raise quality, pleasing customers and lifting profit. To reduce conflict: consult early, compromise, share gains (profit-related pay, employee share schemes), and decide whose concerns matter most.

Worked example: one conflict, 4 marks. Riverside Hotel wants to turn its car park into a 40-room extension. Building takes a year; guests would park in the town centre. Explain one conflict between two stakeholders. [4 marks]
  1. Pick two groups affected in opposite ways: shareholders and the local community.
  2. Shareholders gain: 40 more rooms mean more revenue and, in time, higher dividends.
  3. The local community loses: a year of building noise, and guests taking town-centre parking.
  4. The conflict: the more the hotel grows, the more residents bear the cost. It could reduce this by limiting building hours or renting a car park, at a cost to profit.
Why this step? (step 1: write two key words, then open)

A conflict needs two groups pulled in opposite directions by the same decision.

Finish it. Hilltop Foods, a factory with 120 staff, plans a night shift to meet a big new order from a supermarket. Explain one conflict between two stakeholders. [4 marks]
  1. Two groups pulled in opposite ways: shareholders and employees.
  2. Shareholders gain: the big new order means more revenue and profit.
  3. Employees lose: ________.
  4. The conflict and one way to reduce it: ________.
Answer

3: some of the 120 staff must work nights, harming sleep and family life. 4: shareholders want the profit, staff want normal hours; volunteers plus a night-shift bonus would reduce the conflict but cut some profit.

Exam traps. (1) Stakeholder is not shareholder. (2) Explain the three or four most affected groups with the case; do not list them all.
Self-check 1.4 (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. Internal or external: managers, a bank that lent money, a pressure group, shareholders?
  2. A supermarket cuts prices by paying farmers less. Who gains and who loses?

Answers. 1: internal, external, external, internal. 2: customers gain (lower prices); farmers (suppliers) lose income.

Strategic allianceFirms cooperate on a project but stay independent; no new businessCheap and flexibleLess commitment
FranchisingFranchisees buy the right to trade under the franchisor's brand, for a fee plus royalties (a share of sales)Growth paid for by franchiseesLess control over quality

Reasons to grow: economies of scale, market share, survival, spreading risk. Reasons to stay small: the owner keeps control and contact with customers, no diseconomies, a niche market, no debt.

Exam traps. (1) Economies of scale lower average cost, not total cost. (2) External economies (the industry grows) are not external growth (joining other firms).
Self-check 1.5 (write two key words for each, then open)
  1. Total cost is $90 000 for 30 000 units. Average cost?
  2. A supermarket chain doubles its stores and suppliers now give a 10% discount. Which economy?
  3. An airline and a hotel group give each other's customers loyalty points, each staying independent. Which growth method?

Answers. 1: 90 000 ÷ 30 000 = $3. 2: internal purchasing economy. 3: strategic alliance (no new business is created).

Extra, not needed today: directions of mergers

Horizontal (same stage, two supermarkets), vertical backward (towards suppliers: a supermarket buys a dairy), vertical forward (towards customers: a dairy buys shops), conglomerate (unrelated industries). Useful words, but not listed in the IB statement.

Multinational companies (MNCs)

When a company sets up in your country, who gains and who pays?

By the end of this section I can

Multinational company (MNC) Learn
A company with its head office in one country that produces goods or services in more than one country.
Home country / host country Learn
Home: where the head office is. Host: any other country where it operates.

Benefits for the host: jobs, investment, new skills, tax revenue, orders for local suppliers. Costs: local firms close, profits sent home, top jobs go to foreign staff, harm where laws are weak, the MNC may leave quickly. It depends on: the kind of jobs, local buying, tax actually paid, how long it stays.

Worked example: weighing an impact. VoltaCell, a South Korean battery maker, plans a factory in a host country with high youth unemployment: 1 500 jobs, a 10-year tax holiday, most parts imported, heavy water use in a dry region.
  1. Benefit: 1 500 jobs where youth unemployment is high, plus battery skills.
  2. Costs: little tax for 10 years; imported parts mean few local orders; water use could harm farmers.
  3. Limitation: the case does not say how many jobs are skilled or whether VoltaCell stays after the tax holiday.
  4. Judgement: positive in the short term, good in the long term only if the government sets conditions (local hiring, water limits).
Why this step? (step 4: write two key words, then open)

AO3 needs a judgement with a condition from the case.

Finish it. A Spanish clothing MNC opens 3 factories in Morocco: 2 000 jobs, cotton bought from local farms, a 5-year tax holiday.
  1. Benefit: 2 000 jobs and orders for local cotton farms.
  2. Cost: ________. Judgement: ________.
Answer

Cost: little tax for 5 years; profits go to Spain. Judgement: mostly positive (jobs, local buying), if it stays after the tax holiday.

Exam trap. Writing about the MNC's gains when the question asks about the host country.

  1. A US coffee chain opens 200 cafés in Vietnam. Home and host?
  2. Why might profits sent back home matter to the host country?

Answers. 1: home, the USA; host, Vietnam. 2: the money earned there leaves instead of being spent or reinvested there.

How to write a 10-mark answer

Section B of both papers ends with one 10-mark question, marked with markbands.

The command term is AO3, such as discuss (balanced review, clear conclusion) or evaluate (weigh strengths and limitations, then judge). The examiner picks the band (1 to 2 up to 9 to 10) that fits best on four lines: answering the actual question, accurate business tools and theories, use of the case, and substantiated, balanced arguments. For 9 to 10 you also explain the limitations of the case.

The frame

  1. Decode. Underline the command term, the decision and whose view. Plan in one line per step.
  2. Introduce. Define the key term and say what is being decided, with one fact from the case.
  3. Use a tool. Stakeholder interests, CSR or a calculation, applied to the case.
  4. Arguments for and against. Each paragraph: Point, Case evidence, Explanation, Business consequence, Qualification ("However, this depends on...").
  5. Limitations. What the case does not tell you.
  6. Judgement. Answer the question in one sentence, give the main reason and what it depends on.

The five-part paragraph is from your teacher's 10-mark guide. Band descriptors: exam technique page.

1. Model answer

Tala Tea, owned by two founders, has four bubble-tea cafés: revenue $1.2 million, profit $180 000, online rating 4.7 out of 5. Option A: borrow $400 000 to open four more cafés itself. Option B: franchise to twelve local entrepreneurs, each paying a $25 000 fee and 6% of revenue. Last year a friend's pop-up stall under the Tala name had hygiene complaints.

Evaluate whether Tala Tea should grow by franchising rather than by opening its own cafés. [10 marks]

IntroduceFranchising is external growth: franchisees buy the right to trade under Tala Tea's brand for a fee and royalties. The alternative is four own cafés with a $400 000 loan.

Tool: calculationEach café earns about $300 000 and $45 000 profit, so four new cafés might add 4 × $45 000 = $180 000 a year before interest. Twelve franchises would bring 12 × $25 000 = $300 000 in fees, and royalties of 6% × $300 000 × 12 = $216 000 a year if they sell as much as current cafés, with no loan.

ForFranchising gives speed with little financial risk. Franchisees pay for their cafés, so Tala Tea avoids $400 000 of debt, over two years of its profit. Franchisees are owners, so they have a strong incentive to work hard.

AgainstHowever, the 4.7 rating is Tala Tea's main asset, and franchising loses direct control of quality. The pop-up stall's hygiene complaints show the risk: one bad franchise could damage all sixteen cafés.

LimitationsThe royalty figure assumes franchises sell as much as current cafés, which is uncertain, and the case gives no interest rate, so the cost of Option A is unknown.

JudgementOn balance Tala Tea should franchise, because it brings faster growth and more income without debt. This depends on quality control: start with a few franchisees, write strict standards into the contracts and inspect regularly.

Why this step? (the Limitations paragraph: write two key words, then open)

The 9 to 10 band asks for it.

2. Paragraph frame: fill the blanks

Bright Steps Childcare (BC) runs three childcare centres for mainly working parents, with 42 nursery assistants (many part-time) and a local food supplier. Rising rent and wage costs have cut profits. The directors plan a 12% fee rise, partly to fund staff training and better staff-to-child ratios. Some parents already complain about cost; employees fear children may be withdrawn. (Question 1 of the 1.4 Stakeholders questions, shortened.)

Discuss the possible effects of BC's proposed fee increase on its stakeholders. [10 marks]

Write it out on paper, filling each blank.

Point: The owners and directors may benefit from the fee increase because ________.
Case evidence: The case says rising ________ and ________ costs have reduced profits.
Explanation: A 12% rise means ________, which ________.
Business consequence: BC could then spend some of the extra revenue on ________, which also helps ________ (another stakeholder).
Qualification: However, this depends on whether ________.

Filled version

BC's owners and directors may benefit from the fee increase because it should raise revenue. The case says rising rent and wage costs have reduced profits. A 12% rise means more income per child, which could restore profit and keep all three centres open. BC could then spend some of the extra revenue on staff training and better staff-to-child ratios, which also helps employees (skills, less pressure) and children (safer care). However, this depends on whether enough parents stay: if many withdraw their children, revenue could fall instead.

3. Your turn: the opposing paragraph

On paper, write the against paragraph with the same five parts: how the rise could hurt parents and then employees. Use at least two facts from the case (the complaints, the 42 mostly part-time assistants). Then check it against the third paragraph of the sample answer in the stakeholders mark scheme.

★ If time, the full answer: add an introduction, a conflict paragraph and a judgement. The mark scheme gives 2 marks each for: internal stakeholders; external stakeholders; positive effects; negative effects or conflict; a balanced judgement. Then check the band: no limitation (such as no figures on how many parents might leave) means 7 to 8 at most.

Quick quiz

1. Which business operates in the quaternary sector?

Quaternary is knowledge and information work. The bank branch is tertiary, the car plant secondary, the mine primary. The trap: thinking every modern service is quaternary.

2. "Limited liability" means that

The most a shareholder can lose is what they paid for their shares. The last option is the opposite, unlimited liability.

3. A publicly held company differs from a privately held company because it

"Publicly held" is about who can buy the shares; it is still in the private sector (the trap). Both kinds have limited liability.

4. Which of these is a vision statement?

A vision is the long-term ideal future. The first option is a mission (what it does now); the last two are tactical objectives.

5. Which group is an internal stakeholder?

Shareholders own the business, so they are internal even if they do not work there (the trap). The others are external.

6. Output rises from 10 000 to 25 000 units and total cost from $50 000 to $100 000. What happens to average cost?

50 000 ÷ 10 000 = $5 and 100 000 ÷ 25 000 = $4. Total cost always rises with output; scale is about cost per unit. $2.50 comes from halving without dividing.

7. Two firms set up a new company, owned by both, sharing its costs, control and profits. This is

A joint venture creates a new, separate business. In a strategic alliance no new business is created (the trap).

Questions 8 to 10 are marked with point-based mark schemes, like section A of the real papers: for "explain two..." [4], 1 mark for each point identified and 1 mark for explaining it with the case. Markbands are only used for the 10-mark question.

8. (1.2) Kofi runs a successful phone-repair shop as a sole trader. He wants to open two more shops costing $60 000. His brother offers to invest $25 000 if he can own part of the business. Explain two advantages for Kofi of changing to a privately held company. [4 marks]

Show model answer

Limited liability. As a sole trader Kofi is personally liable for all debts; if the $60 000 expansion fails he could lose his own savings or home. As a company, he could lose only what he invested.

Raising capital while keeping control. He can sell shares to his brother for $25 000, over 40% of the cost, without selling shares to the public, so Kofi still decides who becomes an owner.

Mark points (max 4): [1] advantage 1 identified; [1] explained with the case ($60 000, his personal assets); [1] advantage 2 identified; [1] explained with the case (brother, $25 000). Also accepted: the business continues if Kofi leaves. No case facts: max 2.

9. (1.5) FreshMart grew from 20 to 45 supermarkets in three years, all run from one head office in the capital. Store managers say decisions from head office now take weeks. Explain one internal economy of scale and one internal diseconomy of scale FreshMart may experience. [4 marks]

Show model answer

Economy: purchasing. Buying for 45 stores instead of 20 lets FreshMart order in bulk and get supplier discounts, so the average cost of each product falls.

Diseconomy: communication or coordination. One head office now controls 45 stores, and managers say decisions take weeks, so stores react slowly to local demand, which can raise waste and average cost.

Mark points (max 4): [1] a correct internal economy; [1] explained with the case (45 stores, bulk); [1] a correct internal diseconomy; [1] explained with the case (decisions take weeks). An external economy (from the industry) gets 0 for that point.

10. (1.3, 1.4) Lumo Clothing sells 200 000 shirts a year and made $300 000 profit last year. As part of its CSR plan it will switch to a certified fair-wage factory that charges $0.40 more per shirt, and raise its price by $0.50. Explain how this decision may affect two of Lumo's stakeholders. [4 marks]

Show model answer

Shareholders. Costs rise by 200 000 × $0.40 = $80 000 a year, about 27% of last year's profit. The $0.50 price rise covers this only if sales stay at 200 000 (an extra $100 000), so dividends could fall if customers buy fewer shirts.

Customers. They pay $0.50 more per shirt. Price-sensitive customers may switch to cheaper brands, while those who care about workers' pay may become more loyal to Lumo.

Mark points (max 4): per stakeholder, [1] the group and the effect identified; [1] the effect explained with the case (the $80 000, the $0.50 rise). Also accepted: factory workers in the supply chain (fairer pay) or employees (pride in the brand).
