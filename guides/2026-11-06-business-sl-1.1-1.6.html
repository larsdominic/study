1.1

What is a business?

Resources go in, something people want comes out, and value is added on the way.

By the end of this section I can

The nature of business

A business takes inputs (resources), turns them into something in a process, and sells the output: a physical good such as a phone, or a service such as a haircut. Economists group the inputs into four factors of production: land (natural resources), labour (people's work), capital (man-made tools, machines, buildings) and enterprise (the person who takes the risk and organises the other three).

Every business, from a market stall to an airline, has the same four functions, and they are Units 2 to 5 of your course: human resources (the people), finance and accounts (the money), marketing (the customers) and operations (making the product).

Business Learn
An organisation that uses resources to provide goods or services that people need or want, usually in exchange for money.
Added value Learn
The difference between the price a business sells its output for and the cost of the materials and services it bought in to make it. Wages, rent and profit all have to come out of it, so added value is not the same as profit.
Entrepreneur Learn
A person who spots a business opportunity, brings together the resources to act on it and accepts the risk that it may fail.
Intrapreneur Learn
An employee who acts like an entrepreneur inside an existing organisation, for example by developing a new product, using the organisation's money rather than their own.
Worked example 1: added value. A café pays $1.10 for the beans, milk and cup in one flat white and sells it for $4.00. Find the added value per cup and say what it has to pay for.
  1. Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in inputs.
  2. = 4.00 − 1.10 = $2.90 per cup.
  3. The $2.90 is not profit: the barista's wages, the rent and the electricity are paid from it, and only what is left after those is profit.
  4. Ways to add more value: a higher price through branding or a better experience, or cheaper inputs (for example a bulk deal on beans).

Sectoral change. As a country develops, the share of jobs and output moves from primary to secondary (industrialisation), and later from secondary to tertiary and quaternary (deindustrialisation). Machines and imports replace farm and factory jobs, and richer people spend more on services. For a business this changes where it finds workers, which skills are scarce and which markets grow.

Entrepreneurship, and starting up

People start businesses to be their own boss, to earn more, to turn a skill or hobby into income, to fill a gap in the market they have noticed, because they cannot find a job, or to solve a social problem. Writing a business plan (a toolkit tool) forces the founder to set out the idea, the target market, the costs, the finance needed and the forecast, and banks usually ask for one before they lend.

Worked example 2: a 4-mark "explain" answer on start-up challenges. Noor, 24, plans to open a bike-repair stall at a train station. She has $3 000 of savings; the stall costs $9 000 to set up. A chain store two streets away offers repairs too. Explain two challenges Noor may face. [4 marks]
  1. Read the marks: 4 marks for two challenges means 2 + 2, one mark for naming each, one for explaining it with the case.
  2. Challenge 1, finance. Noor has only $3 000 of the $9 000 she needs, so she must borrow $6 000. As a new business with no trading record, a bank may refuse or charge a high interest rate, which raises her costs from the first month.
  3. Challenge 2, competition. The chain store nearby is already known to cyclists and may be able to charge less because it buys parts in bulk, so Noor may struggle to win customers until she builds a reputation.
  4. Check: each point names the challenge, uses a fact from the case ($6 000 gap, the chain two streets away) and says what it means for Noor.
Exam traps. (1) Calling a bank or a hospital "quaternary": serving people face to face is tertiary; quaternary is about knowledge and information. (2) Treating added value as profit. (3) Writing a list of challenges with no link to the case: "lack of finance" on its own earns the identification mark only. (4) The Chopra case study tests entrepreneurs and start-up problems: do it before you look back at this section, so you know what you really remember.

Types of business entities

Who owns it, who is liable for its debts, and what it exists for.

By the end of this section I can

Private sector and public sector

The private sector is every organisation owned by private individuals or groups: from a one-person stall to a global company. Most aim to make a profit. The public sector is every organisation owned or controlled by the government, such as state schools, public hospitals or a state railway. These usually aim to provide a service for everyone, paid for mainly by taxes.

Unlimited liability Learn
The owner is personally responsible for all the business's debts, so their own house, car and savings can be taken to pay them.
Limited liability Learn
The owners (shareholders) can lose at most the money they put into the business; their personal belongings are safe.
Incorporated business Learn
A company that is a separate legal person from its owners: it can own property, sign contracts, sue and be sued in its own name, and it continues if an owner dies or sells.
Social enterprise Learn
An organisation whose main purpose is social or environmental, which earns revenue like a business and puts most of its surplus back into that purpose.
NGO Learn
A non-governmental organisation: a non-profit social enterprise, independent of government, run for a social, humanitarian or environmental cause and funded mainly by donations, grants and fundraising.

Partnerships usually write a deed of partnership: an agreement on how profits are shared, who decides what, and what happens if a partner leaves. In the UK a privately held company is written "Ltd" and a publicly held company "plc"; other countries use their own labels (in the USA "Inc." is used by both kinds). The first sale of shares to the public is an initial public offering (IPO).

Worked example 1: what limited liability means in numbers. A business closes owing $80 000. Selling everything the business owns raises $30 000. Compare Ana, a sole trader, with Ben, who paid $20 000 for shares in a privately held company in the same position.
  1. Debt left after selling the business's assets: 80 000 − 30 000 = $50 000.
  2. Ana has unlimited liability: she must find the $50 000 from her own savings, car or home.
  3. Ben has limited liability: he loses the $20 000 he paid for his shares and nothing more. The company's lenders, not Ben, bear the rest.
  4. This is why owners of risky or growing businesses often change from sole trader to company.

Social enterprises

The syllabus splits social enterprises into for-profit ones (they make a profit, but use most of it for their social purpose) and one non-profit type, the NGO.

TypeMain features
Private sector company (for-profit social enterprise)Owned privately, trades like any company, but its main aim is social or environmental, for example a firm that employs and trains people who were homeless. Most profit is reinvested in the cause.
Public sector company (for-profit social enterprise)Owned by the government but run as a commercial business that sells to customers, such as a state-owned energy or postal company. It aims to cover its costs or make a profit while serving a public purpose, for example keeping a service available in remote areas.
Cooperative (for-profit social enterprise)Owned and run by its members (workers, customers or producers such as farmers). Each member usually has one vote, whatever they invested, and profits are shared among members or reinvested. Strong member loyalty and motivation, but decisions can be slow and raising large amounts of finance is hard.
NGO (non-profit social enterprise)Independent of government, set up for a cause (health, the environment, human rights, disaster relief). It may sell goods or services, but any surplus goes to the cause, never to owners. Relies on donations and grants, which can rise and fall; must show donors it uses money well.
Worked example 2: recommend a legal form. Lina and Marco, both graphic designers, want to open a studio. They need $40 000 for computers and a lease. Lina's parents will invest $15 000 but want a say and do not want to risk more than that. Which legal form suits them?
  1. Two owners rules out a sole trader. Outside investors who want a share and a say point to a company, not a partnership.
  2. The parents want to risk no more than $15 000: only limited liability guarantees that, so a company.
  3. Private or public? They need $40 000, not millions, and want to control who owns shares, so a privately held company.
  4. Downside to mention: set-up costs and published accounts, which a partnership would avoid. A judgement always weighs the cost.
Exam traps. (1) A "publicly held company" is in the private sector: "public" refers to who can buy its shares, not to government ownership. (2) "Non-profit" does not mean an NGO earns no money; it means no profit goes to owners. (3) Saying a sole trader works alone: it has one owner but can employ many staff. (4) Forgetting that in a cooperative votes go per member, not per share.
Self-check 1.2
  1. A state-owned railway and a publicly held airline: which sector is each in?
  2. Give two reasons a successful sole trader might become a privately held company.
  3. How does voting in a cooperative differ from voting in a publicly held company?
  4. Name two ways an NGO can raise money, and one risk of depending on them.

Answers. 1: the railway is public sector (government-owned); the airline is private sector (owned by its shareholders, even though anyone can buy shares). 2: limited liability protects the owner's personal assets; selling shares to family or friends brings in more capital; the company continues if the owner leaves. 3: cooperative, usually one member one vote; publicly held company, one vote per share, so large shareholders have more say. 4: donations, grants, fundraising events, selling goods; donations can fall in a recession or after bad publicity, so income is uncertain.

1.3

Business objectives

Where the business wants to be, and the targets that get it there.

By the end of this section I can

Vision statement Learn
A short statement of what the organisation hopes to become or achieve in the long term: its ideal future. Inspiring, not measurable.
Mission statement Learn
A statement of the organisation's purpose now: what it does, for whom and what it values. It guides everyday decisions.
Objectives Learn
Specific targets the business sets to achieve its aims, ideally SMART: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound.
Strategic objective Learn
A long-term target for the whole organisation, set by senior managers (for example a market share to reach in five years).
Tactical objective Learn
A shorter-term target, usually for one department or area, that helps to achieve a strategic objective (for example a monthly sales target for one region).
Corporate social responsibility (CSR) Learn
A business choosing to act in the interests of society and the environment, beyond what the law requires, and taking responsibility for the effects of its decisions.

Common objectives

  • Growth: more sales, outlets, market share or countries. Brings economies of scale (1.5) and more security, but needs finance and can cost control.
  • Profit: rewards owners and funds investment. A business may accept low profit for a while to grow first.
  • Protecting shareholder value: keeping the share price and dividends rising, so owners stay and investors keep putting money in. Mainly an objective for companies with shareholders.
  • Ethical objectives: acting on moral principles, such as fair wages in the supply chain, honest marketing or cutting emissions, even when the law does not demand it.

Objectives change over time because of internal factors (a new owner or chief executive, the firm's stage of growth, its finances) and external factors (a recession, new competitors, new laws, changing customer attitudes). A STEEPLE analysis (social, technological, economic, environmental, political, legal, ethical) is the toolkit tool for spotting the external ones.

Worked example 1: from vision down to tactics. Pedal, a bike-sharing firm in one city with 400 bikes. Write a vision, a mission, one strategic and one tactical objective.
  1. Vision (the ideal future): "A city where nobody needs a car for a short trip."
  2. Mission (purpose now): "Clean, cheap bikes within five minutes' walk of every home in the city, for every resident."
  3. Strategic objective (whole firm, long term, SMART): "Grow to 2 000 bikes and 30% of all short city trips by 2030."
  4. Tactical objective (one area, shorter term, supports step 3): "Cut the average repair time in the workshop from 5 days to 2 days by June."
  5. Check the link: faster repairs keep more bikes on the road, which supports the market share target, which moves the firm towards its vision.

Worked example 2: putting a number on a CSR choice. A clothing company sells 200 000 shirts a year. A certified fair-wage factory charges $0.40 more per shirt.
  1. Extra cost per year: 200 000 × $0.40 = $80 000.
  2. Against it: lower profit this year, which shareholders may resist.
  3. For it: protection from a scandal about supplier working conditions, and a story to tell customers that may justify a slightly higher price.
  4. Judgement: worth it if the brand sells on values to customers who care, less so for a budget brand competing only on price. "It depends on..." plus a reason is how AO3 answers end.
Exam traps. (1) Swapping vision and mission: vision is the future dream, mission is today's purpose. (2) A "tactical objective" with no time limit or number is not SMART. (3) CSR means going beyond the law; obeying the law is the minimum, not CSR. (4) Writing that a mission statement is "useful" without saying for whom (staff, customers, investors) and how.

Conflict between stakeholders

Stakeholders conflict because a gain for one is often a cost for another. Higher wages please employees but cut profit for shareholders. A new factory pleases the government (jobs) but worries the community (traffic). Lower prices please customers but may mean squeezing suppliers. Not every decision is a conflict, though: a well-trained, well-paid workforce can raise quality, which pleases customers and lifts profit for shareholders too.

Ways a business can reduce conflict: talk to and consult the groups early; compromise (for example a smaller pay rise plus extra training); share gains (profit-related pay, employee share schemes); and decide whose concerns matter most. A useful way to think about the last one is to ask how much power each group has over the decision and how much interest it has in it: the group with high power and high interest has to be persuaded first.

Worked example: interests and a conflict in a short case. Riverside Hotel, a privately held company, wants to turn its car park into a 40-room extension. Building will take a year. Guests would have to park in the town centre. Explain one conflict between two stakeholders. [4 marks]
  1. Pick two groups the decision affects in opposite ways: shareholders and the local community.
  2. Shareholders gain: 40 more rooms means more revenue and, in time, higher profit and dividends.
  3. The local community loses: a year of building noise, and hotel guests taking parking spaces in the town centre.
  4. The conflict: the more the hotel grows, the more the residents bear the cost. The hotel could reduce it by limiting building hours or renting a separate car park for guests, at a cost to profit.
Exam traps. (1) Writing "stakeholders" when you mean "shareholders" or the other way round. (2) Listing every stakeholder with one line each: in a 10-mark answer, choose the three or four most affected and explain each with the case. (3) Saying a decision is bad for "all stakeholders": usually some gain and some lose, and the answer must show both. (4) Forgetting that one group can be split: part-time and full-time staff, or loyal and new customers, may react differently.
Self-check 1.4
  1. Internal or external: managers, a bank that lent money, a pressure group, shareholders?
  2. A supermarket chain cuts prices by paying farmers less. Which stakeholders gain and which lose?
  3. Give one way to reduce conflict between employees and shareholders over pay.

Answers. 1: internal, external, external, internal. 2: customers gain (lower prices) and, if sales rise enough, shareholders; farmers (suppliers) lose income and may later cut quality or switch buyers; the local farming community and pressure groups may campaign against the chain. 3: profit-related pay or an employee share scheme, so staff earn more when profit rises; or consultation and a compromise rise linked to productivity.

  2. From 5 000 to 12 000, average cost rises by $0.20: diseconomies of scale (for example a second shift that is harder to supervise, more waste).
  3. Total cost always rises with output; the question is always what happens to the cost per loaf.

Internal and external growth

Internal (organic) growth: the business grows by itself, by selling more, opening new branches, launching new products or entering new markets, usually financed from its own profit or loans. It is slower and safer, and the owners keep control and the firm's culture. External (inorganic) growth: the business grows by joining with or working through other organisations. It is faster and buys instant market share, skills or brands, but costs more, is riskier and can bring culture clashes.

Whether the overall impact is positive depends on: how many and what kind of jobs go to locals; how much the MNC buys from local suppliers; the tax it actually pays after any tax holiday; how well the host government enforces labour and environmental laws; and how long the MNC is likely to stay.

Worked example: weighing an MNC's impact. VoltaCell, a battery maker based in South Korea, plans a factory in a host country with high youth unemployment. It promises 1 500 jobs and has been offered a 10-year tax holiday. It will import most parts. The factory needs a lot of water in a dry region.
  1. Benefit, with the case: 1 500 jobs in a country with high youth unemployment, plus training in battery technology that local firms could use later.
  2. Cost, with the case: a 10-year tax holiday means little tax revenue for a decade, and imported parts mean few orders for local suppliers.
  3. Cost, with the case: heavy water use in a dry region could harm farmers and the community.
  4. Limitations: the case does not say how many of the 1 500 jobs are skilled, or whether VoltaCell commits to staying after the tax holiday ends.
  5. Judgement: positive in the short term because of the jobs, but only good in the long term if the government sets conditions (local hiring and suppliers, water limits) in return for the tax holiday.
Exam traps. (1) Writing about the MNC's benefits (cheap labour, new markets) when the question asks about the host country. (2) Mixing up home and host country. (3) One-sided answers ("MNCs exploit poor countries") with no evidence from the case. (4) No judgement: AO3 means you must say whether the impact is positive overall, and what it depends on.

How to write a 10-mark answer

Paper 1 section B and Paper 2 section B end with one 10-mark question, marked with the same five markbands.

Command terms in 10-mark questions are AO3: discuss (a balanced review with a clear conclusion), evaluate (weigh strengths and limitations), examine, to what extent, recommend or justify. The examiner finds the band that fits your answer best, using four things: how well you answer the actual question, your business tools and theory, how you use the case, and how substantiated and balanced your arguments are.

The frame: seven steps

  1. Decode the question. Underline the command term, the exact decision or issue, and whose point of view (the business? its stakeholders? the host country?). Write a one-line plan for each step below. (3 min in the exam)
  2. Introduce and define. One or two sentences: define the key term(s) precisely and say what the business is deciding, with one fact from the case.
  3. Use a tool or theory. Name it and apply it: stakeholder interests (1.4), economies of scale (1.5), CSR (1.3), SWOT, STEEPLE, a calculation from the data. A tool you name but do not apply earns little.
  4. Arguments for (two paragraphs). Each one: point, evidence from the case (numbers, quotes, facts beyond the company's name), then the chain of reasoning: "this means... which leads to... so...". That is what "substantiated" means.
  5. Arguments against (two paragraphs), built the same way. This is the "balance" in the top bands.
  6. Limitations of the case or data. One or two sentences on what is missing or uncertain: only one year of figures, a forecast not a fact, no information about competitors, a source that may be biased. The 9 to 10 band asks for this explicitly.
  7. Conclusion with a judgement. Answer the question in one clear sentence, give the main reason, and say what it depends on (short term versus long term, or a condition). Do not add new points here.

A model answer on a case written for this page

Tala Tea is a privately held company owned by two founders, with four bubble-tea cafés in one city. Last year its revenue was $1.2 million and its profit $180 000. Customers queue at weekends, and its online rating is 4.7 out of 5.

The founders want to grow. Option A: borrow $400 000 from a bank to open four more cafés themselves. Option B: franchise. Twelve local entrepreneurs have asked to open Tala Tea cafés; each would pay a $25 000 fee and royalties of 6% of revenue. The founders are worried about quality: last year a friend who ran a pop-up stall under the Tala name received hygiene complaints.

Evaluate whether Tala Tea should grow by franchising rather than by opening its own cafés. [10 marks]

Steps 1 and 2: focus, definition, caseFranchising is a form of external growth in which a business (the franchisor) sells other people (franchisees) the right to trade under its brand and methods, in return for a fee and royalties. Tala Tea must choose between this and internal growth: opening four cafés of its own with a $400 000 loan.

Step 3: tool, a calculationTala Tea's four cafés earn $1.2 million, so about $300 000 each, and $45 000 profit each. Four new own cafés might add about 4 × $45 000 = $180 000 profit a year, before interest on the loan. Twelve franchises would bring 12 × $25 000 = $300 000 in fees at once, and if each franchise sells as much as an existing café, royalties of 6% × $300 000 × 12 = $216 000 a year, with no loan.

Step 4: argument for, substantiatedThe strongest argument for franchising is speed with little financial risk. The franchisees pay for their own cafés, so Tala Tea does not take on $400 000 of debt, which for a firm making $180 000 a year would be over two years of profit. Twelve outlets instead of four would also make the brand known faster and give Tala Tea purchasing economies of scale on tea and cups.

Step 4: second argument forFranchisees are owners, so they have a strong incentive to work hard, which suits a business where the weekend queues show demand is already there.

Step 5: argument against, substantiatedHowever, Tala Tea's 4.7 rating is its main asset, and franchising means losing direct control of quality. The pop-up stall that drew hygiene complaints shows the risk: one badly run franchise could damage the reputation of all sixteen cafés, including the founders' own.

Step 5: second argument againstOpening its own cafés keeps all the profit and the culture under the founders' control. Franchising also has costs the case does not mention: training, inspections and support for twelve new owners, which a two-person management team may struggle to provide.

Step 6: limitationsThe royalty figure assumes franchised cafés sell as much as the current ones, which is uncertain because franchisees are new to the business. The case gives only one year of results and no interest rate for the loan, so the cost of Option A is not known.

Step 7: judgementOn balance, Tala Tea should franchise, because it brings faster growth and more income without debt. This depends on quality control: it should start with a few franchisees, write strict standards into the contracts and inspect regularly before adding more.

Fairly short for an exam answer, but every markband line is met. Notice that every argument uses a number or a fact from the case, and that the judgement names a condition.

Exam traps. (1) Describing theory with no case: the markbands cap that at the low bands. (2) Two arguments for and none against: "mostly one-sided" is the 5 to 6 band. (3) A conclusion that sits on the fence ("there are pros and cons") is not a judgement. (4) Forgetting the limitations sentence, which is the difference between 7 to 8 and 9 to 10. (5) Spending 40 minutes on it in Paper 1: aim for about 30 minutes including the plan.

Ten questions on Unit 1 concepts and definitions

Quick quiz

The real Business Management papers are not multiple choice: they use structured questions and a 10-mark extended answer. The quiz uses multiple choice only so the page can mark it for you. Decide on your answer before you click. Every question explains the trap after you answer. Write any you miss into the Error log.

1. Which business operates in the quaternary sector?

Quaternary activity is knowledge and information work. A bank branch serving customers is tertiary (a service to people), the car plant is secondary and the mine is primary. The trap is thinking every modern-sounding service is quaternary.

2. "Limited liability" means that

Limited liability protects owners' personal assets: the most a shareholder can lose is what they paid for their shares. The last option describes the opposite, unlimited liability, which sole traders and most partners have.

3. The key difference between a publicly held and a privately held company is that a publicly held company

"Publicly held" refers to who can buy the shares. It is still in the private sector, not owned by the government (the trap). Both kinds of company have limited liability, and the publicly held one must publish the most information.

4. Which statement about an NGO is correct?

An NGO is a non-profit social enterprise, independent of government. It can earn money (charity shops, events), but any surplus goes back into the cause. Sharing profit among members with one vote each describes a cooperative.

5. Which of these is a vision statement?

A vision is the long-term ideal future. The first option describes what the bakery does now and what it values: a mission statement. The last two are objectives (tactical ones), with numbers and deadlines.

6. Which is a tactical objective rather than a strategic one?

Tactical objectives are shorter term and cover one part of the business (one depot), helping achieve a strategic aim. The others are long term and for the whole organisation, so strategic.

7. Which group is an internal stakeholder?

Internal stakeholders are part of the organisation: employees, managers and the owners (shareholders). The trap is to think shareholders are external because they do not work there; they own it. Customers, suppliers and the community are external.

8. A firm's output rises from 10 000 to 25 000 units and its total cost rises from $50 000 to $100 000. What happens to its average cost?

Average cost = total cost ÷ output: 50 000 ÷ 10 000 = $5 and 100 000 ÷ 25 000 = $4. Total cost always rises with output; economies of scale are about the cost per unit. $2.50 comes from halving $5 because output more than doubled, without doing the division.

9. Two firms set up a new company, owned by both, to develop a product. They share its costs, control and profits. This is

A joint venture creates a new, separate business that the partners own together. In a strategic alliance the firms cooperate but no new business is created (the trap); in a merger the two firms themselves become one; a takeover is one firm buying control of another against its managers' wishes.

10. Which impact of a multinational company on its host country is most likely to be negative?

Profit repatriation means the money earned in the host country leaves it. The other three are common benefits. The trap is answering from the MNC's point of view; the question asks about the host country.
